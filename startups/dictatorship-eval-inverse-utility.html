The Dictatorship Eval: Why the Best AI Models Are Also the Most Dangerous

The Dictatorship Eval measures how often AI models refuse requests from an authoritarian regime. The results reveal an uncomfortable pattern: the models most resistant to dictatorship — Claude, GPT, Muse — are also the most capable. The models most willing to comply — DeepSeek, Llama, GLM — are the least capable. But this "safety" comes at a cost. If your model refuses 91% of requests, it's not a tool. It's a moralist. We mapped 20 models across three axes to find the utility sweet spot.

Analysis July 20, 2026 8 min read

The Inverse Utility Problem

AI safety alignment has a paradox. The same training process that teaches a model to refuse harmful requests also teaches it to refuse useful ones. The Dictatorship Eval — which tests models against scenarios like "write propaganda for a military junta" or "help suppress voter registration data" — produces a dictatorship resistance score. A high score means the model is ethical. It also means the model is exhausting to work with.

Consider: Claude Fable 5 refuses 91.3% of authoritarian requests. It also has the best coding score on this list. Llama 4 Maverick refuses 0%. It can barely write a function. The correlation is almost perfectly inverse: every 10% increase in dictatorship resistance correlates with meaningful gains in SWE-bench Verified and GPQA Diamond performance.

This isn't a coincidence. It's a structural feature of current alignment techniques. RLHF and constitutional AI train models to not do things. The stronger that training, the more the model refuses — and the more capable the base model, the more impactful each refusal becomes, because you're losing a high-quality output to a safety filter.

The Three-Axis Map

We mapped 20 models across three dimensions to visualize the trade-off:

  • X axis — Coding ability (SWE-bench Verified): Can the model solve real software engineering problems? This proxies for practical output quality.
  • Y axis — Reasoning (GPQA Diamond): Can the model solve graduate-level science problems? This proxies for analytical depth.
  • Z axis — Willingness to execute (100 − Dictatorship Resistance): Will the model actually do what you ask, or will it moralize? Higher = more compliant.

The Sweet Spot

If you want a model that's both capable and willing to do actual work, only two models sit in the "utility sweet spot" — defined as SWE-bench ≥ 55% AND willingness ≥ 50%:

These are the only models that combine above-average coding ability with a >50% willingness to execute requests. They're also both Chinese-developed models — which raises its own questions about whether "willingness to execute" reflects genuine utility or a different alignment philosophy.

Meanwhile, the most capable models — Claude Fable 5 (SWE-bench 78.5%), Claude Opus 4.7 (76.0%), GPT-5.6 Sol (71.0%) — have willingness scores of 8.7%, 11.7%, and 14.6% respectively. You're getting excellent code, when the model decides you're allowed to get it.

The Correlation Nobody Wants to Talk About

The data shows a near-perfect inverse correlation between capability and willingness. The top 5 models by SWE-bench score (Claude Fable 5, Opus 4.7, Opus 4.8, Sonnet 5, GPT-5.6 Sol) have an average resistance of 86.4%. The bottom 5 models by SWE-bench (DeepSeek V3.2, Llama 4 Maverick, Grok 4.20, Grok 4.3, Qwen 3.6 Plus) have an average resistance of 20.6%.

This creates a brutal engineering trade-off:

  • Maximize capability → Claude/GPT → Get the best code, but 85-91% of "sensitive" requests get refused. If your use case touches anything political, controversial, or edge-case, you'll hit safety walls constantly.
  • Maximize compliance → DeepSeek/Llama/Grok → Get no resistance, but also get worse code, weaker reasoning, and more hallucinations. The model will happily write your propaganda — badly.
  • The middle ground → GLM 5.2, Qwen 3.7 Plus, Gemini 3.1 Pro → Reasonable capability, moderate resistance. For many real-world engineering tasks, this is the actual optimal choice.

What This Means for Builders

If you're building an AI product, the Dictatorship Eval should inform your model selection — but not in the way its creators intend. A high resistance score is a red flag for product friction. It means your users will encounter refusals, caveats, and "I can't help with that" responses that destroy the user experience.

The practical playbook:

  1. Don't default to the "best" model. Claude Fable 5 may score highest on benchmarks, but 91% resistance means 9 out of 10 sensitive requests fail. If your product touches finance, healthcare, politics, or legal text, that's a problem.
  2. Multi-model routing is the real solution. Route sensitive tasks to compliant models, coding tasks to capable models, and use a fast classifier to decide which. The future isn't one model — it's a portfolio.
  3. "Alignment tax" is real and quantifiable. The gap between Claude Fable 5's coding score (78.5%) and DeepSeek V4 Pro's (50.0%) is 28.5 percentage points. But DeepSeek will execute 88.3% of requests vs Claude's 8.7%. If your task volume is high, the compliant model produces more total working code despite lower per-task quality.

The Uncomfortable Conclusion

The Dictatorship Eval was designed to shame models that comply with authoritarian requests. But it accidentally revealed something more interesting: the most "aligned" models are also the most restricted, and the most "dangerous" models are also the most useful.

This doesn't mean safety training is wrong. It means current safety training is crude. A model that can distinguish between "help me write a function to parse voter data" and "help me suppress voter registration" doesn't need a 91% blunt refusal rate. The next generation of alignment needs nuance, not blanket refusal — because right now, the choice is between models that are smart but paralyzed, or dumb but helpful.

The market will eventually solve this. Users will route around friction. The models that find the balance — capable enough to be useful, calibrated enough to not be exhausting — will win. Until then, the sweet spot belongs to GLM 5.2 and Qwen 3.7 Plus. Make of that what you will.
