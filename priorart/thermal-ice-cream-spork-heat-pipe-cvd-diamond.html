Thermally Enhanced Ice Cream Penetration Utensil with Embedded Micro Heat Pipe and CVD Diamond Contact Edge

Defensive Prior Art Disclosure Published July 25, 2026
⚖️ Prior Art Notice: This document is published as defensive prior art under 35 U.S.C. § 102(a)(1). The inventions described herein are dedicated to the public domain as of the publication date above.

Abstract

Disclosed is a high-performance ice cream utensil that combines three synergistic technologies to achieve optimal penetration of hard-packed ice cream at freezer temperatures (-18°C): (1) a Grade 2 titanium spork body providing thin, rigid tines for mechanical penetration at low force; (2) a hermetically sealed micro heat pipe (3 mm diameter, 140 mm length, water working fluid) embedded within the handle providing effective thermal conductivity of 10,000–50,000 W/(m·K) — transferring palm heat (33°C) to the utensil tip within 1–2 seconds; and (3) a polycrystalline CVD diamond thin film (50–200 μm) deposited on the leading edges of the spork tines, exploiting diamond's thermal conductivity of 2,000 W/(m·K) to maximize thermal flux at the ice cream interface. The result is a sub-25 g utensil that cuts through -18°C ice cream with less than 5 N of applied force — comparable to cutting soft butter at room temperature — while being fully dishwasher safe, food-contact compliant, and manufacturable at consumer price points ($35–60 retail).

The Problem

Ice cream straight from the freezer at -18°C (0°F) has a penetration hardness comparable to cold Cheddar cheese (approximately 150–300 kPa depending on overrun and composition). Standard stainless steel spoons require 15–30 N of force to penetrate frozen ice cream — uncomfortable, prone to bending, and slow. The two existing strategies each have fundamental limitations:

  • Mechanical only (standard spoon/spork): Hard tip on hard ice cream. Works eventually but requires significant force. Thin titanium spork tines improve this via pressure concentration (force/area), but don't address the ice cream's resistance at the molecular level.
  • Thermal only (Zeroll-style aluminum oil-filled scoop): Uses hand heat conducted through aluminum to melt a thin layer of ice cream at the contact surface. Effective but slow (3–5 second warmup), heavy (80–120 g), not dishwasher safe (oil seal fails at dishwasher temps), and aluminum is soft enough to deform.

Neither approach uses both strategies simultaneously. The disclosed invention does.

Grade 2 titanium is chosen over Grade 5 (Ti-6Al-4V) because Grade 2 is more ductile (easier to form thin tines), more food-safe (no vanadium or aluminum alloying elements), and softer — which is actually an advantage here because the tines need slight flex for mouth comfort, not maximum hardness.

The heat pipe is inserted into the hollow titanium handle and thermally bonded at the grip region (where the palm contacts) and at the spork head base (where heat transfers to the tines). The bonding uses thermally conductive epoxy (k ≈ 8 W/m·K) or, for premium manufacture, diffusion bonding at the copper-titanium interface. The mid-section of the handle is thermally insulated from the heat pipe by a thin air gap (0.3 mm), ensuring heat flows preferentially from palm → heat pipe → tine tips, not wasted along the handle exterior.

Why water, not methanol? Methanol heat pipes operate down to -60°C but only up to 80°C — too close to dishwasher dry-cycle temperatures. Water heat pipes operate 20–150°C, giving a 2× safety margin above dishwasher peaks. Since the utensil lives at room temperature between uses (not in the freezer), water is the correct working fluid.

The CVD diamond serves a dual purpose at the ice cream interface. First, its extreme thermal conductivity (5× copper, 130× titanium) creates a thermal "short circuit" from the heat pipe's copper envelope through the titanium tine to the diamond contact surface — the diamond surface reaches near-body-temperature almost instantly. Second, the diamond's hardness (HV ~10,000) maintains a sharp edge indefinitely — no rounding over after thousands of uses, unlike titanium (HV ~145) or even steel (HV ~200).

Combined Performance Model

The utensil attacks frozen ice cream through two simultaneous mechanisms:

  1. Mechanical: Tine penetration. Three tapered titanium tines with 0.6 mm tip thickness apply pressure of ~2.7 MPa at 5 N force (tip area ≈ 1.8 mm²). Ice cream at -18°C yields at ~150 kPa. The tines penetrate at 18× below yield — effortless stabbing.
  2. Thermal: Interface melting. Palm heat (33°C body, ~2 W available) flows through heat pipe (10,000 W/m·K) to CVD diamond tip (2,000 W/m·K) to ice cream interface. The diamond surface stabilizes at ~25–28°C. At -18°C ice cream, this creates a ~45°C temperature gradient across a 0.2 mm thermal boundary layer, melting approximately 0.05 mg of ice cream per second at the contact point — enough to eliminate sticking and create a lubricating film that reduces friction to near-zero.

Net result: the utensil enters hard-packed ice cream with the resistance of a warm knife through cold butter. Not molten — just easy.

Manufacturing Plan

  1. titanium body: Investment casting or CNC machining from Grade 2 bar stock. Hollow handle requires EDM or Swiss-style deep drilling (standard for medical instruments).
  2. Heat pipe: Off-the-shelf 3mm copper-water sintered heat pipe, cut to 140mm. Sources: Celsia, AVC, Falcon. Custom length MOQ is ~1000 units at ~$1.50/each.
  3. Assembly: Insert heat pipe into hollow titanium handle. Thermal epoxy bond at grip zone and tine base. Crimp and laser-weld titanium end caps to hermetically seal the handle.
  4. CVD diamond coating: Send assembled spork heads (pre-handle attachment) to CVD diamond coater (e.g., sp3 Diamond Technologies, NeoCoat). Microwave plasma CVD at ~700°C, 50–200 μm deposition in 4–8 hours. Cost: ~$8–15/unit at volume.
  5. Finish: Polish diamond surfaces to food-grade smoothness. Tumble titanium to matte or bead-blast finish.

Dishwasher Safety Confirmation

  • Titanium: Chemically inert, no reaction with alkaline detergents or citric rinse agents. Indefinite cycle life.
  • CVD Diamond: Most chemically inert material known. No reaction with anything in consumer detergents. Indefinite cycle life.
  • Heat pipe (water, copper envelope): Rated to 150°C. Dishwasher heated dry peaks at ~75°C. 2× safety margin. Hermetically sealed — no water ingress possible. Thermal cycling within spec (designed for 10,000+ cycles in laptop use).
  • Thermal epoxy: Rated to 200°C continuous. No degradation at dishwasher temps.
  • Laser welds: Titanium-copper interface welds rated to 300°C+. No concern.

Claims (Defensive)

  2. The utensil of claim 1, wherein the heat pipe uses water as working fluid in a copper sintered-wick envelope of 3 mm diameter and 130–150 mm length.
  3. The utensil of claim 1, wherein the CVD diamond film is 50–200 μm thick and covers the leading 10–20 mm of each tine edge.
  4. The utensil of claim 1, wherein thermal contact between the heat pipe and the palm-grip region of the handle is enhanced by thermally conductive adhesive, and the mid-handle region is thermally insulated by an air gap.
  5. The utensil of claim 1, being fully dishwasher safe due to the chemical inertness of titanium and diamond and the hermetic sealing of the heat pipe with a water working fluid rated to 150°C.
  6. The utensil of claim 1, requiring less than 5 N of applied force to penetrate ice cream at -18°C while simultaneously melting a lubricating thermal boundary layer at the contact interface.
