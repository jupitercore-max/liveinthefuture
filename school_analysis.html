Executive Overview

This analysis covers 20+ schools across 4 categories: elite private (6-12), smaller private/alternative (K-8), public (MPCSD & Las Lomitas), and the destination high school (M-A). Every claim is sourced. Where data is unavailable, that absence is noted — a school's refusal to publish outcome data tells you something.

The Three Questions Nobody Answers

School brochures give you test scores, teacher ratios, and campus photos. They never answer the three questions that actually matter for a rational decision:

💰 1. What's the real cost? (It's not tuition.)

Private school tuition is capital that could be compounding in the S&P 500. Nueva K-12 costs $669K in tuition but $3.28 million in foregone market returns by age 28. Even a 7-year stint at Menlo School ($65K/yr) represents $1.87M in opportunity cost. Sacred Heart at $47K/yr is the "cheapest" elite private — still $1.38M foregone. Meanwhile, MPCSD public schools cost $0 in tuition, score 75-84% on CAASPP math (vs 34% state avg), and La Entrada (Las Lomitas) at 83% math proficiency outperforms every MPCSD school. Full analysis →

🎓 2. Do expensive schools actually send more kids to better colleges?

The uncomfortable answer: not obviously. CDE College-Going Rate data shows public Carlmont High sends 18.8% of graduates to UC (109 of 580) — a higher rate than the self-reported numbers from most private schools. Palo Alto High: 16.2%. Gunn: 14.1% with the highest overall college-going rate (91.2%). Private schools don't report to CDE, so we're comparing their polished marketing claims against audited public data. Menlo School claims 30 Stanford admits over 3 years. Harker's 1514 avg SAT and ~10% HYPSM rate is the strongest on paper. But Carlmont does it for free. Full analysis →

☠️ 3. What does the commute actually cost in safety?

Nobody includes commute mortality risk in school evaluations. They should. Using county-specific FARS fatality data: Harker accumulates 110.9 micromorts per year (a micromort = 1-in-a-million chance of death). Over K-12, that's a 1-in-900 chance of a fatal accident just driving to school — equivalent to 158 skydiving jumps. Harker's rate is 65× higher than Menlo School (1.7 µMort/yr) because US-101 through Santa Clara County is the 3rd deadliest highway stretch in the US. The route matters more than the distance: GISSV via 101 accumulates nearly 4× the risk of Nueva via I-280, despite only 25% more distance. Local schools (Menlo School, Sacred Heart, MPCSD) are effectively zero risk. Full analysis →

Traditional Findings

  • For math-obsessed kids: Nueva (USAMO/IMO winners, math through Complex Analysis) and Harker (MATHCOUNTS champions, AMC/AIME pipeline) are in a different league. Everyone else offers "honors tracks" but not competition-level acceleration.
  • For college placement: Menlo School publishes the most transparent data — 30 students to Stanford over 3 years. Castilleja's 51-student class punches way above its weight. Harker's 1514 avg SAT and ~10% HYPSM rate is the strongest on paper.
  • For value: MPCSD public schools at $0 tuition with $27K/student in public spending. La Entrada (Las Lomitas) at 83% math proficiency is the public standout.
  • For affordability in private: Sacred Heart ($47K) and Crystal Springs (~$49K) are cheapest among elite privates. Catholic parish schools (Nativity) are ~$10-16K.
  • The AP question: Castilleja, Menlo, Nueva, and Crystal Springs have all abandoned AP. Only Sacred Heart (28 AP courses) and Harker (27 AP) still run traditional AP programs. This is a national trend among elite independents.

Recent Issues

Key insight: M-A's 54% math proficiency masks a bimodal distribution. The top-track students (41% of AP test-takers scoring 5) are very well served. The school-wide average is dragged down by the much more diverse and lower-income catchment beyond MPCSD. For a student coming from Hillview's compaction track, the experience at M-A will look very different from the average. The question is whether you want your child in that bimodal environment or in a more homogeneous private school.

💰 The S&P 500 Alternative: What If You Invested the Tuition Instead?

Private school tuition is not just a yearly expense. It's capital that could be compounding in the market. This section calculates the opportunity cost of private school tuition invested in an S&P 500 index fund instead, at two milestones: high school graduation (age 18) and age 28 (when early career outcomes are measurable).

This is not an argument against private school. Education returns are real but hard to quantify. This is the financial cost you're implicitly paying on top of tuition — the growth your money would have earned.

Assumptions

  • S&P 500 return: 10.5% nominal annual (1957-2025 historical average, including dividends reinvested)
  • Public school cost: $0 incremental tuition (property taxes fund it regardless)
  • Contributions: Tuition amount invested at the start of each school year
  • After school ends: No new contributions; existing balance continues compounding
  • Pre-tax: Capital gains taxes would reduce these numbers (~15-20% federal LTCG). However, a 529 or UTMA account structure could shelter some or all gains.
  • No tuition increases: Using current tuition held flat (conservative — tuition typically rises 3-5%/year, which would make these numbers higher)

The headline numbers are sobering. Sending a child to Nueva from 3rd grade through 12th means foregoing $3.3 million in potential market returns by the time they turn 28. That's not a tuition bill — it's a trust fund. Castilleja at $1.8M and Menlo at $1.9M aren't far behind.

But the numbers need context:

  • 10.5% is the long-run average, not a guarantee. The S&P returned -37% in 2008 and +29% in 2019. Actual returns depend heavily on the specific decade. A 7% inflation-adjusted return gives roughly 60% of these figures.
  • Taxes erode the gains. Long-term capital gains at 15-20% federal (plus ~13% CA state) would reduce the final number significantly. A 529 plan or UTMA account can shelter some of this, but with restrictions.
  • Education returns are real but unmeasurable. If private school gets your child into a better college, better network, better career trajectory — that has compounding returns too. The Raj Chetty mobility data shows elite college attendance has a measurable income premium, but it's entangled with selection effects (kids who get into elite colleges may have succeeded regardless).
  • The K-8 schools are the value play. Nativity at $13K/year or Peninsula at $36K/year for 3-6 years gives the differentiated elementary experience, then free public high school at M-A (which sends plenty of kids to UCs and Stanford). The opportunity cost is $454K-$1.2M instead of $1.8M-$3.3M.
  • The real question: Is the marginal educational benefit of Nueva over MPCSD public schools worth $3.3M to your child at age 28? That's the question this table forces you to ask honestly.

☠️ Commute Risk: Accumulated Micromorts

A micromort is a 1-in-a-million chance of death. It's how risk analysts compare activities that seem incomparable — one skydive (7 µMort), one scuba dive (5 µMort), one day of skiing (0.7 µMort). The concept was developed by Ronald A. Howard at Stanford — fitting, given where these schools are.

Nobody includes commute mortality risk in school evaluations. They should. The numbers below use county-specific fatality rates from SWITRS/FARS data rather than the national average, because the routes matter. The Menlo Park → San Jose commute (US-101 through Santa Clara County) is one of the deadliest stretches of highway in the United States (3rd overall, 78 fatalities 2015-2019). The Menlo Park → Hillsborough route (I-280 through San Mateo County) is significantly safer.

Key insight: San Mateo and Santa Clara counties are 2-3× safer per mile than the national average — suburban infrastructure, higher incomes (newer cars, more safety features), less impairment. But US-101 specifically is above the national average rate. The corridor your child commutes on matters more than the county average.

The Corridor Problem: 101 vs 280 vs Local Roads

Not all miles are equal. The route to each school determines which fatality rate applies:

  • US-101: High volume, truck traffic, aggressive merges, 6-8 lanes. Santa Clara County segment is the 3rd deadliest highway stretch in the US. Rate: ~1.4 deaths/100M VMT (above national average). Used by: Harker, GISSV.
  • I-280: Often called the "world's most beautiful freeway." Lower volume, no trucks (prohibited in some segments), better sight lines, divided by wide median. Rate: ~0.3-0.4 deaths/100M VMT (estimated from lower fatality counts on this segment). Used by: Crystal Springs, Nueva, Woodside.
  • El Camino Real / local arterials: Lower speed (35-45 mph) but higher pedestrian/bicycle conflict, more intersections, more distraction opportunities. Per-mile rate lower than freeway but per-hour rate potentially comparable. Used by: Castilleja, Menlo School, MPCSD schools.

Counter-intuitive: Highway miles are more dangerous per mile but safer per hour than arterial roads (you cover more distance in less exposure time). A 22-mile freeway commute on I-280 may be comparable in risk to a 10-mile stop-and-go arterial commute — but takes less time.

Normalized Comparison: The Full Pipeline vs Just a Few Years

The raw "Total µMort" column is misleading when comparing schools with different enrollment spans. A school you attend for 10 years will naturally accumulate more risk than one you attend for 3 years, regardless of commute distance. The Normalized 7yr column levels the playing field:

  • Per-year rate is what matters for comparison. Harker at 110.9 µMort/year is 65× more dangerous per year than Menlo School at 1.7 µMort/year. This ratio holds whether you attend for 3 years or 10.
  • Route dominates distance. GISSV (30 miles via 101) accumulates 529 normalized µMort — nearly 4× more than Nueva (24 miles via 280) at 141, despite only 25% more distance. The 101 corridor is that much deadlier.
  • Harker full pipeline (K-12): 1,109 total µMort ≈ a 1-in-900 chance of a fatal accident just from school commuting. Normalized to 7 years: 776 µMort (111 skydives).
  • Same distance, different corridor: A hypothetical 44-mile commute on I-280 instead of 101 would yield ~370 µMort over 10 years — one-third the risk.

What This Means in Human Terms

  • Harker (K-12 via 101): 1,109 accumulated µMort ≈ 1-in-900 chance of a fatal car accident just from the school commute. That's 158 skydiving jumps, or the risk of ~2 tours in Afghanistan (UK military data).
  • GISSV (K-6 via 101): 454 µMort in just 6 years — the 101 corridor rate makes even a shorter enrollment period dangerous. Normalized to 7yr: 529 µMort (76 skydives).
  • Nueva (K-12 via 280): 202 µMort over 10 years. The I-280 route cuts risk dramatically vs a 101 commute of similar distance. Still 29 skydives.
  • Crystal Springs (6-12 via 280): 130 µMort. Moderate but manageable — the 280 corridor keeps this from being alarming.
  • Menlo School / Sacred Heart (local roads): 12 µMort over 7 years — 2 skydives. Essentially a rounding error.
  • MPCSD public schools: 4-18 µMort total. Walking/biking distance for many families. Approaching zero risk.

Time Cost Compounds the Risk

The micromort calculation only captures mortality risk. It doesn't capture the time cost, which compounds the health impact:

  • Harker: ~44 miles RT × 180 days = 7,920 miles/year. At 30 mph average (Bay Area traffic on 101), that's 264 hours/year in the car — 11 full days. Over 10 years: 110 days of your life spent commuting to school.
  • Nueva: ~24 miles RT → 144 hours/year → 60 days over 10 years. (280 traffic is lighter, so effective speed is higher — closer to 90 hours/year.)
  • Menlo School: ~2 miles RT → 12 hours/year → 3.5 days over 7 years.

Those 110 days at Harker could be spent on sleep, homework, extracurriculars, family time, or literally anything else. This is a real cost that never appears on a school's brochure.

Caveats & Methodology

  • County rates are lower than national average. San Mateo (0.47/100M VMT) and Santa Clara (0.56/100M VMT) counties are 2-3× safer than the US average (1.26). This reflects newer vehicles, higher seatbelt compliance, less impaired driving, and better infrastructure. However, US-101 through Santa Clara specifically exceeds the national average (~1.4/100M VMT) due to volume, truck traffic, and design.
  • Corridor rates are estimated. FARS doesn't publish fatality rates by specific highway segment × county. We derived the 101 corridor rate from 78 fatalities over 5 years on the Santa Clara segment (ValuePenguin/FARS), divided by estimated segment VMT. The I-280 rate is estimated from its significantly lower fatality count despite comparable length. These are directional, not precise.
  • Adjusted driver risk is lower. A seatbelted, sober, attentive driver in a modern car has roughly 1/3 the population average risk — perhaps 1 µMort per 130-250 miles (LessWrong analysis). Our county-specific rates already partially reflect this (Bay Area demographics skew toward safer drivers).
  • Teenage drivers face 2-3× the per-mile risk of adult drivers (IIHS). If your child drives themselves to Harker in high school on 101, their personal risk approximately doubles for those years.
  • Carpooling and school buses change the per-family calculus but don't eliminate risk — they redistribute it across fewer trips.
  • The comparison isn't "don't go to Harker." It's "factor this into the decision alongside everything else." A 1-in-900 risk spread over 10 years is small in absolute terms — but it's not zero, and nobody ever tells you about it.

Limitations & What's Missing

Data We Couldn't Get

  • UC enrollment data (public schools only) — CDE College-Going Rate data gives us real UC enrollment numbers for public schools. Private school UC enrollment is only available through self-reporting or the UC Information Center Tableau dashboard (searchable one school at a time).
  • Private school test scores — Private schools are not required to administer CAASPP. There is no apples-to-apples comparison between public and private school academic performance at the school level.
  • Acceptance rates — Most elite privates don't publish this. Estimates (20-25%) come from admissions consultants and should be treated as rough.
  • Parent satisfaction surveys — Niche.com reviews exist but are self-selected and small-sample. Google reviews for schools are unreliable.
  • Diversity data for several schools — Crystal Springs, Nueva, Woodland, Keys, GISSV, INTL, PBS don't publish ethnic breakdowns.
  • Endowment data — Most schools don't disclose. Only Castilleja (~$75M) and Harker ($11M) have confirmed figures.
  • Class-level outcomes — No school publishes "students who entered in 6th grade and were accepted to T20 colleges" type data.
  • St. Raymond School — Could not confirm this Menlo Park parish school currently operates as a K-8. May be closed or merged.
  • Tuition for several schools — Woodland hides tuition behind an inquiry form. Nativity doesn't publish. GISSV grade-by-grade breakdown is unclear.

What These Absences Tell You

A school that doesn't publish outcome data is making a choice. Menlo School publishes a 3-year college matriculation list with specific counts per university. Crystal Springs says "100% go to 4-year colleges." These are not equivalent levels of transparency, and the gap is informative. Schools with strong outcomes have every incentive to publish them.
