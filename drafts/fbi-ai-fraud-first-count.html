The FBI Counted AI Fraud for the First Time. It Found $893 Million. The Real Number Is Closer to $9 Billion.

The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report introduced a dedicated AI section logging 22,000 complaints and $893 million in losses. Cross-referencing with FTC reporting rates, Hiya consumer surveys, and UNODC trafficking data reveals the official figure captures roughly one dollar in ten.

Elena Vasquez · Defense & Cybersecurity · April 21, 2026 · ☕ 11 min read

Twenty-two thousand complaints. That is the number the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center logged in its first dedicated count of AI-enabled fraud, published in the 2025 Internet Crime Report released April 7, 2026. Attached dollar figure: $893 million, which amounts to 4.3% of the report's total of $21 billion in internet crime losses from over one million complaints.

Not a rounding error. Not the real number. It is the first reading from a broken thermometer.

What the FBI Actually Counted

The IC3's new AI section tracks complaints where the victim or the reporting agent identified artificial intelligence as a component of the scheme. That includes voice cloning used to impersonate family members, deepfake video deployed in business email compromise, synthetic identity documents generated to open fraudulent accounts, and AI-written phishing at a sophistication level that defeats standard email filters.

That $893 million breaks down across categories the FBI already tracks. Crypto investment fraud, the report's largest single bucket at $8.6 billion in total losses, increasingly uses AI-generated personas and deepfake "proof-of-life" videos to sustain pig-butchering operations. Business email compromise, still worth $3 billion annually to attackers, now deploys AI voice cloning to bypass the "call them back and verify" defense that security teams spent a decade teaching employees. Tech support scams, $2.1 billion in 2025, use AI chatbots to sustain longer, more convincing interactions with targets.

Government impersonation scams alone generated 32,000 complaints. IC3's report does not say how many used AI voice synthesis, but the Hiya data discussed below, drawn from 12,000 consumers across six countries surveyed at Mobile World Congress in March 2026, suggests the number is substantial and growing at a pace that outstrips the FBI's ability to categorize it.

The Consumer Side: 1 in 4 Americans

Hiya's State of the Call 2026 report, released March 2 at Mobile World Congress, surveyed 12,000 consumers across six countries. Its headline finding: one in four Americans received a deepfake voice call in the past twelve months. Another 24% said they were not confident they could distinguish a cloned voice from a real one. Combined, nearly half of American adults are either exposed to or vulnerable to AI voice fraud.

Americans now receive an average of 9.9 unwanted calls per week, over 500 per year, growing at 16% annually since 2023. When Hiya asked consumers who is winning the battle between carriers and scammers, Americans chose scammers by a two-to-one margin. Thirty-eight percent said they would switch mobile providers if they felt unprotected, sixty-seven percent believe carriers should bear financial responsibility for scam losses, and fifty-five percent want zero-liability fraud protection comparable to what credit card companies offer, a constellation of consumer sentiment that points unmistakably toward regulatory intervention.

Seniors aged 55 and older lose an average of $1,298 per scam incident, triple the amount younger adults report, a disparity that compounds across the 500-plus unwanted calls each American now receives annually. Forty-eight percent of all respondents said phone spam is getting worse, outnumbering those who said it was improving by three to one.

The Global Supply Chain of Fraud

While the FBI counts complaints filed by American victims, the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime has been documenting the industrial infrastructure that generates those complaints. UNODC reports that scam compounds in Southeast Asia, primarily in Myanmar, Cambodia, and Laos, have evolved into vertically integrated fraud operations that use AI for deepfakes, voice cloning, malware generation, and victim targeting algorithms.

Scale is staggering and well-documented. UNODC estimates that the United States alone lost $10 billion to Southeast Asian scam operations in 2024. Raids by Philippine and Cambodian authorities have uncovered forced labor conditions in these compounds, including torture, trafficking, and workers held against their will to meet fraud quotas. A Global Partnership Against Online Scams, launched in December 2025 with participation from 60 countries and platforms including Meta and TikTok, is attempting coordination. But the operations keep scaling despite international pressure.

These compounds now sell cybercrime-as-a-service to buyers worldwide. A local scam group in any country can purchase AI-generated voice models, pre-trained phishing scripts, and victim lead lists from Southeast Asian suppliers. Those 22,000 complaints represent the visible tip of a global manufacturing operation that sells AI-powered fraud tools to anyone with a cryptocurrency wallet and a list of phone numbers, an operation whose supply chain stretches from server farms in Myanmar to call centers in Cambodia to victim bank accounts in every American state.

The Undercount Math

Here is the original calculation that no one has published, based on cross-referencing three independent federal and international data sources to derive a plausible multiplier for what the FBI's voluntary complaint system missed. Three compounding gaps separate the FBI's $893 million from reality.

Gap 1: Reporting. The FTC's 2017 Mass-Market Consumer Fraud Survey, the most rigorous study of fraud underreporting in the United States, found that only 4.8% of consumer fraud victims filed a complaint with any government agency. IC3 is one of several such agencies, meaning its capture rate is lower still. If the IC3 receives complaints from roughly 3-5% of victims, a conservative reporting multiplier is 20-33x. Applied to the $893 million: $17.9 billion to $29.5 billion.

That range is too high, because not all unreported fraud involves AI, so apply a correction.

Gap 2: Attribution. When a victim falls for a phishing email written by an AI language model, they report "phishing," not "AI fraud." When a synthetic identity assembled from AI-generated documents opens a credit line, the lender reports "identity theft." The FBI's AI category captures only those cases where AI involvement was explicitly identified. Given that detection is difficult even for experts (human deepfake detection rates hover around 24.5%, per aggregated research), the attribution gap is wide. If half of AI-involved fraud is correctly attributed, the effective capture rate drops to 1.5-2.5%.

Gap 3: Geography. FBI counts domestic complaints only, but UNODC says Americans lost $10 billion to Southeast Asian operations alone in 2024. Most of those losses flow through channels that never intersect with IC3 reporting: cryptocurrency wallets, international wire transfers, and gift card purchases that are never reversed.

Our estimate: Start with the FBI's $893 million. Apply a conservative 10x multiplier for the combined reporting and attribution gaps (lower than the raw FTC data implies, but accounting for some overlap between Gap 1 and Gap 2), and the result is approximately $9 billion. Cross-check: UNODC's $10 billion figure for Southeast Asian operations targeting the US alone, which includes both AI and non-AI fraud, makes $9 billion in total AI-enabled fraud losses plausible without being aggressive.

Nine billion dollars, not 4.3% of the FBI's total internet crime figure but closer to 43%.

Why Measurement Matters More Than the Number

Specifics matter less than what they reveal about institutional capacity. That $893 million is not wrong; it is simply the best count available from a system that was designed before AI-enabled fraud existed at scale. IC3 relies on voluntary self-reporting by victims who must recognize they have been defrauded, identify the mechanism as AI-related, navigate to the IC3 website, and complete a complaint form. Each step filters out the majority of actual incidents.

Compare this to how the Bureau of Labor Statistics measures unemployment, a domain where passive self-reporting was abandoned decades ago in favor of active survey methodology. BLS does not wait for unemployed people to file complaints. It conducts the Current Population Survey, a monthly sample of 60,000 households with trained interviewers, statistical weighting, and rigorous methodology. Its result is not perfect, but it is a measurement system designed to capture reality rather than wait for it to self-report.

AI fraud has no equivalent to the BLS, just an IC3 that counts whoever walks through the door.

The Phone Is Becoming Unusable

Step back from the dollar figures and consider the infrastructure damage, the slow, compounding degradation of a communications network that 330 million Americans depend on daily. Alexander Graham Bell's invention was designed to connect people, but in 2026, the average American receives 500 unwanted calls per year, and that number grows 16% annually. At current rates, by 2030 it will exceed 900. Nobody answers anymore. Hiya's data shows the trend is accelerating, not stabilizing.

This is not just an annoyance metric. It is a functional degradation of communications infrastructure, a slow-motion collapse of trust in a network that every hospital, every school, every emergency service, and every family depends on for contact that matters. When two-thirds of consumers say carriers should bear financial liability for scam calls, they are articulating a market failure: the telephone network generates negative externalities for its users, and no entity currently bears the cost. Credit card networks solved this decades ago with zero-liability policies that shifted incentives toward fraud prevention. No telephone industry equivalent exists, and with 67% of consumers demanding shared carrier liability, the regulatory question is not whether zero-liability fraud protection arrives but when.

STIR/SHAKEN, the caller ID authentication framework mandated by the FCC, has reduced some forms of spoofing. But AI voice cloning operates within authenticated calls. Caller ID is real, but the person on the other end is not.

What We Did Not Prove

The 10x multiplier is a reasoned estimate, not an audited figure. FTC's 4.8% reporting rate comes from a 2017 survey conducted before AI fraud reached its current scale. If AI fraud victims report at different rates than traditional fraud victims (plausible, since many do not realize AI was involved), the multiplier could be significantly higher or lower.

UNODC's $10 billion figure for US losses to Southeast Asian operations has not been independently verified through financial audit. It is derived from law enforcement seizure data, victim interviews, and financial flow analysis.

Attribution is the weakest link in the chain. No study has measured what percentage of fraud involving AI tools is correctly identified as AI-related by victims. That 50% assumption is a placeholder based on the known difficulty of deepfake detection, and true figures could range anywhere from 20% to 80%, which means the final multiplier carries considerable uncertainty in both directions.

This analysis counts losses, not net harm, and some fraud losses are recovered through chargebacks, insurance, or law enforcement asset seizure. IC3 reports that its Recovery Asset Team successfully froze $561 million in 2025, but that represents less than 3% of total reported losses.

The Strongest Case Against This Analysis

The most compelling objection: the 10x multiplier assumes AI fraud behaves like traditional fraud in terms of reporting rates, when it might not. AI-enabled scams that target high-value victims, like the Arup deepfake video call that stole $25 million, are more likely to be reported than a $200 voice-cloning scam targeting a senior. If large-dollar AI fraud has a higher reporting rate than the FTC's 4.8% baseline, then the multiplier for dollar losses is lower, even if the multiplier for complaint volume remains high.

This is a legitimate structural objection that cuts both ways, and it means the $9 billion figure could be overstated for dollar losses while being understated for victim count. Both versions of the error point to the same conclusion: measurement is inadequate.

What You Can Do

If you receive an unexpected call from someone you know asking for money: Hang up and call them back at a number you already have saved. Do not use a number the caller provides. AI voice clones are convincing in real time but cannot survive a callback to a verified number. This single habit blocks the majority of voice-cloning scams.

If your organization processes payments: Implement dual-authorization for any transaction above your risk threshold, with the second authorization occurring on a different channel than the request. A Slack request confirmed by a phone call, or a phone request confirmed by an in-person verification. Arup's attack succeeded because a single video call was treated as sufficient authorization for $25 million.

If you are a policymaker: Push for a national fraud survey equivalent to the BLS employment methodology. Our current system, waiting for victims to self-report to IC3, was designed for an era when fraud was slower, simpler, and more visible. AI fraud at scale requires active measurement, not passive collection.

If you are a carrier: The 67% of consumers who want carriers to share financial liability for scam calls are telling you something about where regulation is heading. Zero-liability fraud protection, modeled on credit card networks, would realign incentives. Carriers that move first will capture the 38% of consumers actively considering switching.

The Bottom Line

FBI counted AI fraud for the first time, and that matters. The number it found, $893 million, does not capture the reality that three independent data sources converge on: total AI-enabled fraud losses in the United States are likely approaching $9 billion annually, the phone network is functionally degrading at 16% per year, and the measurement infrastructure is a generation behind the threat. The first count is a beginning. Treating it as the answer is the most dangerous possible response.
