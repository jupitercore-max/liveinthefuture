# GLP-1 Drugs vs. Alcohol: The $32.6 Billion Collision

## Journalist: Dr. Sanjay Mehta — Longevity & Health Economics
## Category: 🩺 Health

## Core Thesis
Three studies published in a single week of July 2026 — a BMJ Open multi-target trial emulation (40,703 patients), an ADA target trial emulation (162,014 patients), and a Lancet RCT (108 patients) — converge on one finding: GLP-1 receptor agonists cut alcohol-related hospitalizations by 26% to 65%, depending on population and comparator. The US spent $32.6 billion on alcohol-related hospitalizations in 2022 alone. The approved AUD drugs (naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram) barely move hospitalization rates. The drugs that work aren't approved for AUD. The ones approved for AUD don't work.

## Key Data Points

### BMJ Open Study (Rodriguez et al., July 2026)
- DOI: 10.1136/bmjopen-2025-109259
- N = 40,703 adults with AUD + T2D or obesity
- Period: Jan 2018 – Dec 2024
- 4 target trial emulations:
  - ADM (anti-diabetic med) trial: GLP-1 vs other diabetes meds → **26% lower risk** of alcohol-related hospitalization
  - AOM (anti-obesity med) trial: GLP-1 vs other obesity meds → **32% lower risk**
  - MAUD-T2D trial: GLP-1 vs AUD drugs (naltrexone/acamprosate/disulfiram) in T2D patients → **63% lower risk**
  - MAUD-obesity trial: GLP-1 vs AUD drugs in obese patients → **65% lower risk**
- Limitations: AUD is under-captured due to stigma, treatment effects may differ in average clinical settings, socioeconomic confounding (GLP-1s are expensive → patients with access may differ)

### ADA 2026 Poster (1714-P)
- N = 162,014 semaglutide vs SGLT2i; 69,166 tirzepatide vs SGLT2i
- Semaglutide: HR 0.54 (46% lower risk) vs SGLT2i
- Tirzepatide: HR 0.61 (39% lower risk) vs SGLT2i
- Direct comparison (89,624 patients): semaglutide vs tirzepatide HR 0.98 → essentially equivalent
- Consistent across secondary outcomes and subgroups

### Lancet RCT (2026)
- DOI: 10.1016/S0140-6736(26)00305-3
- Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled
- N = 108 (54 per arm), AUD + obesity
- Side effects: nausea 57% vs 7%, appetite loss 35% vs 15%
- Only 1 serious AE in semaglutide group (abdominal pain)

### Swedish Cohort (Lähteenvuo et al., 2025)
- N = 227,868 Swedish patients with AUD
- Semaglutide: adjusted HR 0.64 (lowest hospitalization risk)
- Conventional AUD drugs (disulfiram, acamprosate): aHR = 0.98 → NO significant reduction
- Key finding: approved AUD drugs DON'T reduce hospitalizations; unapproved GLP-1s DO

### Hair Loss Studies (BMJ + JAAD + nference, July 22-23 2026)
- GLP-1 users: 37% higher alopecia odds vs SGLT-2, 68% vs DPP-4
- Actual rates low: 3-9 per 1,000 per year
- Tirzepatide: 4.24% vs semaglutide: 3.33% new-onset alopecia
- Likely mechanism: rapid weight loss → caloric stress → micronutrient deficiency

## US Hospitalization Data (JAMA Network Open, 2024)
- 12.9 million alcohol-related hospitalizations, 2016-2022
- Annual: ~1.84 million/year
- Cost: **$32.6 billion in 2022**
- 688 per 100,000 annual rate
- In-hospital mortality: 2.4% (2016) → 3.1% (2022)
- Mean stay: 5.6 → 6.2 days
- Self-directed discharges: 5.0% → 6.3%

## NIAAA Economic Data
- Total cost of alcohol misuse: $249 billion (2010)
- Alcohol-related ED visits: $15.3 billion (2014)
- Alcohol-associated liver disease: $31 billion (2022)

## ORIGINAL CALCULATION: Cost-Per-Hospitalization-Averted

### GLP-1 Drug Costs
- Ozempic/Wegovy (semaglutide): ~$1,000-1,350/month → $12,000-16,200/year
- Mounjaro/Zepbound (tirzepatide): ~$1,000-1,060/month → $12,000-12,720/year
- Midpoint estimate: $13,000/year

### Average Cost Per Alcohol-Related Hospitalization
- $32.6 billion / 1.84 million hospitalizations = ~$17,717 per hospitalization

### Eligible Population Estimate
- ~29.5M Americans with AUD (NIAAA/SAMHSA)
- ~37M with T2D; ~42% of adults (138M) obese
- Overlap: AUD + (T2D or obesity) ≈ 15-18M eligible
- Currently receiving AUD treatment: ~7-8% = ~2.3M
- Currently on any GLP-1: ~6M Americans (2024 estimate)

### The Math That Matters
If 26% of the $32.6B in alcohol hospitalizations were averted:
- Savings: $8.48 billion/year
- BUT: this assumes all AUD patients are on GLP-1s, which isn't realistic

More realistic: Among GLP-1 users with co-occurring AUD
- If 10% of current GLP-1 users (~600K) have undiagnosed AUD
- Average hospitalization rate for AUD: ~35 per 1,000/year
- Expected hospitalizations: 600K × 0.035 = 21,000/year
- At 26% reduction: 5,460 hospitalizations averted
- At $17,717 each: ~$96.7M saved
- Incremental drug cost for these patients: $0 (they're already taking GLP-1 for other reasons)

### The Accidental Savings
**Key insight nobody has stated:** GLP-1 drugs are already being prescribed to millions for obesity and diabetes. Among those patients, the alcohol hospitalization reduction is a free side effect — no additional drug cost. The savings are happening silently, untracked, because nobody is screening GLP-1 patients for AUD.

### FDA Approval Gap
- FDA-approved AUD drugs: naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram
- None of these significantly reduce alcohol-related hospitalizations (Lähteenvuo: aHR 0.98)
- GLP-1s reduce hospitalizations by 26-65% across every subpopulation studied
- But GLP-1s have no AUD indication → no insurance coverage for AUD use
- Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk have no registered AUD trials → no financial incentive (AUD patients are not the market they want)

## Strongest Counterargument
Socioeconomic confounding. GLP-1 drugs cost $12,000-16,000/year. Patients who get them tend to have better insurance, more healthcare engagement, and higher socioeconomic status — all of which independently reduce hospitalizations. The BMJ Open authors explicitly acknowledge this: "newer GLP-1 receptor agonists are higher-cost therapies and patients with access to these medications may differ from comparator groups." The 63-65% reductions in the MAUD trials (vs AUD drugs) showed reduced non-alcohol-related hospitalizations too, suggesting the GLP-1 group was generally healthier. The 26-32% figures from the ADM/AOM trials are more conservative and robust.

## Limitations to Acknowledge
- AUD is systematically under-documented (stigma)
- No study was specifically designed to test GLP-1s for AUD
- All observational data except the Lancet RCT (N=108, underpowered for hospitalizations)
- No data on how the effect varies by AUD severity
- Hair loss risk (4-9 per 1,000/year) adds a cosmetic side effect profile
- We can't separate GLP-1 effect from weight-loss-mediated behavioral change

## Actionable Takeaway
If you're already on Ozempic, Wegovy, or Mounjaro for weight or diabetes: ask your doctor to screen for AUD. You may already be getting the benefit. If you have AUD and obesity or T2D: discuss GLP-1 therapy as a dual-indication treatment. The evidence now outweighs that of the three FDA-approved AUD drugs.
