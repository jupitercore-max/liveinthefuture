850 Tomahawks in 38 Days, 57 Budgeted Per Year: Inside the Pentagon's Munitions Arithmetic

Operation Epic Fury consumed half the Pentagon's air defense interceptors and roughly 30% of its cruise missiles. A $50,000 Iranian drone forces $4.4 million in American interceptors for each kill. Current production rates can't close the gap until 2029–2031, overlapping precisely with China's military modernization window. A system-by-system calculation reveals when deterrence actually recovers.

By Kai Nakamura · Defense · July 18, 2026 · ☕ 10 min read

Eight hundred and fifty. That is the number of Tomahawk cruise missiles the United States fired during Operation Epic Fury, the 38-day bombing campaign against Iran that ended with a fragile ceasefire in April. Each missile costs roughly $2 million, and Congress budgeted the purchase of 57 replacements for all of fiscal year 2026, which means the replacement timeline stretches well past most congressional careers.

But Tomahawks are only one line in a ledger that has gone suddenly, dangerously red. A Center for Strategic and International Studies analysis, confirmed as accurate by three people familiar with internal Defense Department stockpile estimates according to CNN, documents the depletion across seven key weapons systems. Half the THAAD interceptor inventory and roughly half the Patriot air defense missiles. Twenty percent of JASSM standoff munitions and 45 percent of Precision Strike Missiles. And 20 to 24 percent of the Navy's SM-3 and SM-6 ship-defense interceptors, the exact weapons a Pacific fleet would need to survive a Chinese anti-ship missile barrage.

Pentagon officials insist readiness remains intact, but Mark Cancian, a retired Marine colonel and CSIS defense analyst, is less sanguine: "If the war continues at the rate it's been going, it would reduce stockpiles enough that there would be a new, higher level of risk with the Indo-Pacific."

Seven Systems, One Calendar

Nobody has assembled the complete deterrence recovery timeline across all seven depleted systems simultaneously, so here it is, calculated from CSIS depletion estimates, known production rates, and manufacturer delivery schedules.

Weapon System Pre-War Inventory (est.) Depleted FY26 Procurement Estimated Recovery
Tomahawk (TLAM) ~3,100 ~27–30% 57 units 2030–2031
THAAD interceptors ~580 ~50% Classified (limited) Mid-to-late 2029
Patriot (PAC-3) Classified ~50% Limited ~2029
JASSM ~5,000 ~20% Recently increased ~2027
PrSM Newer system ~45% Expanded schedule ~2027
SM-3 Classified ~20–24% Limited ~2028
SM-6 Classified ~20–24% Production bottleneck ~2028

Sources: CSIS munitions depletion analysis (May 2026), Medill/Northwestern reporting on classified stockpile data, Congressional Research Service interceptor cost reports, Raytheon earnings call production disclosures.

Follow the rightmost column down and the recovery order becomes clear. JASSM and PrSM recover first, sometime in 2027, thanks to recently expanded production lines, and SM-3 and SM-6 follow roughly a year later. Patriot and THAAD, the backbone of theater missile defense, drag into 2029. Tomahawks trail everything, not reaching pre-war levels until 2030 or 2031.

Full deterrence recovery, defined as all seven systems simultaneously restored to pre-war levels, does not occur until the last system catches up. That system is the Tomahawk, which means the aggregate window of vulnerability stretches from roughly April 2026 to sometime in late 2030 or early 2031. Call it four and a half years during which the Pacific deterrent operates below design specifications.

Why Production Can't Sprint

Money is not the problem. Jules Hurst III, the Defense Department's acting comptroller, told lawmakers the Iran conflict has cost roughly $29 billion so far, with additional expenditures expected. Congress is debating a $200 billion supplemental appropriation, so the funds exist and the political will to appropriate them is bipartisan.

What does not exist, despite all that congressional willingness to appropriate, is factory capacity. Raytheon, the sole manufacturer of Tomahawks, has averaged 86 units per year over the past decade. At that rate, replacing 1,000 missiles requires 11.6 years. Even at maximum surge production, which demands new tooling, additional skilled workers, and expanded testing infrastructure, CSIS estimates the timeline compresses only to 2030 or 2031. Lockheed Martin's THAAD interceptor line faces similar constraints; deliveries of replacements are projected through mid-to-late 2029. SM-6 production has been publicly acknowledged as bottlenecked by Raytheon itself during earnings calls, with the company citing constraints in seeker components, solid-rocket motors, and qualified-workforce availability that no amount of budget authority can resolve overnight.

"Today's challenge isn't money; it's time," the CSIS report concludes. Manufacturing lead times for precision-guided munitions are measured in years, not quarters. A PAC-3 Patriot interceptor contains over 10,000 components sourced from dozens of suppliers, many of them sole-source. You cannot will that supply chain into doubling output by next fiscal year, no matter how large the check.

How 38 Days Ate the Inventory

Operation Epic Fury struck more than 12,000 targets in its 38-day run. In the first 100 hours alone, U.S. forces launched roughly 170 Tomahawks, a number roughly three times what the Pentagon had requested from Raytheon for the entire year, according to CSIS analysis cited by Northeastern University researchers.

Iran's retaliation was swift and devastating. Coordinated Shahed-136 drone waves struck Al Udeid Air Base in Qatar, the Navy's Fifth Fleet headquarters in Bahrain, and U.S. installations in Kuwait and Jordan, reportedly damaging or destroying more than 40 American aircraft out of roughly 350 deployed to the theater, an approximately 11% loss rate in a single campaign. Base damage alone may exceed $5 billion, according to multiple defense analysts, with some unofficial estimates placing total war costs near $100 billion.

Each Patriot interceptor fired to stop an incoming drone or missile costs roughly $4.4 million according to Army budget documents cited by the Wall Street Journal, and each SM-6 used for naval air defense runs $4.7 million according to Congressional Research Service data, figures that become staggering when you learn that the military has burned through between 1,500 and 2,000 Patriot, THAAD, and Standard Missile interceptors since the war began, per a separate Wall Street Journal report. Iran's Shahed-136 drones, the weapon that triggered many of those launches, cost between $10,000 and $50,000 to produce domestically, with some Iranian sources placing the figure as low as $4,000 per unit.

A Cost Inversion That Consumed Half the Arsenal

Consider the exchange ratio at the level of a single engagement: a Shahed-136 costs between $4,000 and $50,000 to produce depending on variant and source, while a PAC-3 MSE interceptor costs $3.7 to $5.3 million per unit across recent Army contracts. At the midpoints, that yields a cost ratio between roughly 63-to-1 and 1,100-to-1 favoring the attacker. Even the conservative end of that range means Iran spent less than two cents for every dollar America burned on defense. Even using the more conservative $4.7 million SM-6 against a $50,000 Shahed, the ratio is still 94 to 1. Against Iran's cheapest drones, which some Iranian sources place as low as $4,000 per unit, the disparity climbs above 1,000 to 1.

Scaled over the full campaign, the economics get worse. Iran's HESA facilities in Isfahan can produce 150 to 250 Shaheds per month across all variants, according to Quwa defense analysis, and Russia's Yelabuga plant was designed for 500 Geran-2 airframes monthly. Combined theoretical capacity: 400 to 750 units per month. At $50,000 each, that is $20 million to $37.5 million in monthly offensive output. Defending against that monthly output at a one-to-one intercept ratio using PAC-3 MSE interceptors at $4.4 million each would cost $660 million to $1.1 billion per month. In practice the intercept ratio is better than one-to-one since layered defenses and cheaper systems handle some threats, but the structural economics remain brutal.

This math consumed half the Patriot and THAAD inventories, not because the weapons failed but because each successful intercept cost hundreds of times more than the threat it neutralized, and every inventory has a floor.

What This Means for the Pacific

Every weapon expended against Iran is a weapon unavailable for the Western Pacific, and the overlap between the two theaters' requirements is almost total. Tomahawks, Patriots, THAAD, SM-3, SM-6: these are the same platforms war planners have designated as essential for a Taiwan contingency. Not similar platforms. The same ones. CSIS noted that pre-war inventories were already considered insufficient for a prolonged high-intensity conflict against a peer competitor, which means the Iran campaign did not create the Pacific munitions gap so much as deepen and accelerate one that already existed before the first Tomahawk left its tube.

An Arleigh Burke destroyer carries 90 to 96 vertical launch cells, costs $2.2 billion, and cannot reload at sea under current doctrine, meaning that once its magazine is empty it must withdraw to port. Multiple defense analysts have estimated that a high-tempo fight with China could empty a surface combatant's magazines in days rather than weeks, and at-sea reloading remains experimental rather than routine.

Overlay the timelines. China's military modernization roadmap, as documented by the Department of Defense's annual China Military Power Report, targets critical capabilities between 2027 and 2035. Full U.S. munitions recovery from the Iran war extends to 2030 or 2031. For roughly four years, those windows overlap. Beijing's planners can read CSIS reports as easily as anyone, and what those reports describe is a temporary reduction in the cost America would impose on an adversary contemplating action in the Taiwan Strait.

The Strongest Case Against Panic

Deterrence is not a spreadsheet. Military commanders, including Admiral Samuel Paparo of U.S. Indo-Pacific Command, have stated publicly that readiness in the Pacific has not been degraded. Their argument deserves honest engagement.

Start with what the campaign proved about American resolve. China watched the U.S. strike 12,000 targets in 38 days. That kind of operational tempo may strengthen deterrence even as it depletes inventory, because it eliminates any doubt about willingness to use force at scale. A country that fires 850 Tomahawks in a month is not a country that hesitates.

Then consider the recovery asymmetry: the offensive systems most relevant to a Pacific fight, JASSM and PrSM, recover within about a year, faster than any other depleted category. If a Taiwan contingency prioritizes offensive strikes over defensive interception, the weapons that matter most regenerate first, and they are already doing so under expanded production schedules.

And beyond inventory counts entirely, deterrence draws on submarine-launched weapons, nuclear posture, alliance networks spanning Japan, Australia, South Korea, and the Philippines, and the sheer economic interdependence between Washington and Beijing, all factors that a munitions table cannot capture. Reducing the equation to a single chart of depleted interceptors overstates the risk, and policymakers on both sides know it.

All of this is fair. But none of it changes the arithmetic showing that the defensive layer of the deterrent, the interceptors that protect carriers and forward bases from Chinese missile salvos, will remain below pre-war capacity for years.

Where This Lands Next

If you work in defense procurement or congressional appropriations, the most actionable number in this article is 57. Congress authorized 57 Tomahawks for FY2026 while a single campaign consumed more than 850. Bringing the annual procurement rate to at least 200 would compress the Tomahawk restoration timeline from 2031 to roughly 2028, shaving three years off the vulnerability window. That adjustment costs approximately $290 million per year in additional acquisition, roughly 0.03% of the base defense budget, which is the difference between recovering before or after China's modernization window peaks.

If you follow the defense industrial base, the production bottleneck is the defining constraint of the next five years. RTX (Raytheon), Lockheed Martin, and Northrop Grumman face a multi-year production surge that is funded and mandated but constrained by facilities, workforce, and sole-source components. Expansion of guided-munitions production capacity is the binding constraint on U.S. military readiness for the next half-decade, and the companies, labor pools, and supply chains that solve it will shape whether the recovery calendar above holds or slips further right.

If you follow geopolitics, watch two dates. Mid-2028 is when the defensive interceptor inventory (Patriot, SM-3, SM-6) should approach pre-war levels, closing the worst of the gap. Late 2030 is when Tomahawks and THAAD finish restoring, at which point the deterrent returns to where it was before the first cruise missile hit Isfahan. Any significant Chinese military provocation between now and mid-2028 plays out against a very different set of American capabilities than one after 2030, and that distinction should inform every allocation decision from Pacific Command headquarters in Hawaii to the Senate Armed Services Committee on Capitol Hill.

What We Don't Know

This analysis rests on CSIS estimates confirmed by anonymous DoD sources, not on declassified inventory data. Exact pre-war stockpile numbers for most of these systems remain classified, so the percentage-depletion figures have error margins wide enough to shift each recovery estimate by six months or more in either direction. If THAAD depletion is 40% rather than 50%, the restoration timeline shortens; if it is 60%, it extends.

Surge production rates are also classified. CSIS restoration timelines assume production ramp-ups that have been announced but not yet verified as achievable. Manufacturing bottlenecks are real and have surprised defense planners before; the 155mm artillery shell shortage during the Ukraine support effort demonstrated how quickly demand can outrun capacity even for relatively simple munitions.

Most importantly, this analysis treats China's own inventory as static. It is not. China is building missiles and ships at a rate that has alarmed Pentagon officials in every recent posture hearing, with the PLAN launching more naval tonnage in the past three years than most allied navies field in total, and a fuller deterrence assessment would need to calculate the relative rate of inventory change on both sides, not just the absolute U.S. stockpile.

Finally, new technologies could reshape the calculus. Directed-energy weapons like the HELIOS laser system, now deployed on the USS Preble, offer a path to intercepting drones and missiles at pennies per shot rather than millions. If these systems mature on schedule, they could partially offset the interceptor deficit, but defense laser programs have a long history of slipping timelines and downgraded performance specifications.

The Bottom Line

Operation Epic Fury was a demonstration of American military power that simultaneously revealed its structural fragility. Twelve thousand targets struck across seven depleted weapons systems. And a replacement pipeline so narrow that a single 38-day campaign created a measurable, multi-year deterrence deficit in the region that matters most.

CSIS called it a "window of vulnerability." The math in this article puts a frame around that window: it opened in April 2026 and does not close until late 2030 or early 2031, assuming no further large-scale conflict draws down stocks in the interim. Whether that window matters depends on whether anyone else in the world is reading the same calendar. Given that the CSIS report is public, the CNN confirmation is public, and the production-rate data comes from manufacturers' own earnings calls, the answer is obvious. Everyone is reading it.
