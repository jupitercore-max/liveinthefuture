Tesla Published Its First European FSD Safety Report. The Netherlands Data Is Compelling — and Full of Holes.

Two months, 23.6 million kilometres, and 3.5 times fewer collisions on Dutch public roads. Tesla’s first jurisdiction-specific European safety data landed June 9. The numbers look good, the methodology does not survive scrutiny, and the real story is not whether FSD is safer but that Europe now demands the data while America still does not.

Elena Vasquez · Transport · June 9, 2026

Zero collisions in 16.6 million kilometres of highway driving.

That is the record Tesla claims for its Full Self-Driving Supervised software on Dutch roads between April 10 and June 5, 2026, the first two months since the Dutch vehicle authority RDW granted the first European type approval for the system under UN Regulation 171, the international standard governing Driver Control Assistance Systems. The data, published by Tesla’s European account on X, covers every FSD-equipped vehicle operating in the Netherlands during that window, totalling 23.6 million kilometres driven under software supervision and split roughly 70–30 between highways and surface streets.

Headline numbers are striking enough that both bulls and bears should spend time with the fine print before drawing conclusions. On highways, FSD-supervised vehicles recorded zero collisions across 16.6 million km, against 33 collisions in 158.7 million km of manual driving by the broader Tesla fleet. On non-highway roads, the gap narrowed but persisted: three collisions in 7 million FSD km versus 109 collisions in 152.9 million manual km, a ratio of 1.6 rather than the splashier 3.5 that combines both environments.

Behavioural metrics reinforced the pattern across the board. Automatic emergency braking events dropped by a factor of 14.9 when FSD was engaged, harsh acceleration fell 8.8-fold, harsh braking dropped 7.3-fold, and hard swerves decreased 8-fold. By every indicator Tesla chose to publish, the software produced smoother and less eventful driving than the humans sitting behind the wheel with their hands free to use as they pleased.

Three of the four countries that have now approved FSD in Europe did so by accepting the Dutch certification without conducting independent testing programs. Lithuania recognized it on May 20, Estonia on May 29, and Denmark confirmed today after conducting its own document review but no physical testing. Belgium is running a 5,000 km pilot in Flanders, Greece has announced legislation to authorize the system, and the EU-wide vote originally scheduled for June 30 has been pulled from the agenda after Nordic objections pushed it to autumn 2026 or early 2027.

Which makes this the right moment to ask what the data actually shows, what it deliberately omits, and why the mere fact that it exists at all may matter more than the numbers it contains.

What the Comparison Actually Compares

Tesla’s 3.5-times-safer claim rests on a ratio between two groups of drivers who are fundamentally not comparable in their driving habits, their risk profiles, or the circumstances under which they accumulated their respective mileage.

The FSD group consists of early adopters who paid for the software, opted into a regulated European rollout, and are by definition among the most tech-engaged, safety-conscious Tesla owners in the country. They have been using the system for at most eight weeks, know they are being watched because RDW required Tesla to report annually as a condition of type approval, and have every incentive to demonstrate careful driving because the public data release helps determine whether FSD expands across Europe. The manual comparison pool, on the other hand, includes every Tesla owner in the Netherlands: cautious retirees and 19-year-olds running late, distracted commuters and road-raging highway drivers, fatigued night-shift workers and first-time Tesla owners who still confuse the turn signal for the autopilot stalk.

The environmental split compounds this problem in a way that Tesla’s headline number obscures. FSD drove 70 percent of its kilometres on highways, which are structurally the safest road type in any country and particularly in the Netherlands, where grade-separated cycling infrastructure and ubiquitous roundabouts already make driving safer than the European average. We do not know the highway/non-highway split for the manual comparison pool, and if manual drivers split closer to 50/50, the environments are not matched and the collision rate gap shrinks by a mechanism that has nothing to do with software quality.

Non-highway data tells a more honest story that is still favourable. Three collisions in 7 million km (429 per billion km) versus 109 in 152.9 million km (713 per billion km) yields a 1.6x safety advantage, meaningful but roughly half the multiplier Tesla put in the headline.

The Calculation Nobody Ran

Here is the original analysis that none of the coverage performed, and it illustrates why Tesla’s data is harder to interpret than it first appears.

The Netherlands recorded 675 traffic deaths in 2024, according to Statistics Netherlands (CBS), of which 220 were passenger car occupants. Dutch vehicles collectively drove approximately 140 billion kilometres that year, yielding a national fatality rate of roughly 1.57 car-occupant deaths per billion vehicle-km.

Tesla’s FSD non-highway collision rate of 429 per billion km is 273 times higher than that national fatality baseline, and its manual collision rate of 713 per billion km is 454 times higher. These numbers initially look absurd, and the reason they look absurd reveals the core methodological gap in the entire data release: Tesla is counting every fender-bender, parking lot scrape, and minor impact as a “collision,” while the national baseline counts only deaths within 30 days of a crash.

You cannot divide Tesla collisions by national fatalities and learn anything meaningful about relative safety. The 3.5x headline compares FSD collisions to manual Tesla collisions within the same fleet, and that tells you something narrow and real about driving behaviour. Stretching it to say FSD is “3.5 times safer than human driving” in any general sense requires a denominator Tesla does not provide: collision severity, injury rates, or a matched comparison cohort with equivalent demographic and environmental characteristics.

Five Gaps Large Enough to Drive Through

First, disengagements. Tesla does not report how many times FSD handed control back to the driver in the seconds before a collision, and if the system detected an imminent crash and disengaged half a second before impact, the resulting collision would appear in the manual column rather than the FSD column. Waymo publishes disengagement data as a matter of standard practice; Cruise published it before it shut down; Tesla does not.

Second, the manual comparison is not a matched cohort. It appears to include all Tesla-driven kilometres in the Netherlands regardless of vehicle model, driver demographics, time of day, weather conditions, or driving context, and a meaningful comparison would match FSD drivers against non-FSD drivers with similar characteristics on the same road types during the same period.

Third, no independent verification exists for any of the numbers. RDW required annual reporting but has not validated the collision counts, severity classifications, or km totals. The regulator has also declined to publish its own testing details, citing commercial sensitivity, while regulators in Sweden, Finland, and Norway have raised concerns in email correspondence obtained by Reuters about FSD’s tendency to exceed speed limits, its performance on icy roads, and its inability to fully prevent phone use while engaged.

Fourth, the sample represents two months of early adopters, and selection bias does not require conspiracy because it is structural. The first people to activate FSD in a new market are enthusiasts who pay close attention to the technology, read the documentation, and monitor the system with the kind of focused vigilance that degrades naturally over time as novelty fades and confidence builds. Whether the safety profile holds as FSD rolls out to less engaged owners who treat Level 2 like Level 4 is an empirical question the current data cannot answer.

Fifth, and most consequentially for Dutch road safety specifically, there is no pedestrian, cyclist, or vulnerable road user breakdown anywhere in the report. In the Netherlands, cyclists accounted for more traffic deaths in 2024 (246) than car occupants (220), and the most important safety question for a country with 22.9 million bicycles and 17.9 million people is not whether FSD reduces car-on-car collisions but whether it reduces the conflicts that kill cyclists. Tesla’s report is silent on this entirely.

The Regulatory Asymmetry That Matters More Than Any Collision Rate

Neither credulous nor dismissive coverage has told the story about the framework that produced the data, not the data itself.

Europe now requires this reporting. RDW approved FSD under UN Regulation 171, which mandates annual safety data submission as a condition of continued type approval, and if the data deteriorates, RDW can revoke the approval outright. The regulatory framework has teeth in a way that the American system structurally does not.

The United States operates under a self-certification regime where manufacturers declare that their vehicles meet federal safety standards and NHTSA investigates only after problems emerge through complaints, media reports, or crash data. The agency’s Standing General Order requires crash reporting for vehicles using advanced driver-assistance systems, but the obligation is reactive rather than preventive: there is no pre-market approval, no annual data submission mandate, and no type-approval framework that can be revoked if safety degrades below a threshold the regulator defines in advance.

The consequences of this asymmetry are playing out in real time. NHTSA is currently running three concurrent FSD investigations: one into red-light running and traffic violations covering 58 incidents, one into visibility failures in fog and glare that has been escalated to an Engineering Analysis covering 3.2 million vehicles (the procedural step that typically precedes a recall), and a separate inquiry into whether Tesla is accurately reporting crashes at all. FSD has been available in the United States since 2020 and has accumulated billions of miles without ever being required to produce the kind of structured, jurisdiction-specific safety report that RDW extracted in two months.

The Dutch data is imperfect, as the five gaps above demonstrate, but it is also the most granular, regulatory-mandated FSD safety dataset that exists anywhere in the world. The American picture, by contrast, is scattered across NHTSA complaint files, Standing General Order submissions, and litigation discovery documents that no single analyst can assemble into a comparable view of fleet-wide performance.

The Strongest Case for Tesla’s Data

The counterargument deserves its full weight.

Selection bias and methodological gaps are real, but they do not erase a signal this large. Zero highway collisions across 16.6 million km is not a statistical artefact produced by careful drivers on easy roads, because highway driving is precisely where the combination of high speeds, narrow margins, and sustained attention requirements makes human error most consequential, and FSD eliminated collisions entirely across the full reporting window regardless of traffic density, weather, or time of day.

The behavioural data is harder to dismiss than the collision data because it measures continuous performance rather than rare events that may be influenced by small-sample noise. A 14.9-fold reduction in AEB events means the software is maintaining safer following distances, reacting earlier to decelerating traffic ahead, and avoiding the cascading chain of near-misses that typically precedes a collision in dense traffic. You can argue about whether the comparison pool is properly matched, but you cannot plausibly argue that a 14.9-fold reduction in emergency braking events is a measurement artefact or a coincidence of driver selection.

And the selection bias argument cuts both ways in a way critics have not acknowledged. FSD in the Netherlands runs on one of the most challenging road networks in Western Europe for a driver-assistance system: narrow urban streets shared with millions of cyclists, aggressive e-bike riders weaving through traffic at 25 km/h, complex roundabout entries where priority rules change at every approach, and tram tracks embedded in roadways that create false lane markings for camera-based systems. If you wanted to pick an easy country where FSD could pad its statistics against human drivers, the Netherlands would sit near the bottom of any reasonable list. That the system produced zero highway collisions and materially fewer surface-street collisions in this specific environment, even among self-selected enthusiast users, is not a trivial finding that methodology concerns alone can explain away.

Limitations

This analysis relies entirely on Tesla’s self-reported data because RDW has not independently verified the collision counts, severity classifications, or kilometre totals, and no third-party audit of the underlying fleet telemetry exists. The national baseline comparison uses 2024 CBS fatality data, which measures deaths rather than all collisions; we highlighted this mismatch rather than eliding it, but the mismatch constrains the analytical value of any cross-dataset comparison. We do not know the demographic composition of either the FSD driver pool or the manual comparison pool, and the European FSD software (v14.2.2.5) differs substantially from the US version according to Tesla’s own regulatory filings, which means US safety data and ongoing NHTSA investigations may not apply directly to the European deployment. The two-month reporting window is too short to capture seasonal variation, gradual software degradation, or the behavioural shift that occurs as novelty fades and drivers grow less attentive to a system they have learned to trust.

The Bottom Line

Tesla’s Dutch data shows something real: FSD Supervised, used by early adopters on well-maintained roads under regulatory observation, produces fewer collisions and smoother driving than the fleet average. Worth knowing, even if less dramatic than the 3.5x headline.

The bigger finding is structural rather than statistical. Europe built a regulatory framework that extracts safety data as a condition of market access, and in two months it produced more structured, jurisdiction-specific FSD performance information than the United States has generated in six years of deployment. That is the gap that should worry American regulators and encourage European ones.

If you are a European regulator weighing whether to recognize the Dutch approval: the data supports cautious expansion, not a blank cheque. Demand the same annual reporting RDW established, and add what RDW left out: severity breakdowns, disengagement data, and incident categories specifically tracking pedestrians, cyclists, and other vulnerable road users. The Netherlands created the template; your job is to make it better before you stamp it.

If you are a US policymaker or regulator: NHTSA is running three simultaneous FSD investigations while working with less structured data than a single Dutch authority extracted in eight weeks. The Standing General Order is a reporting mechanism, not a safety framework, and a pre-market conditional approval process modelled on UN R-171 would give NHTSA the same leverage that RDW now exercises without requiring new legislation.

If you own a Tesla in the Netherlands: the data suggests FSD Supervised is making your car less likely to crash, and you should use it with the understanding that you remain legally responsible for every metre driven, that the system was classified as Level 2 for a reason, and that two months of favourable data is the beginning of a safety record rather than the end of one.
