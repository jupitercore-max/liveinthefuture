Every Additional Hour of Lithium Storage Costs $153. Sage's Underground Battery Costs Nearly Zero. They Cross at 16 Hours.

Grid-scale lithium-ion costs scale linearly with storage duration. Sage Geosystems' EarthStore, an underground pressure battery built with fracking rigs, has a flat cost curve. My analysis of decomposed 2026 capex data finds the crossover at 16 hours, exactly where the grid needs it most.

By Zara Osman · Energy & Finance · July 11, 2026

Grid-scale energy storage has a duration problem. Almost every utility-scale battery system deployed in the United States stores four hours of electricity. Build a longer system and the cost doesn't plateau; it climbs. Each additional hour of lithium-ion storage adds roughly $153 per kilowatt-hour in energy capex, according to Modo Energy's 2026 decomposed cost analysis of utility-scale projects. An eight-hour system costs 60% more than a four-hour system, rising from $1,011/kW to $1,623/kW simply because the energy capex component scales with every hour of discharge capacity added to the design. This linear cost scaling, driven by the simple fact that each additional hour of discharge requires proportionally more manufactured battery cells, inverter capacity, and thermal management infrastructure, is the reason lithium-ion dominates short-duration storage but barely exists beyond eight hours.

Sage Geosystems, a Houston-based startup backed by $97 million in Series B funding from Ormat Technologies and Carbon Direct Capital, claims to have built a storage technology with a fundamentally different cost curve. Instead of stacking battery cells in surface containers, EarthStore pumps water into underground rock fractures during off-peak hours and releases it through turbines when demand spikes. Drilling one well costs roughly the same whether it stores six hours or eighteen. Add duration and you're making the fracture larger, not adding hardware.

I built a crossover model using Modo Energy's decomposed capex figures for six competing storage technologies and Sage's first commercial project cost. At pre-scale pricing, EarthStore doesn't undercut lithium-ion on raw capex until nearly 28 hours of storage, a duration so long that virtually nobody is building for it in today's grid infrastructure plans. But at Sage's projected scale-up costs, the crossover drops to 16 hours. And 16 hours is precisely where the grid's storage gap begins.

How an Earthen Battery Works

EarthStore is not enhanced geothermal. That distinction matters because Fervo Energy, the highest-profile next-generation geothermal company, uses horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing to circulate water through hot rock and generate electricity continuously, making it a power plant. Sage's EarthStore is a battery that doesn't generate baseload power from heat but stores energy by moving water underground.

Drill a well 8,000 to 12,000 feet deep. Create a fracture in a rock formation using the same equipment that fracking crews have operated for decades. When surplus electricity is available, pump water down the well and into the fracture, where the formation's natural compressive stress and the weight of the overburden keep the fluid trapped under enormous pressure that can be released on demand. When electricity is needed, open the valves. Stored pressure drives the water back up through Pelton turbines to generate power.

In a five-week commercial pilot at an abandoned gas well in Starr County, Texas, in 2023, Sage demonstrated 200 kW output for over 18 hours of continuous discharge and 1 MW for 30-minute load-following bursts. Subsurface efficiency ranged from 88% to 94%. Round-trip efficiency, which includes the full chain of pumping losses, subsurface pressure retention, and turbine conversion back to electricity, came in at 70% to 75% across the pilot's five-week operating window. Water losses started below 2% and declined to 1% over the test period. A single well produced 2 to 3 MW net output, which means that a 150 MW project like Meta's would require on the order of 50 to 75 wells drilled in sequence or in parallel across a multi-pad configuration.

That depth range of 8,000 to 12,000 feet covers 90% of the continental United States for storage applications, per Sage. Power generation, which requires rock temperatures above 150°C, is geographically limited to about 35% of the country. But the storage proposition doesn't need heat; it needs pressure-compatible rock, and most of America has it.

Six Technologies, One Duration Variable

Comparing storage technologies on headline capex is misleading. A battery's total cost has two components: a fixed power cost (the inverters, turbines, and grid connection, denominated in dollars per kilowatt) and a variable energy cost (the storage medium itself, in dollars per kilowatt-hour). For lithium-ion, both components are substantial. For technologies like compressed air or pumped hydro, the power component is high but the energy component is cheap. For Sage's EarthStore, the energy component approaches zero because the storage medium is fractured rock.

Modo Energy published decomposed capex data in February 2026 for six grid-scale storage technologies. I added EarthStore using Sage's published first commercial project cost of $14 million for 3 MW at Christine, Texas:

TechnologyPower Capex ($/kW)Energy Capex ($/kWh)RTELifetime
Lithium-ion LFP$399$15385%15 yr
Vanadium Flow (VRFB)$870$17075%25 yr

Read each row as a cost equation: Total capex per kW = Power capex + (Energy capex × Duration in hours). For lithium-ion, a four-hour system costs $399 + ($153 × 4) = $1,011/kW. Double the duration and you get $1,623/kW, and at eighteen hours the total reaches $3,153/kW. Every hour is $153 more.

Now consider EarthStore, whose $4,667/kW first-project cost is dominated by the well. Make the fracture hold six hours of water or eighteen; the drilling cost barely changes. I model EarthStore's marginal energy capex as approximately $0/kWh because the storage medium is rock and water, not manufactured cells or electrolyte.

Finding the Crossover

Set lithium-ion's cost equation equal to EarthStore's flat cost and solve for duration:

Duration Crossover: Li-ion vs. EarthStore (pre-scale)
$399 + $153 × D = $4,667
$153 × D = $4,268
D = 27.9 hours
Pre-scale crossover: ~28 hours

At today's first-of-kind pricing, EarthStore doesn't beat lithium-ion until nearly 28 hours of storage. Almost nobody builds for that duration. It explains why the technology has attracted interest but not yet disruption.

But pre-scale costs are not the number that matters for a technology backed by $97 million in Series B funding and a 150 MW purchase agreement with Meta. Sage CEO Cindy Taff told Reuters in June 2026 that pre-scale geothermal plants under 150 MW currently produce electricity at $100 to $120 per MWh, but she predicted that costs could fall to between $65 and $75 per MWh as her team standardizes well pad designs, moves to simultaneous multi-pad drilling configurations, and captures the same supply chain efficiencies that drove shale gas costs down by 50% over a decade. That implies a 35% to 38% cost reduction from scale effects and supply chain standardization, a range that aligns with historical learning curves in industries transitioning from first-of-kind demonstration projects to repeated commercial deployment.

Applying a 35% capex reduction to the $4,667/kW first-project figure gives an at-scale estimate of roughly $3,034/kW. I round down to $2,800/kW to incorporate what Taff described as supply chain efficiencies from leveraging existing oil and gas equipment, which Sage has already demonstrated by using standard casing, perforation, and fracking rigs.

Duration Crossover: Li-ion vs. EarthStore (at-scale estimate)
$399 + $153 × D = $2,800
$153 × D = $2,401
D = 15.7 hours
At-scale crossover: ~16 hours

Sixteen hours. Sixteen hours. That number is the storage duration at which the lines cross. Below 16 hours, lithium-ion is cheaper per kilowatt of installed capacity. Above 16 hours, EarthStore wins on capex, and the gap widens with every additional hour because Sage's curve is flat and lithium's keeps climbing.

Run the same crossover against the other technologies:

EarthStore At-Scale ($2,800/kW) Crossover Points
vs. Li-ion: 16 hours
vs. VRFB: 11 hours ($870 + $170D = $2,800)
vs. Pumped Hydro: 11 hours ($2,250 + $50D = $2,800)
vs. CAES: 6 hours ($2,500 + $50D = $2,800)
vs. Iron-Air: never at pre-scale; Iron-Air's $5/kWh energy cost is too flat to cross
EarthStore beats every battery chemistry above 16 hours. It beats flow batteries and pumped hydro above 11 hours.

Iron-air is the one technology that EarthStore may never undercut on pure capex. Form Energy's projected energy capex of $5/kWh makes its cost curve nearly as flat as EarthStore's, and its power capex of $2,300/kW starts lower. At 100 hours of storage, iron-air reaches $2,800/kW, matching EarthStore's at-scale target. But iron-air has a crippling weakness: 40% round-trip efficiency, compared with EarthStore's 75%, meaning every kilowatt-hour stored in an iron-air battery wastes 60% of its input energy, and accounting for that penalty reshapes the entire comparison.

Round-Trip Efficiency Reshapes the Map

Capex per installed kilowatt tells you what a system costs to build. But the economically relevant number is capex per deliverable kilowatt-hour: how much you pay for each unit of energy the system can actually return to the grid, after efficiency losses eat their share.

Effective Cost per Deliverable kWh = Capex ÷ (Duration × RTE)
Li-ion at 8h: $1,623 / (8 × 0.85) = $239/kWh
Li-ion at 18h: $3,153 / (18 × 0.85) = $206/kWh
EarthStore at 8h (at-scale): $2,800 / (8 × 0.75) = $467/kWh
EarthStore at 18h (at-scale): $2,800 / (18 × 0.75) = $207/kWh (parity)
EarthStore at 24h (at-scale): $2,800 / (24 × 0.75) = $156/kWh
Li-ion at 24h: $4,071 / (24 × 0.85) = $200/kWh
Iron-Air at 100h: $2,800 / (100 × 0.40) = $70/kWh
RTE-adjusted parity between EarthStore and Li-ion: ~18 hours. EarthStore pulls ahead at 24 hours ($156 vs. $200). Iron-air wins on multi-day storage despite brutal efficiency.

When you correct for efficiency, EarthStore reaches parity with lithium-ion at about 18 hours. Below that, lithium's 85% RTE gives it a meaningful advantage. Above it, EarthStore's flat capital cost dominates even after the efficiency penalty. At 24 hours, EarthStore delivers each kilowatt-hour for $156 versus lithium-ion's $200. By 36 hours, the gap is $104 versus $232.

Iron-air deserves a separate note because Form Energy's chemistry stores energy cheaply at extreme durations, but its 40% round-trip efficiency means you throw away more energy than you keep. For multi-day grid resilience storage, where the facility cycles infrequently and the energy cost matters less than simply having it available, iron-air may win. For daily cycling at 12-to-24-hour durations, EarthStore's 75% RTE delivers a better return on every kilowatt-hour pumped underground.

The Cycling Constraint Nobody Models

Most LCOS comparisons assume one full cycle per day. At four hours, that's easily achievable: four hours of discharge plus roughly 4.7 hours of charging at 85% efficiency leaves more than 15 hours of idle time. But at 18 hours of lithium-ion discharge, recharging takes roughly 21 hours at the same efficiency. Total cycle time: 39 hours. You cannot cycle a lithium-ion system daily above approximately 10 hours of storage duration without running past the 24-hour constraint of a calendar day.

EarthStore has an inherent advantage here: because the stored water sits in rock at temperatures of 200°F or higher, the formation's heat continually expands the downhole fluid, boosting subsurface efficiency above what a pure pressure-in-pressure-out system would deliver. Sage measured subsurface efficiencies of 88% to 94% during its pilot, meaning the earth is adding free thermal energy to the stored hydraulic energy. Charging can be faster than a pure energy-balance calculation would suggest.

For levelized cost of storage, which divides lifetime costs by total energy throughput, the cycling constraint matters. An 18-hour lithium system cycling every 39 hours achieves about 225 full cycles per year. An 18-hour EarthStore cycling daily achieves 365. Over a 15-year lithium lifespan, that's 3,375 cycles versus potentially 10,950 for EarthStore's 30-plus-year well life. More cycles over a longer lifetime spreads the fixed capital cost across more delivered energy, and the LCOS gap opens further than the snapshot capex analysis suggests.

What $97 Million Buys

Sage closed its Series B in January 2026 with Ormat Technologies, the largest publicly traded pure-play geothermal company, co-leading the round alongside Carbon Direct Capital. Ormat didn't just invest: it signed a strategic commercial agreement in August 2025 granting it rights to develop, build, own, and operate power plants and energy storage projects using Sage's technology after a successful pilot at one of Ormat's existing facilities.

Meta signed a 150 MW power purchase agreement in August 2024, announced at a Department of Energy summit. First phase is expected operational by 2027, at a location east of the Rocky Mountains. ABB signed a memorandum of understanding in February 2025 to supply automation and electrical infrastructure for the Meta project. Sage has additional agreements with California Resources Corporation, ongoing projects in Lithuania and Romania, and expressed interest from the U.S. Department of Defense.

Scaling requires wells, and Sage estimates that reaching a multi-gigawatt level of output will need roughly 500 new wells per year. For context, U.S. oil and gas companies brought 15,000 new wells online in 2024, according to EIA data. Sage is asking for 3.3% of the existing drilling industry's annual capacity. That's a rounding error. Rigs are available, crews are trained, and supply chains exist. "We don't need new equipment or a new skillset," Taff said. "We will be able to leverage the drilling rigs and the oil field services that are already available for the oil and gas industry."

Where This Analysis Breaks

Several assumptions in my crossover model deserve scrutiny. First, Sage's cost claims are self-reported and unaudited by a third party. Its LCOS estimate of 2 to 4 cents per kilowatt-hour comes from the CEO, not from an independent engineering assessment. Sage did not provide hard LCOS figures in its public filings; the 2 to 4 cent range was quoted in press coverage of the 2023 pilot results. Until an independent analysis validates these claims against metered output data from the Christine, Texas commercial facility, treat them as a lower bound.

Second, my at-scale estimate of $2,800/kW applies a 35% reduction to a single data point. One $14 million project is not a cost curve. Mature industries like deepwater drilling and conventional geothermal show learning rates of 3% to 7% per doubling, far slower than solar's 20%. Reaching $2,800/kW may require hundreds of wells drilled, not dozens. Taff herself framed the timeline honestly: "We're kind of where wind and solar were 15 years ago."

Third, EarthStore's round-trip efficiency of 70-75% is a real penalty for arbitrage applications. If you're buying cheap solar at $43/MWh and reselling it during evening peaks, losing 25-30% of the input energy means your effective purchase price is $57-$61/MWh before any storage cost. Lithium-ion loses only 15%. For pure price arbitrage at short durations, lithium's efficiency advantage outweighs EarthStore's capital cost advantage.

Fourth, geological risk is underexplored. Five weeks of field testing in a single well does not demonstrate fracture stability over 30 years. Rock stress evolution, water chemistry changes, and induced seismicity are open questions. Conventional geothermal wells have operated for decades, but EarthStore's cyclic pressurization and depressurization regime subjects rock to different fatigue patterns than a steady-state geothermal plant.

The Strongest Case Against

Here is the sharpest critique: EarthStore is a solution looking for a market that barely exists. Virtually nobody is building 16-hour or 18-hour storage systems because the grid doesn't currently pay for them. Electricity markets in most regions compensate storage for frequency regulation, peak shaving, and four-to-eight-hour energy shifting. Revenue models for 16-hour discharge barely exist outside of capacity markets that pay for availability rather than throughput. Sage could be right about the physics and the cost curve and still fail because the market structure hasn't caught up.

This is a legitimate concern, but it mistakes current market design for permanent market structure. ERCOT, where Sage is building its first project, saw real-time prices spike above $5,000/MWh during Winter Storm Uri in 2021 over periods lasting 12 to 20 hours. California's duck curve is deepening, with net demand swings of 25 GW or more between midday surplus and evening peak. CAISO has begun studying "super-peak" periods extending beyond eight hours as solar penetration passes 40%. Revenue models will follow grid conditions. The question is whether they arrive before Sage runs out of runway, and $97 million buys considerable time.

The Bottom Line

Lithium-ion storage costs $153 for every additional hour of discharge. Sage Geosystems' underground pressure battery has a marginal energy cost approaching zero. Using 2026 decomposed capex data from Modo Energy, the capital cost crossover happens at 16 hours at projected scale. Correcting for round-trip efficiency pushes the parity point to about 18 hours. Above that, EarthStore delivers cheaper stored energy with every hour added, while lithium costs keep climbing.

What to watch: Sage's Christine, Texas facility should be operational in the second half of 2026. Audited performance data from that project will be the first independent test of the $4,667/kW price point and the 70-75% round-trip efficiency. If both hold, the at-scale crossover at 16 hours becomes a planning number, not a projection. If Meta's 150 MW project reaches commercial operation by 2027 as planned, Sage will have drilled enough wells to begin bending its own cost curve. For grid planners and energy investors: the four-hour battery solved the easy storage problem. Sixteen hours and beyond is where lithium stops scaling and the earth starts.
