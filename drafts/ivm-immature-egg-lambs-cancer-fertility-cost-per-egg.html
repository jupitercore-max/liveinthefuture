Five Lambs Born from Eggs That Never Matured in a Body. The Cost-Per-Usable-Egg Math Changes Everything.

A University of Leeds team cultured fully immature follicles from sheep ovaries into mature eggs in a dish, fertilized them, and got five healthy lambs, the first time this has worked in any large animal. The cost-per-usable-egg drops from $1,500 to under $120, and it could close the cruelest gap in cancer treatment.

By Anya Volkov · Biotech & Genomics

July 18, 2026

300,000 eggs. That is approximately how many immature follicles sit in a woman's ovaries at puberty. Standard IVF ignores nearly all of them, spending $3,000–$7,000 in hormonal drugs to coax roughly ten into maturity per cycle. The other 299,990 wait in biological cold storage until they die. On July 7, Helen Picton of the University of Leeds stood at the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology conference in London and showed what happens when you stop ignoring them.

Five lambs. Born from eggs that had never matured inside a living animal. Picton's team collected dozens of immature primordial follicles from sheep ovaries, bathed them in FSH and growth factors, watched 60% mature into viable eggs, fertilized them, and transferred embryos into 18 recipients. The first arrived in early 2024, four more followed, and one female went on to conceive and deliver two offspring of her own, proving the technique produces reproductively normal animals. It had never been done in a large animal before. Not even close.

"It's really a major breakthrough," Stine Kristensen of Copenhagen University Hospital told New Scientist. The last time anyone achieved IVM-to-live-birth from fully immature eggs was in mice in 1996. Thirty years to cross one species barrier.

The Cost-Per-Egg Inversion

Standard IVF is expensive because it treats mature eggs as scarce. The stimulation-monitoring-retrieval sequence runs $8,000–$12,000 and yields six to seven usable mature eggs, putting the cost per mature egg at roughly $1,100–$2,000 before any laboratory work begins, and every downstream expense compounds from there (ICSI at $2,000, genetic testing at $5,000, storage at $600 per year) because the egg is the bottleneck and everything else in the system scales around its scarcity.

Now extrapolate to a hypothetical human protocol. A laparoscopic ovarian biopsy runs $2,000–$4,000, extended follicle culture in specialized media adds $2,000–$5,000, and the total comes to $4,000–$9,000. A small ovarian cortex strip contains 50 to 200 primordial follicles depending on the patient's age, and at the 60% maturation rate Picton demonstrated in sheep, that single biopsy yields 30 to 120 mature eggs from one procedure.

Even at half the sheep efficiency, the math inverts. Cut the maturation rate to 30% on 100 follicles and you still get 30 mature eggs, apply the 30% fertilization rate and you have nine embryos from one procedure — nearly double what standard IVF produces at four times the cost per embryo. The scarce resource stops being scarce. Gone. And the entire pricing architecture that fertility clinics have built over four decades around the assumption of egg scarcity becomes structurally obsolete.

The Cancer Patients Nobody Calls

The numbers look different when "fertility preservation" means choosing between eggs and life. Roughly 120,000 women under 44 are diagnosed with cancer annually in the United States, and between 50% and 60% receive gonadotoxic treatment (chemotherapy, radiation, or both) that can permanently destroy ovarian function. Standard fertility preservation requires two to three weeks of hormonal stimulation before cancer treatment can begin, which means every day spent saving future fertility is a day the tumor keeps growing. For aggressive cancers, that delay is unacceptable, so most oncologists skip the conversation entirely. Only 22.5% of eligible patients are even referred for fertility preservation, according to a 2025 study of 510 patients. Of those referred, roughly half complete the process. The rest drop off before treatment even starts. Net result: about 7% of at-risk women preserve their fertility. The other 93% start chemotherapy and hope, because hormonal stimulation is incompatible with cancer treatment urgency.

IVM attacks every barrier in that funnel. Every one. No hormonal stimulation means no two-week delay, and ovarian tissue can be harvested during the same surgery that removes the tumor. No stimulation drugs eliminates $3,000–$7,000 in costs and removes the contraindication for hormone-sensitive cancers. And critically, because the eggs are extracted and matured outside the body, there is zero risk of reintroducing cancer cells, a genuine hazard with ovarian tissue transplantation where frozen cortex strips can harbor dormant malignant cells.

If IVM raised the preservation rate from 7% to 25% of at-risk women, an additional 12,000 women per year would bank viable eggs at roughly $6,000 per procedure versus the current $10,000–$15,000. Each of those women avoids a potential future donor-egg cycle costing $25,000–$45,000, or the compounding toll of post-treatment infertility — the realization that a window closed not because of biology but because of a scheduling conflict between surgery and egg retrieval. Conservative downstream avoided cost per woman: $20,000, putting total annual system savings at $240 million.

What Hasn't Been Proven

The strongest argument against excitement is three decades of disappointing human IVM data. Since Eppig and O'Brien matured mouse eggs in 1996, clinical IVM in humans has achieved maturation rates of 27%–50%, well below the sheep study's 60%, with lower per-embryo implantation rates. The gap between mouse success and this sheep success was 30 years, and Kristensen estimates a human live birth is another decade away at minimum, meaning any woman who needs this technology before 2036 will not have it and any woman who eventually benefits will owe something to five sheep born in a Leeds laboratory.

Picton herself was explicit that this will not replace standard IVF for routine use: the ovarian biopsy requires laparoscopic surgery under general anesthesia, which is far more invasive than the transvaginal needle used in conventional retrieval, and the protocol makes sense only for women who face permanent ovarian damage from cancer treatment or who have already exhausted conventional IVF options and are running out of time. Five lambs from 18 recipients is a 28% success rate per transfer, roughly comparable to human IVF but with a tiny sample and wide confidence intervals. The study has not been peer-reviewed; it was a conference presentation at ESHRE, meaning independent scrutiny of the culture conditions, embryo viability data, and offspring health metrics is still pending.

The fertilization rate matters: thirty percent is half the standard IVF rate of 60%–70%. That gap could reflect subtle deficiencies in lab-matured eggs: chromosomal abnormalities, epigenetic irregularities, or cytoplasmic immaturity that do not manifest until embryo development or, worse, in the next generation. Picton's one female lamb producing two healthy offspring of her own is encouraging but constitutes a single data point.

The Bottom Line

Five lambs will not change anyone's fertility treatment next year or the year after. What they change is the assumption governing reproductive medicine for five decades: that mature eggs are inherently scarce, that we must pay $1,500 each to retrieve them, and that cancer patients who cannot wait three weeks for stimulation should accept the loss. Picton's 60% maturation rate in a large-animal model is the first credible evidence that egg supply is an engineering problem, not a biological wall. If human IVM reaches even half that efficiency, the economics of a $10 billion US fertility industry invert. If you are a cancer patient under 40 facing gonadotoxic chemotherapy, ask your oncologist about ovarian tissue banking. The eggs sitting untouched in those samples may be more useful than anyone thought. Just not yet.
