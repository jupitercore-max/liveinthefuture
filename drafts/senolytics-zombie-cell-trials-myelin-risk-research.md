# Research Notes: Senolytics Zombie Cell Trials — The Myelin Risk

## Story Summary
30+ clinical trials are now testing senolytic drugs — compounds that kill "zombie cells" (senescent cells) — across Alzheimer's, pulmonary fibrosis, diabetic eye disease, osteoarthritis, and frailty. The field has attracted a projected $53.39B market by 2033. But the leading publicly traded senolytics company (Unity Biotechnology) delisted from Nasdaq and trades at $0.06/share. And a March 2026 UConn study published in PNAS found that the most popular senolytic combination — dasatinib + quercetin — damages brain myelin in mice, resembling multiple sclerosis pathology. Meanwhile, thousands of people are self-dosing these drugs off-label via online group buys.

## Strongest Counterargument
The UConn myelin study used continuous dosing in mice, not the intermittent "hit-and-run" protocol used in human trials. The Mayo Clinic's standard regimen is 3 days on, weeks off — specifically designed to let target engagement occur without sustained toxic exposure. The dosing regimen matters enormously: dasatinib's half-life is 3-5 hours, so intermittent dosing clears the drug before oligodendrocytes sustain cumulative damage. It's plausible the UConn findings don't translate to intermittent human protocols. But nobody has tested this explicitly, and the self-dosing community isn't following controlled protocols.

## Limitations
- Most human trial data is Phase 1 (safety, not efficacy)
- Mouse-to-human translation for brain myelin effects unknown
- No long-term senolytic safety data in humans (longest: ~2 years)
- Unity's commercial failure may reflect business execution, not science failure
- ClinicalTrials.gov count includes not-yet-recruiting and completed
- Off-label self-dosing scale unknown (forum anecdotes, not surveys)
