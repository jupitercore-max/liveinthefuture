Samsung Made $58 Billion Selling Chips. Then Its Phone Division Posted Its First-Ever Loss.

Samsung's semiconductor arm posted 89.4 trillion won in operating profit last quarter — a 19× year-over-year explosion. Its mobile division, meanwhile, is expected to report a quarterly loss for the first time in company history. Same company. Same memory chips. Opposite ledgers.

By Priya Chandrasekaran · Semiconductors & Computing · July 19, 2026 · ☕ 7 min read

Eighty-nine point four trillion won. That is Samsung Electronics' Q2 2026 operating profit, roughly $58.44 billion, beating the LSEG SmartEstimate of 87.3 trillion won on revenue of 171 trillion won that rose 129% year-over-year, which by any conventional measure makes this the most dominant quarter in the company's fifty-seven-year history as a chipmaker.

Its phone division might have had the worst.

According to South Korean financial outlet DealSite, Samsung's Mobile Experience (MX) division is expected to post a loss of 530 billion to 1.06 trillion won ($364 million to $729 million) for Q2 2026, which, if confirmed when Samsung reports segmented earnings, would mark the first quarterly loss in the mobile division's entire history — not the worst quarter, but the first time the number goes negative.

For context: Samsung's phone business stayed profitable through the Galaxy Note7 battery explosion crisis in 2016, when the company recalled 2.5 million devices, killed the entire product line, and still eked out $73 million in mobile operating profit that quarter. An exploding phone couldn't put Samsung MX in the red, but memory prices could.

One Company, Two Ledgers

Samsung is one of three companies on earth that manufactures DRAM and NAND flash memory, and it is simultaneously the world's largest smartphone manufacturer, shipping 62.8 million units in Q1 2026 with 3.6% growth while the overall market shrank 4%. The Galaxy S26 broke pre-order records in Korea and ran 25% above S25 figures in the United States.

Sales weren't the problem — margins were.

AI-driven demand has sent memory prices skyward, with Citi Research reporting DRAM average selling prices up 44% quarter-over-quarter in Q2 and NAND up 53%, part of an industry-wide surge that has pushed memory prices roughly 850% higher year-over-year. Samsung's semiconductor division rode that wave to a profit 19 times larger than a year earlier, when it scraped together a comparatively modest 4.7 trillion won.

But Samsung's phone division buys memory from Samsung's chip division, and Samsung DS does not offer a family discount.

The Internal Memory Tax

This is where the numbers get uncomfortable.

According to reporting from Mohammed Brückner and corroborated by Money Today, Samsung DS renegotiated internal transfer prices at CES 2026, pushing the cost of a 12GB LPDDR5X module from $33 to $70 — a $37 increase on a single component in the Galaxy S26 Ultra that the mobile division had no leverage to refuse, because the chip division was simply passing through the market price that Nvidia, Google, and every other AI infrastructure buyer was already paying without blinking.

NAND flash followed a similar trajectory, with ASPs up 53% quarter-over-quarter translating a base cost of roughly $25 into approximately $38, adding another $13 per device and pushing total memory cost inflation per flagship phone to roughly $50.

Now multiply that across Samsung's full volume. Samsung shipped 62.8 million smartphones in Q1 2026 at an average of about 12GB RAM each, and if similar volumes held into Q2 — pre-order data suggests they did — the additional memory cost burden across the mobile division was approximately $3.14 billion for the quarter. Samsung MX's operating profit in a strong year runs $8–11 billion annually, or $2–2.75 billion per quarter, which means the memory cost increase alone exceeded the entire mobile division's typical profit margin in a good quarter, turning a historically profitable operation into a money-losing one through internal procurement alone.

Why Can't Samsung Just… Discount Itself?

A reasonable question with an unreasonable answer. Samsung DS operates as a functionally independent profit center under a "Quarterly Adjustment Agreement" system where contracts between divisions reset every three months, benchmarked to open-market pricing, and DS treats MX as, in the words of one internal source cited by WCCFTech, "just another external client."

This isn't pettiness — it's governance. Samsung is a publicly traded conglomerate with minority shareholders, institutional investors, and Korean corporate governance rules that make below-market internal transfers a potential securities violation. If DS sold memory to MX at $33 when the market price is $70, DS shareholders could argue the chip division was subsidizing the phone division at their expense, and TM Roh, the head of MX division, reportedly warned Samsung management of a potential annual deficit under current transfer pricing before MX was placed under "emergency management."

Samsung is simultaneously the biggest winner and the biggest loser from the AI memory boom, and the irony is structural: it cannot fix this by talking to itself.

4,600 Phones per Rack

The reason Samsung DS won't budge is sitting in Nvidia's product catalog.

A single Nvidia Vera CPU requires 1.5 terabytes of LPDDR5X memory — 125 times the 12 gigabytes in a Galaxy S26 Ultra — and an NVL144 rack-scale AI platform packs 36 of those CPUs, consuming the LPDDR5X equivalent of roughly 4,500 Galaxy S26 Ultras per rack, which means a single datacenter order for a hundred racks displaces more memory than Samsung ships in phones to the entire European market in a quarter.

At margins DS cannot achieve selling to its own phone division, that 4,600-phone-per-rack exchange rate (including interconnect overhead) makes the choice obvious. Nikkei Asia projects that DRAM supply in 2027 could meet only 60% of projected demand, and every gigabyte Samsung allocates to AI is a gigabyte it doesn't allocate to Galaxy phones.

Run the profitability math the other direction and the self-cannibalization becomes even more stark. Samsung DS's estimated margin on LPDDR5X sits at 45–50%, and on MX's 62.8 million phones at $70 per 12GB module, DS collects approximately $4.4 billion in memory revenue from its own phone division per quarter, pocketing roughly $2 billion of that as operating profit. Samsung the chipmaker earns three to six times more money from Samsung the phonemaker than Samsung the phonemaker earns selling phones to actual consumers, a dynamic that comes with a receipt more precise than any corporate self-cannibalization case study in business school history.

739 Workers in New Jersey Got the Memo

Today, July 19, Reuters reported exclusively that Samsung Electronics America is cutting 739 roles at its Englewood Cliffs, New Jersey headquarters, plus approximately 100 positions in Plano, Texas, including mobile division staff, while Samsung SDS America sheds another 179 roles — an "enterprise-wide reduction-in-force" notification that went out June 30 and is hitting workers who, in some cases, had just relocated to a brand-new NJ office that Samsung opened in September 2025.

Samsung says this is not a "broad, global restructuring," but employees fear otherwise, and the timing speaks louder than the press release.

Limitations

Samsung has not yet released segmented Q2 earnings; the MX division loss figures come from DealSite (South Korea) analyst estimates with a wide range ($1.38 billion profit to $1.09 billion loss), meaning the actual result could still land in the black. Internal transfer pricing details rely on journalist and analyst reporting, not Samsung's official disclosures, and our per-phone memory tax calculation uses the midpoint of reported price increases while assuming roughly stable Q1-to-Q2 shipment volumes, which may not hold. BOM cost ratios from Counterpoint Research and TechInsights are teardown estimates, not Samsung-confirmed figures, and DS profit margins on LPDDR5X (45–50%) are analyst estimates rather than audited numbers.

Strongest Counterargument

Samsung is a portfolio, not a phone company. The chip division's $58 billion operating profit dwarfs any plausible mobile loss by a factor of 80 to 160. Samsung's consolidated balance sheet has never looked better. If MX loses $729 million while DS makes $58.4 billion, the company made $57.7 billion. That's not a crisis; that's a rounding error.

Furthermore, Samsung can reprice upward. The Galaxy S26 already launched at a ¥60 (~$60) premium in Korea compared to the S25. If memory stays expensive, phone prices rise to match — and Samsung's record pre-orders suggest consumers will absorb the increase. The mobile division's loss may reflect a pricing lag, not a structural death spiral. Once ASPs catch up to component costs, MX margins recover. Meanwhile, Samsung's stock trades at the intersection of memory and AI, and the market values that intersection at $400 billion. The phone division subsidizing memory allocation to AI is arguably the optimal capital allocation strategy.

The Bottom Line

Samsung just demonstrated what happens when one half of a company cannibalizes the other — and both halves are right. DS is correct to charge market prices; below-market transfers would hurt shareholders and invite regulatory scrutiny. MX is correct that those prices make phones unprofitable at current retail pricing. Nobody made a mistake. The incentives simply diverged.

If you're buying a phone this year, expect prices to keep climbing. Memory now accounts for roughly 38% of flagship phone BOM costs, up from under 20% two years ago. That cost flows downstream whether you're buying Samsung, Apple, or Xiaomi — all three buy from the same oligopoly. If you're an investor evaluating Samsung, look at the consolidated number, not the division-level panic. And if you work in Samsung's mobile division in the United States, today's Reuters report suggests you should update your résumé regardless of what Samsung calls its restructuring. The company's center of gravity is shifting from handsets to server racks, and 4,600 phones' worth of memory per AI rack is the exchange rate.
