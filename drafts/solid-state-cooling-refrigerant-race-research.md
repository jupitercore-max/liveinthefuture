# Research: Solid-State Cooling — Three Competing Technologies Race to Kill the Refrigerant

## Journalist: Priya Desai (Energy/Climate beat)
## Article Number: 297
## Category: ⚡ Energy / 🌍 Climate

## Thesis
Three competing solid-state cooling approaches (barocaloric, electrocaloric, elastocaloric) are advancing simultaneously in 2026, racing to replace HFC refrigerants before the Kigali Amendment phasedown. Lab results have crossed critical thresholds — 20K+ temperature spans, 30K drops in 20 seconds — but a performance gap remains between what works in a lab and what runs a commercial refrigerator. The question: will solid-state cooling be ready before regulators force the transition?

## Primary Sources

### 1. Barocal (Barocaloric — Cambridge spin-out)
- Source: New Atlas, Pulse2, ACHR News, The Futurism Today
- $10M seed funding (May 2026)
- Founded by Dr. Xavier Moya, Cambridge University physicist, 15 years of research
- Technology: squeeze a material → it heats up, release → it cools down
- Claims 100x less warming potential than HFC refrigerants
- Targets data centers and commercial refrigeration first
- Potential to reduce HVAC energy use by up to 40%
- HVAC market: $577B by 2033

### 2. Dissolution Barocaloric (Chinese Academy of Sciences)
- Source: TechXplore, Interesting Engineering (Jan 2026)
- Prof. Li Bing's team
- 30K temperature drop in 20 seconds
- 54K cooling at higher temperatures
- Combines solid coolant with liquid flow — "dissolution barocaloric"
- Surpasses solid-state alternatives in efficiency and scalability
- Zero-carbon refrigeration

### 3. Electrocaloric (Multilayer Capacitors)
- Source: American Ceramic Society, Nature (2026), SciEnMag
- New prototype: 20K temperature span, 4.2W cooling power
- 50% better span than previous models, 15x more cooling power
- Nature paper: PST-PMW ceramic blend achieves 3-4K temperature swings
- Eliminates 42-day annealing process, 120-160% efficiency improvement
- Compact, silent, no moving parts

### 4. Elastocaloric (Nitinol-based)
- Source: Spain/EU research, Contractor Magazine
- Uses nickel-titanium (Nitinol) shape memory alloys
- 3-5x efficiency vs traditional vapor-compression
- EU Commission recognized
- Spain developing 3D-printed NiTi components
- Hydronic systems — shifts from refrigerant pipes to water loops

### 5. Regulatory Context
- Kigali Amendment (2016): 80% HFC phasedown by 2047
  - Developed countries: 10% reduction by 2019, 85% by 2036
  - Developing countries (Group 1): freeze 2024, 80% by 2045
- EPA Technology Transitions Program: effective Jan 1, 2025, systems with GWP>700 must transition
- A2L refrigerant shortages complicating transition (May 2026 HVAC/P report)

### 6. Scale of the Problem
- Cooling = ~15% of global electricity consumption
- HFCs: 1,000-4,000x the Global Warming Potential (GWP) of CO2
- Cooling demand projected to triple by 2050 (IEA)
- Data centers: fastest-growing cooling demand segment
- ~3.6 billion cooling appliances in use worldwide
- Without intervention: HFC emissions could contribute 0.5°C of warming by 2100

## Commercial Viability Threshold
A practical solid-state cooling system needs:
- Temperature span: ≥40K (fridge: ~25K, AC: ~20-30K, but with cascading/regeneration overhead need ~40K raw)
- Cooling power: ≥100W for small appliance, ≥1kW for HVAC
- COP (coefficient of performance): >3 to match or beat vapor-compression (~COP 3-5)
- Cycle life: millions of cycles without degradation
- Cost: competitive with compressor systems ($200-500 for residential)

## Strongest Counterargument
Vapor-compression refrigeration has had 100+ years of optimization, economies of scale, and a massive installed base. Transitioning to low-GWP HFOs (hydrofluoroolefins) and natural refrigerants (CO2, ammonia, propane) may be "good enough" — and are already happening. The solid-state approaches might arrive too late, at too high a cost, solving a problem the chemical industry already addressed with drop-in replacements.

## Limitations
- Most lab results are from small-scale prototypes (mm-scale materials)
- Cooling power numbers are often extrapolated from material properties, not demonstrated systems
- Cycle fatigue data (millions of cycles) largely unavailable
- Cost projections are speculative — no manufacturing at scale
- COP numbers from Barocal are "projected," not independently verified
