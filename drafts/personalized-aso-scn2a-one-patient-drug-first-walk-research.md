# Research: Personalized ASO Therapy for SCN2A Epilepsy — N-of-1 Drugs That Let a 15-Year-Old Walk

## Primary Sources

### 1. Nature Medicine Paper (July 21, 2026)
- **Citation:** Kim-McManus, O., et al. (2026). Individualized antisense oligonucleotides for SCN2A-related developmental epileptic encephalopathy. *Nature Medicine*. DOI: 10.1038/s41591-026-04527-y
- **Institution:** UC San Diego + Rady Children's Institute for Genomic Medicine
- **PI:** Olivia Kim-McManus, MD (associate professor of neurosciences, UC San Diego)
- **Trial design:** Two separate n-of-1, open-label, single-center, single-patient clinical trials under FDA research INDs
- **Patients:** Two children (ages 9 and 14 at study start) with SCN2A-related developmental epileptic encephalopathy (DEE)
- **Follow-up:** 2 years per patient

### 2. Results
**Patient 1 (age 9):**
- Had seizures nearly every day before treatment
- 26% reduction in seizure frequency
- Reduced medication load
- Improvements in language, motor skills, sensory processing, adaptive behaviors
- Reduction in autism-related behaviors

**Patient 2 (age 14):**
- 90% reduction in seizure frequency
- Reached stretches of seizure-free days
- Walked independently for the first time at age 15
- Chronic gastrointestinal issues improved, requiring less medication
- Reduced medication load

**Safety:** No serious side effects or ASO-related adverse events. Routine labs, ECGs, EEGs remained stable.

### 3. Mechanism
- SCN2A mutations are de novo (not inherited), affect one copy of the gene
- Allele-selective ASOs target harmless SNP regions near the disease-causing mutation on the pathogenic haplotype
- Silences the mutant gene while leaving the functional copy intact
- Intrathecal delivery (lumbar injection under anesthesia)
- Not permanent — affects gene expression, not DNA sequence
- Repeated every 2-3 months
- When doses spaced too far apart, benefits (like walking) began to wane → frequency adjusted with FDA approval

### 4. Context: JAMA Neurology Study (May 2026)
- **Citation:** JAMA Neurology, Precision Antisense Oligonucleotide Therapy Amenability for Infantile Genetic Epilepsies
- 160 infants assessed with genetic epilepsies (172 total variants, 152 unique)
- **16% (25/160 infants) amenable to ASO therapies**
  - 54% for knockdown, 29% for wild-type upregulation, 13% for splice correction, 4% for exon-skipping
- Of the 25 amenable, 68% (17/25) could be currently considered for ASO therapies
- Additional 3 (12%) now deceased, could have been considered at seizure onset

### 5. Context: Zorevunersen (Stoke Therapeutics) — Dravet Syndrome
- Phase 1/2a results (Northwestern, March 2026)
- 81 patients with Dravet syndrome, ages 2-18
- 85% seizure reduction at 3 months, 73% at 6 months (70 mg dose)
- Open-label extension: 58-90% reduction over 20 months (45 mg dose every 4 months)
- Phase 3 double-blind placebo-controlled trial underway
- This is a "one-size-fits-some" approach — targets SCN1A Dravet broadly, not individualized

### 6. Precedent: Milasen (2019)
- First personalized ASO: Timothy Yu, MD, PhD, at Boston Children's Hospital
- Created for Mila Makovec, a single child with Batten disease (CLN7)
- Published in NEJM, 2019
- Cost reported as ~$150K development (academic setting, donated services)
- Mila sadly passed away in 2021 — disease was too advanced when treatment started
- Demonstrated feasibility of N-of-1 ASO development

## Original Analysis

### Cost of N-of-1 Drug Development
**Development costs (estimated range):**
- Whole genome sequencing: $300-$1,000
- ASO design and screening (computational + in vitro): $20,000-$50,000
- GMP manufacturing of custom ASO: $50,000-$100,000 per batch
- Preclinical safety studies (abbreviated for n-of-1): $30,000-$80,000
- FDA IND application and regulatory: $10,000-$20,000
- **Total development: ~$110,000-$250,000 per patient**

**Ongoing treatment costs (estimated):**
- ASO synthesis per dose: $500-$2,000
- Intrathecal injection (hospital procedure under anesthesia): $3,000-$8,000
- 4-6 doses per year
- **Annual treatment: ~$14,000-$60,000 per year**

### Cost comparison: Refractory epilepsy standard care
- Annual direct medical costs for drug-resistant epilepsy: $16,000-$47,000/year (CDC, Epilepsy Foundation data)
- Emergency department visits: $3,000-$5,000 per visit
- Average 3+ anti-seizure medications: $5,000-$15,000/year
- Hospitalizations: $10,000+ per episode
- Neurodevelopmental support services: $10,000-$30,000/year
- **Lifetime cost for refractory pediatric epilepsy: $1M-$4M+**

### Kill test: Original contribution
1. **Nobody has calculated the addressable population for personalized ASOs in epilepsy:** Using the JAMA data (16% of infantile genetic epilepsies amenable), I'll extrapolate to the US epilepsy population. ~3.4 million Americans have epilepsy. ~30% are drug-resistant (~1 million). Of those, genetic causes account for roughly 30-50% of pediatric epilepsies. If 16% of genetic epilepsies are ASO-amenable, that's a calculable number.
2. **Timeline comparison nobody ran:** Traditional drug development = 10-15 years, $2.6B average (Tufts). Milasen was developed in ~1 year. These SCN2A ASOs — what was the timeline from sequencing to first dose?
3. **Cost-per-QALY comparison:** If a child goes from daily seizures to 90% reduction and gains independent walking, what's the QALY gain? Traditional cost-effectiveness thresholds are $50,000-$150,000/QALY. This therapy could be wildly cost-effective even at $250K development cost if it produces years of dramatically improved quality of life.

### Strongest counterargument
- N=2 with no blinding, no placebo control. Each child served as their own control, but the open-label design means placebo effects and observer bias cannot be ruled out.
- The 26% reduction in the younger patient is modest — standard ASMs can achieve similar or better reduction.
- Treatment must continue indefinitely (every 2-3 months under anesthesia) — this isn't a cure.
- Scalability is questionable: each drug requires custom design, GMP manufacturing, and FDA IND filing. Can this work for thousands of patients?
- The developmental gains (like walking) are hard to attribute solely to ASO therapy — children develop over 2 years regardless.

### Limitations
- Two patients. Cannot generalize safety or efficacy.
- No randomization, no blinding, no placebo arm.
- Open-label design creates bias in subjective outcome measures (developmental assessments).
- Treatment durability unknown beyond 2 years.
- Cost estimates are rough — actual regulatory and manufacturing costs may vary significantly.
- Addressable population calculations require assumptions about genetic testing penetration and diagnostic rates.
