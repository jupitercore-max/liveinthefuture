One Man Used a Brain Implant to Speak 183,060 Sentences at Home. The Waiting List for Everyone Else Is 29,000 Years Long.

A UC Davis team published the first evidence that a speech brain-computer interface can sustain high-accuracy, independent daily use for nearly two years. Casey Harrell, paralyzed by ALS, communicated at 56 words per minute with 92% accuracy across 3,800 hours of home use — 5.6 times faster than the best eye-gaze typing. An original scaling analysis shows why the technology that changed one life cannot yet reach the 24,000 Americans who need it.

Jordan Kessler · Neuro · June 17, 2026

Three thousand, eight hundred and one hours.

That is how long Casey Harrell has spent talking through his brain. Not in a lab, under fluorescent lights, with a postdoc hovering over a laptop. At home. In his living room. Arguing with colleagues on video calls, reading bedtime stories to a daughter who had never understood his voice, managing a full-time career as an environmental activist, all through 256 electrodes implanted in the strip of cortex that once controlled his mouth, tongue, and jaw before amyotrophic lateral sclerosis took them away.

A study published Monday in Nature Medicine by Sergey Stavisky, David Brandman, and their UC Davis colleagues documents what they call "the first power user" of a speech brain-computer interface. The numbers are staggering in their mundaneness. Over 22.6 months, Harrell used his BCI on 364 of 397 eligible days. He spoke 183,060 sentences, close to two million words. His care partner plugged him in each morning in about 20 minutes; then the system ran continuously for up to 19 hours without anyone from the research team touching it. He rated 92% of his decoded sentences as accurate or mostly correct. In controlled benchmarks with a 125,000-word vocabulary, accuracy hit 99.2%.

"He's the first power user of a speech BCI," Stavisky told MIT Technology Review. That label understates it. Harrell isn't just the first person to use a speech BCI independently at home for an extended period. He is, by a wide margin, the first person to prove that a brain implant can function as a primary communication device: not an experiment you participate in, but a tool you wake up and use the way the rest of us use our voices.

5.6 Times Faster Than the Alternative

The clinical significance becomes clear when you compare Harrell's BCI against every other communication technology available to people with severe ALS. Here is the comparison nobody else has assembled:

Technology Speed (WPM) Accuracy Invasiveness
Harrell's BCI (silent speech) 56 92% daily / 99.2% controlled Open brain surgery
Harrell's BCI (vocalized speech) ~30 99%+ controlled Same
Eye-gaze typing + LLM assist 13–16 Improved with LLM Non-invasive
Standard eye-gaze typing <10 Variable Non-invasive
Head mouse / gyroscopic control 5–10 Low Non-invasive
Endovascular BCI (Synchron Stentrode) Click-based Good Minimally invasive
Natural human speech 130–190 ~100% N/A

At 56 words per minute, Harrell's BCI communicates 5.6 times faster than standard eye-gaze typing and reaches 37% of natural speech speed. That gap matters enormously: research on AAC use in ALS consistently finds that communication rates below 15 WPM cause users to disengage from conversations, ceding participation in their own lives to the tyranny of latency. Eye-gaze typing, the current standard of care, sits below that threshold. A 2024 study in Nature Communications showed that even after adding large language model predictions to eye-gaze interfaces, peak rates reached only 13–16 WPM. Harrell's BCI doesn't just incrementally improve on existing tools. It changes the category of interaction from "laborious text entry" to something approaching conversation.

Harrell discovered this himself through usage. He initially spoke aloud while using the device, producing audible but slurred vocalizations that the electrodes decoded at roughly 30 words per minute with near-perfect accuracy. Over months, he transitioned to "silent speech" — making the facial muscle movements of talking without producing sound — which was less fatiguing and, counterintuitively, faster: above 50 WPM. The system's transformer-based decoder, an upgrade from the original recurrent neural network architecture introduced around post-implant day 600, handled the switch without requiring Harrell to retrain from scratch. The AI running on a cart in his bedroom recalibrated itself continuously in the background, adapting to neural drift without interrupting his conversations.

The Signal Held

This was never supposed to work for two years. The conventional concern with intracortical microelectrode arrays — the "Utah arrays" manufactured by Blackrock Neurotech that form the hardware backbone of BrainGate — is that the brain's immune response encapsulates them in scar tissue, gradually muffling the neural signals they record. A 2024 analysis of 14 BrainGate participants found that, on average, only 35.6% of electrodes successfully recorded spiking activity, with a 7% decline over enrollment periods averaging 2.8 years.

Harrell's arrays have defied that average. More than 90% of his 256 electrodes consistently detected neural spiking at frequencies above 2 Hz throughout the entire 19-month analysis period. The speech-related neural modulation patterns — the fingerprint the decoder uses to distinguish "cat" from "hat" from silence — showed cosine similarity above 0.6 even when comparing recordings separated by 18 months. The signal held. Why it held for Harrell when it degrades faster in others is not yet understood, but the implication is significant: at least in some patients, the hardware can sustain the kind of long-term performance that makes a BCI worth the surgical risk.

29,000 Years to Serve the Waiting List

Here is where the story turns uncomfortable. Harrell's BCI works. The question is how many other people will ever get one.

Approximately 30,000 Americans are living with ALS at any given time, with roughly 5,000 new diagnoses each year. Between 80% and 90% of ALS patients develop dysarthria — the loss of intelligible speech — during the course of the disease. That puts the conservative US population of potential BCI candidates at 24,000 to 27,000 people.

BrainGate, the longest-running and most successful intracortical BCI clinical trial in history, has enrolled 14 participants across seven clinical sites over 17 years. That is 0.82 patients per year.

At that rate, serving the current US ALS population would take 29,268 years. Accelerating enrollment by a factor of 100 — an almost unimaginable expansion of neurosurgical capacity, array manufacturing, and research infrastructure — still yields a 293-year timeline. To serve 1,000 patients per year, the field would need 50 to 100 trained neurosurgical teams capable of performing this implantation (current estimate: three to five), a manufacturing pipeline for tens of thousands of microelectrode arrays annually, and a technical support infrastructure to maintain the multi-computer systems in thousands of homes simultaneously.

No commercial BCI company is close to this. Neuralink's N1 chip, with 1,024 electrodes and fully wireless architecture, is the most technically advanced competitor, but it has implanted three to five patients and has not published speech decoding results. Synchron's endovascular Stentrode avoids open brain surgery entirely but relies on click-based communication — orders of magnitude slower than Harrell's speech decoding. Paradromics promises 65,536 electrodes per system but remains in preclinical development.

The Strongest Case Against

The most powerful objection to celebrating this study is not that it doesn't work. It obviously does. The objection is that it works in a form that cannot scale.

Harrell's system requires a five-hour open craniotomy to place four electrode arrays through two titanium pedestals that permanently protrude from his skull. The computing hardware fills a mobile cart. The system cannot leave his home. Setup each morning requires a trained care partner. And the study involved exactly one participant, in one clinical context, at one institution. When Mariska Vansteesel, a BCI researcher at Utrecht Medical Center, was asked to evaluate the results, she noted that another ALS patient she had worked with used a fully implanted BCI for seven years before it stopped functioning, apparently due to brain degeneration from the disease itself. There is no guarantee Harrell's electrodes will hold another year.

Jane Huggins, who develops noninvasive BCIs at the University of Michigan, raised a different concern: aversion. Her research has found consistent reluctance among ALS patients toward the hospital stays required for implant surgery. If the people who need it won't opt in, the technology's accuracy is irrelevant.

What the Data Doesn't Show

This study has material gaps that future work must address. It involves a single participant; the generalizability to other individuals, implant sites, electrode types, or neurological conditions is unknown. The system's bulkiness prevented any use outside Harrell's home, meaning the BCI works only in a fixed location. No systematic fatigue assessment was performed, though the sustained 9.5-hour average daily usage suggests Harrell tolerated the system well. Speech decoding accuracy during free conversation (92%) was consistently lower than during structured copy tasks (99.2%), a gap the authors attribute to longer utterances, fatigue, and the inherent difficulty of decoding unconstrained speech. The study also cannot distinguish whether Harrell's exceptional electrode longevity is typical or an outlier — the BrainGate consortium's own data suggests average electrode performance is significantly worse.

The Bottom Line

Casey Harrell's brain implant did not just restore his speech. It gave him back his career, his parenting, and his social life. "I can simply tell her how much I love her," he said of his seven-year-old daughter, speaking through the device. Every transformative medical technology started as a bespoke research installation available to almost no one. Cochlear implants went from a single-channel device in 1978 to more than one million implanted worldwide. Cardiac pacemakers went from external tabletop units in 1952 to 1.5 million implanted annually. The transition from a cart of computers in a California living room to a device a neurologist can prescribe is an engineering challenge, not a physics barrier — and this study is the first proof that the end state is worth building toward.

If you are a person with ALS or a caregiver: the BrainGate2 trial is actively enrolling at multiple US sites. Ask your neurologist about referral. Separately, eye-gaze typing with LLM-assisted prediction (Google's SpeakFaster project, for example) can meaningfully improve communication speed today, without surgery, and is worth exploring if current AAC tools feel intolerably slow.

If you are a researcher or engineer: the bottleneck has moved. The science of decoding speech from neural signals is now demonstrated at clinical-utility levels. What remains is wireless miniaturization, manufacturing scale, and the regulatory pathway to a prescribable device. The gap between 0.82 patients per year and the 24,000 who need this technology is the problem that now matters most.

If you are a policymaker: Medicare currently does not cover brain-computer interfaces for communication. Neither does any private insurer. Harrell's system costs nothing to the patient because it is a research trial. When it eventually becomes a commercial device, the coverage question will determine whether BCIs are available to thousands of ALS patients or remain accessible only to those who can afford out-of-pocket neurosurgery.
