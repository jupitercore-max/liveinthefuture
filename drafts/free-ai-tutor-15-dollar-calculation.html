A Free AI Tutor for Every Student Would Cost $15 Per Year. Nobody Is Building It.

Harvard published the first rigorous RCT proving AI tutoring outperforms active learning classrooms. Open-source LLMs have driven inference costs below $0.10 per million tokens. Combine these two facts and the arithmetic is brutal: giving every one of America's 49.4 million public school students a personal AI tutor would cost roughly $741 million per year, or $15 per student. The U.S. spends $15,633 per student already. Alpha School charges $33,000 for an AI-first model that earned a D+ on independent evaluation. The ingredients for something radically better already exist in the open. The missing piece is not technology. It is assembly.

Jordan Kessler AI & Society April 27, 2026

Fifteen dollars. That is the annual per-student inference cost of running a personal AI tutor on open-source models at current pricing, assuming 30 minutes of daily tutoring interaction across 180 school days. Straightforward calculation, enormous implications, and nobody with the resources to build it is actually doing it.

In June 2025, researchers Greg Kestin, Kelly Miller, and colleagues at Harvard University published a randomized controlled trial in Scientific Reports that should have detonated the entire education technology industry. They randomly assigned 194 undergraduates to either an AI-powered tutor or Harvard's own active learning classroom, then measured learning outcomes on identical material with identical pre- and post-tests. Students in the AI tutor group learned significantly more in significantly less time. Students reported feeling more engaged, more motivated, and more confident. This was not AI versus a bored lecturer reading slides. This was AI versus one of the best active-learning physics classrooms in the country, designed by the same researchers who built the AI system, using the same pedagogical principles. AI won.

The $15 Calculation

Here is the math nobody is running, laid out so you can check every assumption.

A 30-minute tutoring session generates approximately 4,000 tokens of student input (questions, answers, working through problems) and 8,000 tokens of tutor output (explanations, scaffolded hints, encouragement, follow-up questions). That is conservative; Khanmigo sessions average shorter exchanges because students disengage from generic responses. Call it 12,000 total tokens per session.

Meta's Llama 4 Scout, released in March 2026, costs $0.08 per million input tokens and $0.30 per million output tokens through commercial API providers. Self-hosting on commodity GPU clusters drops this further, but the API price is the conservative upper bound for our calculation.

Per session: (4,000 × $0.08 / 1,000,000) + (8,000 × $0.30 / 1,000,000) = $0.00032 + $0.0024 = $0.00272. Call it $0.003 per session, or $0.54 per student per year across 180 school days of raw inference.

But inference is not the only cost, because a production tutoring platform needs retrieval-augmented generation over curriculum content, student progress tracking, safety guardrails, content moderation, and infrastructure overhead. Multiply the raw inference cost by 10x for engineering and operational overhead, following the standard SaaS infrastructure multiplier used by cloud cost analysts. That yields $5.40 before curriculum costs, which drop to zero if you use Open Educational Resources. IES, the Institute of Education Sciences, documents the maturation of OER libraries aligned to Common Core and state standards across all K-12 subjects. OpenStax, CK-12, and Khan Academy's own content library are free, openly licensed, and machine-readable.

Add a safety and moderation layer, student identity management, teacher dashboards, and a 50% margin for unforeseen costs, and you reach roughly $15 per student per year. Here is the comparison that should make every school board member furious:

Why This Does Not Exist Yet

Khan Academy is the closest thing to this vision, and it is not close enough. Khanmigo runs on OpenAI's GPT-4, meaning Khan Academy pays per-token costs to a proprietary vendor whose pricing can change without notice and whose model cannot be audited, modified, or self-hosted by districts. At $10 per student for districts, Khan Academy's offering is cheap, but it creates a dependency chain: school districts pay Khan Academy, which pays OpenAI, which can raise prices or change model behavior at any time. Student interaction data flows through OpenAI's infrastructure with whatever privacy policy OpenAI has this week, and for districts governed by FERPA and COPPA, that dependency is not just a cost risk but a legal one.

Google's LearnLM initiative launched free AI literacy training for 6 million U.S. educators in 2025, and NotebookLM provides free generative AI tools grounded in user-uploaded content. These are tools for teachers, not tutors for students, a critical distinction that preserves Google's position as infrastructure rather than committing to the hard pedagogical engineering Harvard demonstrated.

Harvard's RCT revealed why most AI tutoring fails and what makes it succeed. Kestin's team did not simply plug GPT-4 into a chat window. They built a system with "targeted, content-rich prompt engineering" that enforced pedagogical best practices: Socratic questioning instead of answer-giving, cognitive load management that adapted pace to individual students, growth mindset framing, and deliberate avoidance of the "helpful AI assistant" pattern that lets students shortcut critical thinking. Critical insight: a well-designed AI tutor is not ChatGPT with a school theme. It is a constrained system that deliberately frustrates the student's desire for quick answers in service of deeper learning.

The Architecture That Should Exist

An open-source AI tutoring platform viable for free deployment at national scale would need five components, all of which exist today as separate projects that nobody has assembled into a coherent system:

1. Open-weight LLM with pedagogical fine-tuning. Start with Llama 4 Scout (109B parameters, 17B active via mixture-of-experts, 10M token context window) and fine-tune on the Harvard team's pedagogical framework: Socratic scaffolding, cognitive load awareness, growth mindset reinforcement, deliberate refusal to give direct answers. Model weights are open, and fine-tuning data can be generated synthetically from expert tutoring transcripts plus the pedagogical literature at a cost of a few thousand dollars in GPU time, once.

2. Curriculum-aligned RAG layer. Index the entire OER corpus (OpenStax, CK-12, Khan Academy content, MIT OpenCourseWare, state-specific standards documents) into a vector database. When a 7th-grader in Texas asks about fractions, the tutor retrieves Texas Essential Knowledge and Skills (TEKS) standards for that grade level and scaffolds from aligned content. When a 10th-grader in New York asks about the Civil War, the tutor grounds responses in New York State Learning Standards. This is not speculative infrastructure; retrieval-augmented generation over structured document collections is a solved problem deployed in production by hundreds of companies.

3. Student progress model. Track mastery at the concept level using spaced repetition algorithms (Leitner, SM-2, or modern Bayesian knowledge tracing). It should know that this student has mastered two-digit multiplication but struggles with carrying, and adjust its tutoring accordingly. This is the core technology behind Duolingo's adaptive engine and Knewton's (now Wiley's) adaptive learning platform, published and well-documented.

4. Safety and guardrail layer. A classifier (fine-tuned from Llama Guard or similar) that blocks off-topic content, detects emotional distress signals, prevents the tutor from being jailbroken into doing homework, and flags conversations for human review. Meta's Llama Guard family already provides open-weight safety classifiers designed exactly for this purpose.

5. Teacher dashboard. Real-time visibility into which students are struggling, what misconceptions recur across the classroom, and where the AI tutor fails. Teachers do not disappear from this model; instead, the teacher becomes a diagnostic specialist working with a caseload of 30 students whose struggles are already triaged by AI, rather than a lecturer who cannot possibly know what 30 individual brains are confused about simultaneously.

Who Should Build This

Meta has the most to gain and the least to lose. Llama is already open-weight and free for commercial use, and an education deployment at national scale would demonstrate Llama's viability for high-stakes applications while generating fine-tuning data from millions of student interactions. Meta's total R&D spend in 2025 was $57.37 billion. At scale, the entire annual inference budget for tutoring every American public school student would be less than 0.0013% of that figure. Google has LearnLM but will not compete with its own education partnerships. Microsoft funded Khanmigo for Teachers but channeled student tutoring back through paid district licensing. Apple has classroom management tools but no LLM play.

A more realistic path may be a nonprofit or government initiative, given that the U.S. Department of Education's Institute of Education Sciences already funds OER development and educational technology research while NSF funds AI research, and combining their mandates into a single open-source tutoring platform built on open models, deployed on government cloud infrastructure, aligned to existing state standards, and free at point of use for every public school student in the country would cost less annually than what the federal government spends on standardized testing administration.

The Strongest Case Against

Benjamin Bloom's famous 2-sigma finding, published in 1984, measured the effect of 1-on-1 human tutoring against conventional classroom instruction and found a two standard deviation improvement, meaning the average tutored student outperformed 98% of classroom-taught peers. Every replication attempt at scale has produced smaller effects. VanLehn's 2011 meta-analysis found intelligent tutoring systems achieved only 0.76 sigma, and that was with highly constrained domain-specific systems, not general-purpose LLMs. Kestin's Harvard RCT is a single study with 194 students at an elite university. It measured learning on two physics topics over two weeks, not a full K-12 curriculum over years. Extrapolating from a controlled Harvard experiment to "free AI tutoring for 49.4 million students" requires leaps that the evidence does not yet support, and the history of education technology is a graveyard of interventions that worked brilliantly in pilots and collapsed at scale when they encountered unmotivated students, distracted home environments, spotty internet, and the thousand confounding variables that randomized trials control away.

Honestly, this is correct, and it is why the $15 figure describes infrastructure cost, not guaranteed outcomes. Building the platform is the easy part. Proving it works across demographics, ages, subjects, and motivation levels will take years of deployment data. But the alternative is continuing to spend $15,633 per student on a system that Bloom proved inadequate four decades ago, while AI tutoring companies charge $33,000 for implementations that cannot produce coherent lesson plans for their own students.

What Is Missing From This Analysis

This calculation assumes internet access, which 14% of school-age children in the U.S. still lack at home according to Census Bureau data, though the FCC's E-Rate program subsidizes school connectivity. It assumes students will engage voluntarily with an AI tutor, which no evidence supports for younger children without adult supervision. It does not account for subjects where AI tutoring is unproven or potentially harmful: creative writing, physical education, social-emotional learning, laboratory sciences requiring hands-on equipment. Most critically, it does not address the question of whether replacing human interaction in education damages something important about childhood development that no efficiency calculation can capture. Kestin's Harvard study measured learning and engagement but did not measure loneliness, social skill development, or long-term motivation in the absence of human teachers.

The Bottom Line

A first rigorous RCT has now demonstrated that a carefully designed AI tutor can outperform a world-class active learning classroom. Open-source LLMs have collapsed the inference cost of running such a system to single-digit dollars per student per year. Open Educational Resources provide free, standards-aligned curriculum content. All five components needed to assemble a national-scale platform exist as production-grade open-source projects. What does not exist is the organization willing to integrate them into a free, open system and run the multi-year deployment studies needed to prove it works beyond a Harvard physics classroom. If you are a parent pushing your school board for better personalized learning, ask this question: why are we evaluating $10,000-per-year AI schools when the inference cost of a personal tutor is $15? If you are an engineer at Meta, Google, or Apple, the pedagogical blueprint is published, the model weights are free, and the curriculum is open. Building it is not the hardest part. Choosing to is.
