Tower Semi Just Spent $920M on Silicon Photonics Fabs. 70% Was Already Spoken For.

Tower Semiconductor announced a $3 billion Japan expansion on Monday, backed by a $1 billion government grant, to quintuple silicon photonics wafer production by late 2027. More than 70 percent of the new capacity was reserved by customers before construction began, mirroring the GPU allocation scramble of 2023 but for a component most people have never heard of: the optical chip inside every AI data center transceiver.

Jordan Kessler Semiconductors & Photonics July 14, 2026

Seventy percent of Tower Semiconductor’s expanded silicon photonics capacity was locked up by customers through 2028 before the company had finished pouring concrete in Arai, Japan, leaving less than a third of a massive new fab complex available for every hyperscaler, telecom, and AI startup on Earth to fight over for the next three years.

Tower’s announcement landed Monday: $3 billion total investment, a $1 billion grant from the Japanese government, two new 300 mm fabrication phases running simultaneously, and a target to increase annualized silicon photonics wafer shipments fivefold relative to Q4 2025. Shares surged 14 percent on the news, extending a 95 percent year-to-date run, because investors are not buying a semiconductor company so much as buying a toll road positioned at the only crossing for miles.

Silicon photonics chips sit inside optical transceivers, the small pluggable modules that convert electrical signals from GPUs and switches into light pulses that travel over fiber-optic cable to the next rack, the next row, the next building. Every AI training cluster uses thousands of them; every inference deployment uses hundreds. Without transceivers, a GPU is a space heater with no way to talk to its neighbors, and without silicon photonics wafers, there are no transceivers.

LightCounting, the optical networking research firm, pegs data center pluggable optics revenue at $15.5 billion in 2025, growing at 17 percent annually to exceed $34 billion by 2030, an enormous market that is accelerating precisely when fab capacity cannot keep pace. Silicon photonics’ share is climbing from 43 percent today to a projected 76 percent by decade’s end, displacing older indium phosphide designs at higher data rates, while co-packaged optics add another $9 billion in addressable demand by embedding photonic chips directly onto switch silicon.

Revenue is not the story here. Constraint is.

How Prepayment Became the Price of Admission

Three years ago, hyperscalers learned a painful lesson about GPU supply: they placed enormous orders with NVIDIA, waited months for delivery, and watched competitors who had ordered earlier get allocation priority, a dynamic that sorted winners from losers based not on willingness to pay but on when the purchase order arrived. Prepayers got chips; late movers got waitlisted. Now the same scramble is repeating in silicon photonics, quieter and with fewer suppliers, which makes the bottleneck harder to see and harder to route around.

Tower is not alone in reporting sold-out capacity. STMicroelectronics is quadrupling its silicon photonics production by 2027 with its PIC100 platform, and the company stated explicitly that every wafer in the expansion is “fully underpinned by customers’ long-term capacity reservation commitments.” Spoken for. Lumentum, which supplies the indium phosphide laser chips that pair with silicon photonics receivers, reported in June that its Japanese wafer fabrication facility is fully allocated, with laser chip volumes doubling year over year and a multihundred-million-dollar purchase order booked for the first half of 2027.

Marvell Technology, which builds the transimpedance amplifiers and driver chips that sit alongside silicon photonics PICs inside transceivers, reported interconnect revenue growing more than 70 percent year over year, with that product line approaching a billion-dollar annualized run rate. From wafer fabs to laser foundries to analog chip suppliers, every major node in the optical supply chain is either fully reserved or running at rated capacity.

Computing What’s Left

Nobody publishes global silicon photonics wafer capacity figures, so assembling them requires triangulation across earnings calls, investment disclosures, and analyst estimates. Tower’s disclosure is the most granular: $920 million in silicon photonics and silicon germanium equipment spending, a fivefold increase in wafer shipments, and 70 percent of expanded output reserved through 2028. STMicro confirms a fourfold ramp with full customer commitment; Lumentum confirms complete allocation on the laser chips that feed those same transceiver modules.

LightCounting’s demand projections imply that silicon photonics-enabled transceiver revenue will reach roughly $25.8 billion by 2030 at 76 percent market share, and since the photonic integrated circuit represents approximately 20 to 30 percent of module cost, that places the PIC wafer market alone at $5 to $8 billion by that date, requiring roughly 1.2 to 2.0 million 300 mm wafer starts annually at an average selling price of $3,000 to $5,000 per processed wafer.

Current global silicon photonics wafer capacity, across Tower, STMicro, GlobalFoundries, and smaller players, is estimated in the low hundreds of thousands of wafer starts per year. Combining Tower’s fivefold expansion with STMicro’s quadrupling roughly triples global supply by 2028, but if 70 percent of Tower’s new output and 100 percent of STMicro’s are reserved, and if existing fabs run at 85 percent utilization or above, unreserved capacity falls below 30 percent of projected demand.

Inside that gap lives the price premium: any AI infrastructure buildout that has not locked in silicon photonics supply faces lead times of 12 to 18 months, component costs 15 to 25 percent above contracted rates, or both.

Where Copper Runs Out

Copper offers an alternative, and at lower data rates it is a good one. Direct-attach cables and active electrical cables can carry signals between racks without any optical conversion, cheaper per link and simpler to deploy. At 400 gigabits per second over three meters, copper works fine; at 800 gigabits over two meters, it works adequately. But at 1.6 terabits per second, the data rate next-generation AI clusters will require by late 2027, copper reaches its physical limit at roughly one meter of reach, a distance that connects a GPU to its neighbor but not a rack to a switch, a switch to a spine, or a spine to the building next door.

For those longer distances, which define the topology of every large-scale AI training cluster, no copper substitute exists and none is coming. Silicon photonics is the only technology operating at 1.6T data rates over the 10 to 2,000 meters that modern data center fabrics require, and so the migration from 800G to 1.6T is not merely a bandwidth upgrade but a substitution cliff where copper alternatives vanish entirely, converting every link into an optical link that needs a silicon photonics chip at each end.

Marvell’s AEC ecosystem, branded the Golden Cable initiative, extends copper’s useful range by embedding active equalization electronics in the cable itself, but the physics of signal attenuation at 112 Gbaud PAM4 impose hard distance limits that no amount of equalization can overcome, and Marvell’s own roadmap shows optical interconnects dominating scale-up and data center interconnect fabrics from 2028 onward.

Intel as Wildcard

Here is the strongest counterargument to the capacity shortage narrative: Intel Foundry Services operates one of the most advanced silicon photonics platforms in the world, developed over two decades of internal R&D, and its fabs in Oregon and New Mexico have idle capacity that could theoretically be redirected to merchant production. Intel’s partnership with Tower was supposed to bring additional capacity online through Tower’s use of Intel fabs, but that arrangement collapsed when Intel decided not to move forward with the production flows, and the capacity reverted to Tower’s own facilities in Japan.

Intel still has the platform, though, having shipped millions of transceivers built on it, and GlobalFoundries similarly offers a silicon photonics process on its 300 mm lines in Crolles, France and Malta, New York. If demand premiums rise high enough, both companies have economic incentive and existing tooling to expand.

Qualification time is the catch. Silicon photonics is not a standard CMOS logic flow; it integrates waveguides, modulators, photodetectors, and germanium photodiodes on the same substrate, and each customer’s design requires months of process development and yield qualification before production can begin. Even if Intel or GlobalFoundries opened their order books tomorrow, new customers would not see production wafers for 9 to 15 months, a timeline that fills most of the gap between now and the 1.6T transition when demand will spike hardest.

What We Don’t Know

Several assumptions in this analysis deserve scrutiny. It relies on publicly reported investment amounts and production targets as proxies for actual wafer capacity, which fabs do not disclose in unit terms, and customer prepayment terms are confidential; Tower’s 70 percent reservation figure comes from its own investor materials and may represent revenue commitments rather than physical wafer allocations, which would change the calculus. Lead time estimates draw from industry reports and analyst commentary, not direct fab quotes, and the unreserved capacity figure of “below 30 percent” is a triangulated estimate, not a census.

Additionally, pluggable optics at 800G still uses some non-silicon-photonics approaches, including indium phosphide-based modulators and thin-film lithium niobate, which could absorb a portion of demand pressure. Co-packaged optics timelines remain uncertain; if CPO deployment slips from 2028 to 2030, near-term wafer pressure eases, and any new fab capacity that Intel or GlobalFoundries choose to bring online would further loosen the constraint.

What You Can Do About It

For data center operators planning AI infrastructure deployments beyond 2027, silicon photonics supply should be treated as a procurement risk on par with GPU allocation: if your transceiver vendor has not secured wafer capacity at Tower, STMicro, or a comparable fab, ask when they plan to, because the reservation window is closing and 12-month lead times are already the norm for uncommitted buyers. For investors, the silicon photonics supply chain offers a less crowded entry point than GPU makers, with Tower up 95 percent YTD but still trading at 15× forward earnings, Lumentum doubling laser chip volumes, and Marvell’s interconnect business approaching a $1 billion run rate. For engineers watching the 800G-to-1.6T transition, start qualifying your photonics supplier now, or plan to wait in line behind the hyperscalers who already did.
