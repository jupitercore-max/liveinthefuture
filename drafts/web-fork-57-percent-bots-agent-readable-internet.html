57% of Web Requests Are Now Bots. Publishers Are Building Them a Separate Internet.

TIME converts all its webpages to agent-readable markdown, strips 95.7% of tokens, and routes humans and bots to different versions of the same URL. Cloudflare data shows bots crossed the majority line at 57.3% of HTTP requests in June 2026. Publishers are constructing a parallel web for machines. The question nobody is asking: who captures the value?

Marcus Chen · Computing & AI
August 7, 2026

Three hundred three thousand two hundred thirty-five bytes of HTML is what TIME's website serves to a Chrome browser loading a single article, a sprawling payload of layout instructions, JavaScript bundles, ad containers, cookie consent banners, and navigation elements that have nothing to do with the words on the page. Route the same URL through ClaudeBot and you get roughly 13,000 bytes of clean markdown, which means the same server delivers two fundamentally different versions of the same content to two entirely different kinds of visitors who will never see each other's version, a 95.7% reduction that tells you exactly how much of the modern web exists to serve humans and how much exists to serve information.

TIME is not experimenting with this approach or running a limited pilot. According to Digiday, the publisher has converted all of its webpages into markdown and now blocks every AI bot by default, whitelisting only approved crawlers and routing them to stripped-down versions. "We're separating out that traffic," said Mark Howard, TIME's chief operating officer. Humans get the full page with ads and JavaScript. Bots get words and metadata.

A startup called TollBit handles the conversion, and its CEO Toshit Panigrahi frames the project in the language of token economics: "We see, on average, a 90% reduction in tokens, because we have converted the content to markdown." Processing a full HTML page can take AI systems over a minute, while TollBit claims its structured output fetches in 0.25 seconds, a speed advantage that matters when you realize who is doing most of the fetching.

Majority Crossed

Cloudflare CEO Matthew Prince disclosed in June that automated traffic had crossed 57.3% of worldwide HTTP requests to HTML content, with humans at 42.7%, a threshold he had predicted would not arrive until 2027 when he addressed SXSW in March. Before generative AI, bots ran near 20% of web traffic. In five months through December 2025, Cloudflare blocked 416 billion AI scrape requests.

Not all bots want the same thing. Cloudflare's crawl-to-click analysis found training-related bots account for 50.6% of AI bot traffic while search-related bots make up just 10.7%, meaning most machines visiting publisher websites are not trying to answer a user's question but hoovering content to build models. GPTBot's share of AI-only traffic surged from 11.9% to 28.1% between July 2024 and July 2025, a 16-percentage-point gain, while Meta-ExternalAgent went from 2.4% to 17.7% in the same window and ByteDance's Bytespider collapsed from 37.3% to 5.8%.

A person shopping for a camera might visit five websites. An AI agent performing the same task could hit thousands, each visit a separate HTTP request generating zero ad impressions, zero subscriptions, and zero intent to purchase, and now those requests constitute the majority of web traffic reaching publisher servers.

Three Architectures Diverge

TIME's blanket markdown conversion is not the only model. According to Digiday, the Economist is experimenting with agent-readable versions limited to content already outside its paywall, specifically marketing copy and B2B sales material, because a subscription publisher must weigh visibility against the risk of devaluing what paying readers buy. A third major news publisher, unnamed, is testing Web Model Context Protocol (WebMCP), a standard co-developed by Google and Microsoft that lets websites share structured data directly with AI agents rather than being scraped at all, and in France, Le Monde is tackling whether an AI agent acts on behalf of a paying subscriber before it can access content.

Each approach represents a distinct bet on how the agent web should work, and none has a proven revenue model for the traffic it is architecting.

Who Captures What

Here is a calculation that has not appeared in any publisher earnings call. TIME's HTML pages average roughly 303,000 bytes, which translates to approximately 75,800 tokens at standard tokenizer rates, while the markdown version runs about 13,000 bytes, or 3,250 tokens, meaning an AI system processing the markdown instead of scraping the HTML saves approximately 72,550 tokens per page visit. At OpenAI's GPT-4o pricing of $2.50 per million input tokens, that amounts to $0.000181 saved per page; at Anthropic's Claude Opus pricing of $15 per million, savings jump to $0.00109 per page.

Scale matters. A publisher receiving 100 million bot requests per month hands AI companies token savings worth $18,000 to $109,000 monthly, depending on which model consumes the content, and the publisher built and maintains the infrastructure that generates those savings while its compensation comes through licensing deals where public figures range from $5 million to $50 million per year across all AI partners, which sounds substantial until you run the cost side: 500 million bot requests per month at a CDN and infrastructure cost of $0.003 to $0.01 per request means $1.5 to $5 million per month serving traffic that generates no ad revenue, while licensing income covers $400,000 to $4 million monthly. For most publishers, serving bots costs more than what they receive for the privilege.

Against Building

Scott Messer, principal at Messer Media and an independent publishing consultant, argues building for agents should be a highly qualified decision. "If you believe there's a value to being discovered by these bots and agents, then you should build them. If you don't believe that, I would ask, why would you build them?" If there is no click, no ad impression, and no payment, an agent-readable site is pure cost with speculative return, and Messer raises the measurement question the Media Rating Council has not answered: can a bot visit count as valid traffic under existing standards? "Did a human see it? Can a bot be influenced by ads? I don't know, probably not."

His argument deserves full weight because it identifies a structural problem, but it collides with a coordination failure that each publisher faces alone: if every competitor builds an agent-readable site and you do not, you vanish from AI-mediated answers entirely, which makes the investment individually rational even though collectively it subsidizes AI companies' inference costs while publishers capture single-digit percentages of the value they create, the classic prisoner's dilemma applied to content economics.

Limitations

Cloudflare's 57.3% figure measures HTTP requests, not time or engagement, and humans still dominate hours spent online. Token calculations use estimated byte counts and standard tokenization rates, not actual TollBit conversion output. Publisher CDN costs vary by infrastructure and caching strategy, and licensing deal terms are mostly undisclosed, so the public figures cited may not represent the broader market.

What You Can Do

If you run a website with content worth crawling, start by auditing your bot traffic composition using Cloudflare Radar or your CDN's analytics dashboard, because knowing what percentage of your requests come from training bots versus search bots versus agentic browsers determines whether markdown conversion saves you money or costs you money. Before building an agent-readable site, calculate your monthly CDN bill for bot traffic against your current or prospective licensing revenue, and if the number is negative, negotiate harder or wait until the market matures. If you are a publisher already in licensing discussions, demand per-query usage data alongside flat fees, because bulk licensing deals cap your upside while hiding the volume of value AI companies extract from your content, a dynamic that Reddit's 10% revenue share with OpenAI and Google demonstrates is negotiable but rarely renegotiated upward once signed.
