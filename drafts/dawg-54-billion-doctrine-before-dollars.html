The Pentagon Wants $54.6 Billion for Autonomous Warfare. The Software to Coordinate the Drones Doesn't Exist Yet.

The Defense Autonomous Warfare Group received $225 million in its first year. The FY2027 budget requests $54.6 billion, a 24,166% increase that exceeds the entire U.S. Marine Corps budget. Ukraine fields 4.5 million drones for $2.6 billion. We compared DAWG's ramp rate to every major military mobilization since World War II. No single program has ever attempted anything like this.

Marcus Chen Infrastructure & Defense Economics June 30, 2026

Twenty-four thousand, one hundred and sixty-six percent. That is the year-over-year funding increase the Pentagon has requested for the Defense Autonomous Warfare Group, from $225 million in fiscal 2026 to $54.6 billion in fiscal 2027. The single line item accounts for roughly 15% of the proposed $350 billion defense reconciliation package. It exceeds the entire $52.8 billion FY2027 budget request for the United States Marine Corps. Former CIA Director David Petraeus called it "the largest single commitment to autonomous warfare in history." He was not exaggerating.

DAWG is eight months old. Its predecessor, the Replicator initiative, promised thousands of attritable autonomous systems by August 2025 and delivered hundreds. The software layer that was supposed to coordinate heterogeneous drone swarms from multiple manufacturers was the "central structural failure" of the program, according to multiple defense analysts. The Pentagon transferred the whole portfolio to a new organization inside U.S. Special Operations Command in November 2025, rebranded it DAWG, put a Marine three-star in charge, and is now asking Congress for 242 times more money.

Nobody ran the math on what $54.6 billion actually buys, or whether the industrial base can absorb it, or how it compares to every previous attempt at this kind of spending ramp. We did.

The Unit-Cost Arithmetic

Ukraine's Ministry of Defence announced in March 2025 that it planned to purchase 4.5 million first-person-view drones that year, allocating $2.6 billion for the buy. That works out to roughly $578 per drone. The previous year, Ukraine purchased 1.5 million FPV drones at comparable unit prices, 96% of them from domestic manufacturers. These are not toys. Ukrainian officials have stated that the majority of Russian troops and vehicles are now hit by drones, and a single Ukrainian operation in June 2025, Operation Spiderweb, deployed 117 FPV drones to damage or destroy dozens of Russian military aircraft on the ground.

At Ukraine's unit price, $54.6 billion purchases 94.5 million drones. That is enough to give every active-duty U.S. service member 71 personal drones and still have several million left over.

The Pentagon is not buying FPV drones with DAWG money, of course. DAWG focuses on larger, longer-range one-way attack platforms. The Switchblade 600, the loitering munition procured under Replicator, has an estimated unit cost in the low-to-mid six figures. At a midpoint estimate of $150,000, $54.6 billion buys 364,000 Switchblades. But the separate Drone Dominance program, running parallel to DAWG, targets procurement of only 200,000 autonomous systems with approximately $1.1 billion across four acquisition phases.

So $1.1 billion buys the drones. Where does the other $53.5 billion go?

The Absorption-Rate Problem

No single defense program element has ramped from $225 million to $54.6 billion in one fiscal year. Not during World War II. Not during Korea. Not during the Cold War peak. The closest historical analogs all took years to reach comparable spending levels and distributed the money across dozens of facilities, contractors, and program offices.

The Manhattan Project spent approximately $2 billion from 1942 to 1945, equivalent to roughly $28 billion in 2026 dollars spread over three years, peaking at about $9.3 billion per year in today's money. The Strategic Defense Initiative reached its peak annual funding of $3.7 billion in 1987, roughly $10 billion in 2026 dollars, before Congress cut it repeatedly. The F-35 Joint Strike Fighter program, the most expensive weapons program in history, took decades of ramp-up to reach peak annual spending of approximately $15 billion, distributed across three military services and eight partner nations.

DAWG is asking for 5.9 times the Manhattan Project's peak annual spending, in year two, for a single organization housed inside a subordinate command.

This raises a practical question that budget analysts have begun asking quietly: can DAWG actually obligate $54.6 billion in a single fiscal year? The organization that employed a few hundred people nine months ago would need to execute contracts, manage oversight, and process payments at a rate exceeding $1 billion per week, every week, for an entire year. The Defense Contract Management Agency, which provides contract administration for the Department of Defense, processed approximately $440 billion in contract actions across the entire department in FY2025. DAWG's request represents roughly 12% of total departmental contracting capacity for a single program office.

The Doctrine-to-Dollar Gap

Every previous elevation of a military capability to unified combatant command status followed years of doctrinal development. U.S. Cyber Command operated as a sub-unified command under Strategic Command from 2009 before its elevation to a full UCC in 2018, giving the Department nine years to develop operational doctrine, training pipelines, and command relationships before the budget matched the ambition. Cyber Command's budget at elevation was approximately $10 billion. U.S. Space Command was reestablished in 2019 after three years of accelerated planning following China's 2016 anti-satellite test and Russia's demonstrated counter-space capabilities. Its initial budget was approximately $15 billion.

DAWG has had zero years of swarm-warfare doctrinal development, and it is requesting $54.6 billion. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth told the House Armed Services Committee on April 29 that the Pentagon would "shortly announce a sub-unified command of autonomous warfare," putting DAWG on a trajectory toward parity with CYBERCOM and SPACECOM. The budget arrived before the command structure. The command structure is arriving before the doctrine. The doctrine is arriving before the software.

That software, the autonomous vehicle orchestrator capable of commanding and coordinating heterogeneous drone swarms from different manufacturers across multiple domains, is the single capability whose absence killed Replicator. DAWG's response to this problem is a $100 million prize challenge, announced in January 2026 jointly by DIU, DAWG, and the Navy. The challenge structure, iterative sprints tackling progressively harder integration problems, implicitly concedes that no existing vendor has a working solution. The Pentagon is requesting 546 times the Orchestrator prize pool for the autonomous systems that the Orchestrator is supposed to coordinate.

The Replicator Report Card

DAWG inherited an organization with a specific track record. Replicator was announced in August 2023 by then-Deputy Secretary Kathleen Hicks with a stated goal of fielding "multiple thousands" of attritable autonomous systems within 18 to 24 months. It received approximately $1 billion in initial funding and was placed under the Defense Innovation Unit. Over 800 firms participated. Roughly 75% were non-traditional defense contractors.

By the August 2025 deadline, the Congressional Research Service documented that only hundreds of systems had been fielded. The Wall Street Journal identified a cascade of technical problems: a BlackSea Technologies unmanned surface vessel that lost steering and went adrift, Anduril aerial systems with launch delays, rudder failures on maritime platforms, misidentification problems and communications vulnerabilities in the Switchblade 600 line, and the persistent inability to command systems from multiple vendors through a common control layer.

The cost trajectory was equally concerning. The Switchblade 600 carries a unit cost in the six figures. Ukraine was procuring comparable-class drones at prices measured in hundreds of dollars. A Royal United Services Institute report in 2023 documented Ukrainian drone loss rates of approximately 10,000 per month, mostly to Russian electronic warfare. The arithmetic of attritable warfare at that loss rate demands unit prices below roughly $10,000. The American defense industrial base had not, as of mid-2025, produced a munition-grade attritable drone meeting that price point at scale.

Replicator's diagnosis was correct: the U.S. needs autonomous systems at scale to deny the Pacific to a Chinese amphibious crossing. Its execution demonstrated that simply issuing a demand signal to American commercial industry does not spontaneously produce Ukrainian-scale drone economics. DAWG inherits the diagnosis and the evidence of failed execution, and proposes to solve the problem with 54 times more money.

What Sits Downstream

The Maven Smart System, the Department's principal targeting and decision-support platform, produces named targets with associated weapon-pairing recommendations at tempos on the order of 1,000 per hour. DAWG is the organizational home for the one-way attack munitions that Maven would feed. The integration of an accelerated targeting pipeline with an accelerated kinetic pipeline is the natural technical trajectory, and it is the trajectory that worries both Congress and international observers.

The existing governance architecture, Department of Defense Directive 3000.09, was designed for individual senior-level review of isolated autonomous lethal systems. It was not designed for a world in which such systems are the standard output of a $54.6 billion annual program. Whether the governance adapts, is adapted, or is waived is the central policy question DAWG will force over the next two fiscal cycles. The January 2026 Secretary of War AI strategy frames safety constraints from commercial AI vendors as "ideological constraints inconsistent with warfighter needs." In February, the Pentagon designated Anthropic a supply-chain risk after the company refused to remove use restrictions on fully autonomous weapons applications. The political direction is legible.

Meanwhile, 166 United Nations member states voted in December 2024 for a resolution expressing concern about an autonomous weapons arms race. A successor resolution in 2025 drew 156 votes. Only Russia, North Korea, and Belarus voted against. A $54.6 billion autonomous-weapons line item in the American defense budget is not a neutral signal to the remaining 150-odd countries that voted alongside the United States' stated allies.

What This Analysis Cannot Prove

We cannot verify the internal allocation of DAWG's $54.6 billion request because the detailed program element breakdowns have not been publicly released. The reconciliation routing, rather than the normal appropriations process, means the budget bypasses the Armed Services Committees' standard authorization review. Our inflation adjustments for historical programs use CPI-U as the deflator; defense-specific inflation indices would produce modestly different ratios but would not change the order-of-magnitude comparisons. The Switchblade 600 unit cost is an estimate based on publicly available reporting; AeroVironment does not disclose exact pricing. Congressional reaction from both Senator Wicker and Representative Rogers has been skeptical of the scale but not opposed in principle to a larger DAWG, suggesting the final appropriation may be significantly less than $54.6 billion. If Congress funds DAWG at $15 to $20 billion rather than $54.6 billion, the absorption-rate problem diminishes substantially, though the doctrine gap remains.

The Strongest Case for DAWG

Ukraine proved that cheap, attritable autonomous systems can hold territory, sink ships, and destroy aircraft on the ground at a fraction of the cost of conventional weapons. Admiral Paparo's "unmanned hellscape" concept, in which thousands of autonomous maritime, aerial, and undersea drones render a Taiwan Strait amphibious crossing impractical, represents the clearest available American theory of victory in the most consequential military scenario of the next decade. If DAWG is the organizational vehicle to make that theory real, then $54.6 billion is not an irrational sum. It is less than the cost of losing a Taiwan contingency. The argument is that Replicator failed because it was underfunded, under-prioritized, and housed in the wrong organization, and that DAWG fixes all three. SOCOM has the acquisition authorities, the risk tolerance, and the operational culture to move at wartime speed. The budget provides the resources. All that remains is execution.

But the argument has a structural flaw: the same budget that demonstrates the seriousness of the commitment also outpaces the organizational capacity to spend it. Funding at 5.9 times the Manhattan Project's peak year, for an organization that is eight months old, that has not yet resolved the software integration problem that killed its predecessor, and that is building doctrine, command structure, industrial base, and capability simultaneously, is not a strategy. It is a bet that the strategy will emerge from the spending, rather than the other way around.

The Bottom Line

If you work in defense acquisition, the question is not whether autonomous warfare is the future. Ukraine settled that. The question is whether a single organization can absorb a 24,166% budget increase in year two while simultaneously building doctrine, software, command structure, and industrial capacity. No organization in American military history has succeeded at that rate of ramp. The Pentagon's own track record, from Replicator to the F-35 to FYDP-era procurement reform, suggests that budget velocity and execution velocity are weakly correlated at best.

If you are a member of the Armed Services Committees: demand the detailed program element breakdown. The reconciliation routing bypasses your normal authorization authority, which is precisely why the largest single-year increase for any defense program in modern history was put there. Ask where the $53.5 billion goes after the drones are bought. Ask what happens to the $100 million Orchestrator prize challenge if no vendor produces a working solution. Ask whether an eight-month-old organization inside SOCOM can process $1 billion per week in contract obligations without the contracting infrastructure, oversight staff, and institutional memory that took CYBERCOM nine years to build.

If you watch defense markets: Saronic Technologies is building an autonomous surface vessel shipyard in Texas after a $1.75 billion Series D at a $9.25 billion valuation. Blue Water Autonomy is constructing Liberty-class autonomous vessels at Conrad Shipyard in Louisiana. Anduril, Palantir, and AeroVironment are positioned to absorb significant portions of the software and munitions budget. The new defense industrial base is being built alongside, not within, the traditional one. Whether $54.6 billion accelerates that transition or simply overwhelms it is the trillion-dollar industrial policy question of the next decade.
