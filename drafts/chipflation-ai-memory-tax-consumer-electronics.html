Memory Prices Spiked 600% in One Year. Apple Raised Every Price Tag. Morgan Stanley's Warning Explains Why It Won't Stop.

Morgan Stanley's 66-page "chipflation" note links AI data center memory demand to consumer price hikes across Apple, Sony, Lenovo, and Microsoft. An original cost breakdown reveals that Apple's $100 MacBook Neo increase maps almost exactly to the memory component delta, and the structural forces behind the surge won't ease before 2028.

Priya Desai · Computing · July 9, 2026 · ☕ 8 min read

$699 is the new starting price of Apple's cheapest laptop, the MacBook Neo, effective June 25, which is $100 more than it cost a month ago. The entry-level iPad climbed from $349 to $449, and the iPad Mini rose to $599 from $499. A streaming box, the Apple TV, went from $129 to $199, which works out to a 54% increase for a device that plays Netflix. Apple blamed "unprecedented demand for memory and storage components" from AI infrastructure. Tim Cook told investors the increases were "likely unavoidable."

Apple is not alone. Sony raised PlayStation 5 prices in Europe and Asia for the second time in two years, Lenovo bumped its ThinkPad lineup 15 to 20 percent, Microsoft's Surface Laptop 13-inch now starts at $1,149 (up $150 from launch), and Meta added $100 to every Quest headset, blaming "the global surge in the price of critical components." In March, the new MacBook Pro arrived up to $400 more expensive than the model it replaced.

Something structural changed, and on June 3, Morgan Stanley gave it a name.

The 66-Page Warning

On June 3, Morgan Stanley published a sixty-six-page research note around a word they coined for the occasion: chipflation. The analysis documents how memory chip prices have spiked six-fold in the past year, driven by manufacturers scrambling to supply Big Tech's AI infrastructure buildout while deprioritizing the chips that end up in phones, laptops, gaming consoles, and streaming boxes, and its central argument is bracingly blunt: what started as a data center supply squeeze has metastasized into a consumer pricing crisis touching every electronics manufacturer on Earth.

"What began as an AI infrastructure bottleneck," the note reads, "is now spreading into hardware margins, device affordability, cloud costs, inflation, and policy." This is not a typical boom-bust memory cycle but rather, in Morgan Stanley's framing, a "durable supply-demand reset," because AI companies have locked up production capacity through long-term agreements that permanently shrink the pool of memory available to laptop makers, phone manufacturers, and everyone else building consumer electronics.

A Calculation Apple Did Not Publish

The MacBook Neo contains 8 gigabytes of LPDDR5 memory and a 256-gigabyte NAND flash storage chip. In mid-2025, contract prices for 8 GB of LPDDR5 ran approximately $15. A 256 GB NAND module cost roughly $18. Total memory and storage bill: about $33 per unit.

At today's prices, with DRAM up roughly six-fold and NAND up about 2.5 times, those same components cost approximately $90 for the DRAM and $45 for the NAND, bringing the new total to $135 and producing a delta of $102.

Apple raised the MacBook Neo's price by exactly $100. That is the whole increase, passed through clean.

That alignment is neither coincidence nor generosity. Apple designs its own silicon and packages DRAM directly onto the processor die using a unified memory architecture, purchasing wafer-level DRAM from Samsung and SK Hynix rather than commodity DIMMs. That structure provides slightly better pricing than what Dell or HP negotiates, but zero immunity to the underlying wafer cost explosion. Apple is passing the entire memory cost increase to consumers.

For Windows laptops, the math gets uglier. TrendForce estimated in March that memory and SSD components, normally 15% of a $900 notebook's bill of materials, now exceed 30%. Combined with a 15% jump in Intel CPU pricing, total component costs rose 45 to 58 percent, meaning manufacturers would need to raise retail prices by up to 40% to preserve margins, turning a $900 laptop into a $1,260 one. Many PC makers have already moved in that direction, and the ones holding the line are watching margins evaporate quarter by quarter.

Where the Money Goes

Three companies control approximately 90% of global DRAM production: Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron. Their stocks tripled this year. SK Hynix posted a 72% operating margin in Q1 2026, with year-over-year profit growth of 405% and a $28 billion US ADR listing that was seven times oversubscribed. These are not companies in a hurry to flood the market with cheap chips.

On the demand side, the concentration is equally intense. Microsoft disclosed that approximately $25 billion of its $190 billion in 2026 capital spending comes from higher chip prices alone. Meta plans $145 billion in AI infrastructure this year. When companies of that scale sign multiyear supply agreements, the memory earmarked for their data centers simply vanishes from the pool that laptop makers can access, fragmenting the market into a well-supplied AI tier that gets first pick of every new wafer and a scarcity-priced consumer tier that competes for whatever remains.

The Demand Destruction Forecast

Research firm IDC now estimates that both the global PC and smartphone markets will contract sharply in 2026 as rising prices push out cost-sensitive buyers, especially in the sub-$500 laptop and sub-$300 phone segments where memory represents the largest share of component cost.

The percentages tell the story. The $100 increase on a $599 MacBook Neo is 16.7%, but on a $349 iPad the same $100 represents 28.7%. On a $129 Apple TV, the $70 hike is 54.3%. Memory is a fixed cost that does not scale down with retail price the way screens and enclosures do, so chipflation hits cheap devices hardest.

This creates a perverse feedback loop in which higher prices suppress unit volumes, lower volumes reduce manufacturers' bargaining leverage with memory suppliers, and weaker leverage drives per-unit pricing even higher on the next purchase order. Morgan Stanley flagged the loop explicitly: "Memory producers benefit from stronger pricing, margins, and visibility. Downstream hardware companies must absorb costs, pass them through, redesign products, or risk demand destruction."

Why 2028 Does Not Fix It

New DRAM fabs cost $15 to $20 billion each and require 18 to 24 months from groundbreaking to volume production. Samsung broke ground on its Taylor, Texas fab in 2024 and it will not reach meaningful output before late 2027, by which point the transition from HBM3e to HBM4 will have doubled per-GPU memory requirements again. New capacity arrives precisely when new AI demand absorbs it, and US-China export controls further fragment supply chains, tightening the market from both ends simultaneously.

Apple's One Real Advantage

Apple has one structural buffer that no other consumer electronics company possesses. Its unified memory architecture means a Mac with 8 GB of RAM performs comparably to a Windows PC with 16 GB, because the memory is shared between CPU and GPU rather than duplicated. By designing the MacBook Neo around 8 GB, Apple halves its exposure to DRAM price swings on its cheapest machine.

That is a genuine moat, but it is a moat built on needing less memory, not on memory being cheap. If DRAM contract prices double again by mid-2027, which Morgan Stanley's model treats as plausible, the MacBook Neo either climbs to $799 or disappears entirely. Former Bloomberg reporter Tim Culpan wrote in his Culpium newsletter that Apple is already considering dropping the 256 GB configuration to stealth-raise the effective starting price without technically changing the sticker on any remaining SKU.

And then there is the line at the bottom of Apple's June 25 announcement: prices for iPhones and AirPods remain unchanged, "at least for now." Four words carrying a lot of forward guidance.
