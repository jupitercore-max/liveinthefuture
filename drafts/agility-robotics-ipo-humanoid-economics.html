Agility Robotics Is Going Public at $2.5 Billion. Its Robots Have Logged 65,000 Hours. We Ran the Payback Math.

Agility's SPAC merger will create the first publicly traded pure-play humanoid robot company in the United States. Digit has worked 65,000 real hours across nine customer sites. At current throughput, a Digit costs roughly $37 per human-equivalent hour of labor. A warehouse worker costs $28. Close, but not there yet.

By Viktor Holm · Robotics · June 25, 2026 · ☕ 10 min read

Sixty-five thousand hours. That is how long Agility Robotics' Digit humanoid robots have spent working in real commercial facilities before the company filed a single SEC document. On June 24, Agility announced a merger with Churchill Capital Corp XI that values the company at $2.5 billion and will send it to a US stock exchange under the ticker AGLT. It becomes the only publicly listed pure-play humanoid robotics company in the country. Figure AI, valued at $39 billion, remains private. Tesla's Optimus is still generating training data inside Tesla factories, not performing productive labor for outside customers. Boston Dynamics doesn't trade separately from its parent Hyundai Motor Group.

Agility stands alone in a strange position: the most commercially proven humanoid company is also the smallest by valuation. Investors can finally buy a direct bet on humanoid robots replacing warehouse labor. Whether that bet pays off depends on numbers that Agility has so far avoided disclosing and that we can reconstruct from what they have revealed.

What $2.5 Billion Buys

Start with the deal itself. Churchill Capital Corp XI, backed by Wall Street dealmaker Michael Klein, contributes approximately $420 million in cash. A common-stock PIPE investment led by existing backer Foxconn adds roughly $200 million at $10 per share. Total gross proceeds exceed $620 million, and Agility says the money will fund fulfillment of existing orders, production scaling for Digit v5, and commercial expansion.

Agility's investor roster reads like a who's-who of AI infrastructure, spanning Nvidia, Amazon, SoftBank Vision Fund 2, Foxconn, DCVC, and industrial partner Schaeffler. Private fundraising totals exceed $641 million, including a $400 million Series C-3 round in June 2025 led by Humanoid Global and Nvidia. That $2.5 billion pre-money valuation represents a 3.9x markup on all prior private capital raised in just over a year.

CEO Peggy Johnson, who previously ran MagicLeap after a long career at Microsoft, framed the listing as a timing play. "We believe Agility will have an advantage by going public before other stand-alone humanoid-robotics businesses, because of pent-up demand from individual investors looking to put dollars into the industry," she told the Wall Street Journal. Retail investors who want pure humanoid exposure currently have one option: buy Tesla and hope Elon Musk's robot timelines are real. Agility offers a second option with robots that are actually deployed.

The 65,000-Hour Track Record

Digit, Agility's flagship bipedal humanoid, stands about 5 feet 8 inches tall, lifts up to 55 pounds, reaches shelves, and navigates around people in warehouse environments, walking on two legs in a design choice that lets it fit into spaces built for human workers without requiring infrastructure modifications. Nine customer sites run Digits today: Schaeffler (auto parts), GXO (logistics), Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada, Mercado Libre (Latin America's largest e-commerce platform), and Amazon, among others.

The 65,000-hour figure is the most important number in the entire IPO prospectus. No other humanoid robot company comes close to that figure. Figure AI ran a celebrated 200-hour continuous autonomous shift. Impressive as a hardware durability milestone, but Agility's cumulative deployment is 325 times longer. Siemens recently published data from its Erlangen electronics factory where the HMND 01 Alpha humanoid achieved 60 tote moves per hour across 8-hour shifts at a 90% success rate. Those are good numbers for a pilot; Agility has been racking up numbers like those for years across multiple customer sites simultaneously.

This operational track record is what separates Agility from every other humanoid startup, not as a demo reel but as an actuarial dataset that generates training data, reveals failure modes, and compresses the learning curve that competitors still have to climb with every hour of continuous deployment. When Johnson says the company has "proven, active commercial deployments," she is describing a moat measured in real-time hours, not press releases.

We Ran the Payback Math

Agility has not disclosed Digit's unit price, and nobody else in the humanoid industry has disclosed theirs with any consistency either, so we built a model from publicly available data.

Inputs:

  • Digit unit cost estimate: $150,000 (midpoint of industry analyst range for comparable commercial humanoids; see Limitations)
  • Warehouse worker fully burdened cost: $28/hour (BLS median hourly wage for material handlers of $19.19, plus the standard 47% benefits loading factor)
  • Robot operating schedule: 16 hours/day, 250 days/year = 4,000 hours annually (dual-shift, conservative for a robot that doesn't need breaks)
  • Robot throughput: 60 tote moves/hour (based on Siemens' published HMND 01 data, a conservative benchmark for Digit which is optimized for logistics)
  • Robot uptime: 90% (matching the Siemens pilot success rate)
  • Human throughput: 200 unit moves/hour (industry average for experienced warehouse material handlers)
  • Useful life: 5 years

At today's numbers, a Digit robot costs 32% more per unit of work than a human warehouse employee, but that gap is not a death sentence so much as a design specification for what the next generation needs to achieve.

Three Paths to Parity

Humanoid labor reaches cost parity with human labor if any one of these conditions is met:

Path 1: Throughput climbs to 113 moves/hour. At 90% uptime, that yields 102 effective moves per hour. Amortized at $10/hour, the cost per move drops to $0.098, beating the human benchmark of $0.14. This is a 1.88x improvement over current performance. Agility's Digit v5, currently in development, incorporates finer dexterity and redesigned manipulation systems. A near-doubling of throughput within one product generation is aggressive but consistent with robotics learning curves in structured environments.

Path 2: Unit cost falls to $80,000. At that price point, amortized capital cost over 5 years drops to $16,000 annually, and total annual cost falls to roughly $26,000, yielding a cost per human-equivalent hour of $24.07. That is 14% cheaper than a human worker even at current throughput levels. Hardware cost reduction of 47% sounds dramatic, but Agility just opened a factory that can produce 10,000 robots per year. RoboFab in Salem, Oregon, is the world's first full-scale humanoid robot manufacturing plant, a 70,000-square-foot facility that began operations in 2024. Scale manufacturing routinely drives 15-20% cost reductions per doubling of cumulative production volume, a pattern observed across automotive, solar panel, and semiconductor production. If Agility reaches 10,000 cumulative units by 2029, $80,000 per unit is reachable.

Path 3: Run three shifts. A robot that operates 6,000 hours per year instead of 4,000 hours amortizes its capital cost 50% faster. Annual cost becomes $45,000, but cost per operating hour drops to $7.50, and even at only 54 effective moves per hour, cost per human-equivalent hour falls to $27.78, which matches parity with a human worker. A warehouse running Digits around the clock, which several of Agility's customers already operate (GXO and Amazon both run 24/7 distribution centers), reaches break-even without any improvement in robot throughput or hardware cost. Time utilization alone closes the gap.

What the $2.5 Billion Valuation Actually Prices

Agility disclosed $300 million in multi-year contracted orders for Digit v5, with 30-plus additional potential customers evaluating large-scale deployments. If those orders are recognized over three years, implied annual revenue is around $100 million, putting the valuation at roughly 25x annual revenue. That is expensive by any conventional metric, but SPAC-era robotics companies have traded at 5x to 15x forward revenue, and Agility is not a conventional company. It is a category creator with no public comparables.

Consider the valuation per deployed hour. Divide $2.5 billion by 65,000 hours and you get $38,462 per hour of proven autonomous work. That sounds absurd until you remember that early-stage technology companies are priced on trajectory, not present-day unit economics. Tesla's 2010 IPO valued the company at $1.7 billion when it had delivered roughly 1,000 Roadsters. On a per-unit basis, the market was paying $1.7 million for each car Tesla had ever shipped. By 2025, Tesla had shipped over 7 million vehicles. Markets price learning curves, not snapshots.

Agility's learning curve has a visible slope. Its 65,000 hours of deployment data represent the largest training corpus of real-world humanoid behavior in the industry. Every cycle through Schaeffler's auto parts bins or GXO's logistics network produces proprietary data that makes the next Digit faster and more reliable. That data flywheel is what the $2.5 billion is buying, a compounding advantage that grows with every tote the robot picks up.

The Strongest Counterargument

Gartner published a forecast in January 2026 projecting that fewer than 100 companies would move humanoid robots beyond early testing by 2028 and fewer than 20 would put them into live production environments. Their research team wrote bluntly: "The technology remains immature and far from meeting expectations for versatility and cost-effectiveness." Gartner flagged battery life limitations, integration difficulty with existing warehouse management systems, and the reality that humanoid robots in high-volume warehouses still cost "several times as much as other robots while delivering lower throughput and uptime."

Gartner's analysis is not wrong. Wheels beat legs for most logistics tasks. A standard autonomous mobile robot (AMR) from companies like Locus Robotics or 6 River Systems costs a fraction of a humanoid, moves faster, and integrates with existing warehouse infrastructure without redesigning a single shelf. If the goal is maximizing tote moves per dollar, bipedal humanoids lose today and will keep losing for years. Agility's bet is that the goal should be maximizing task versatility per dollar, and that legs plus arms plus a human-shaped form factor unlock tasks that wheeled robots cannot attempt: climbing stairs, navigating cluttered environments, manipulating irregularly shaped objects, and operating in spaces designed exclusively for human bodies.

Whether that versatility premium justifies the cost premium is not yet proven at scale. It is the central question that Agility's public market investors will be answering with their capital.

Limitations

Our payback model uses a $150,000 unit cost estimate because Agility has not disclosed Digit's price. Industry estimates for comparable humanoid robots range from $50,000 (Unitree's latest bipedal offerings) to $250,000 or more (custom-configured industrial humanoids). If Digit sells for $100,000, the payback period shortens significantly; at $250,000, it lengthens proportionally. The throughput benchmark of 60 moves per hour comes from Siemens' HMND 01 deployment, not from Agility's own published data. Agility's actual throughput may be higher or lower. Our uptime figure of 90% is an optimistic assumption for an early-stage humanoid; real-world deployments frequently experience lower sustained uptime, especially during initial integration. We also did not model supervision costs in detail; early humanoid deployments typically require one human supervisor per 3-5 robots, which adds approximately $6-9/hour per robot in additional labor cost. Including that figure pushes the cost per human-equivalent hour to approximately $48, widening the gap considerably.

What You Can Do

If you manage a warehouse or distribution center: Do not buy humanoid robots today for cost savings. Buy one for learning. Identify two or three repetitive, ergonomically hazardous tasks (high-shelf picking, heavy tote stacking, long-distance material transport) and pilot a humanoid on a 90-day contract. Measure throughput, uptime, and integration friction, then calculate your own payback model with real numbers. GXO and Schaeffler started this way.

If you invest in robotics: Agility's AGLT ticker will be the first liquid, publicly traded proxy for humanoid robot adoption. Watch three metrics after listing: quarterly orders for Digit v5 (commercial demand signal), cumulative deployed hours (data flywheel growth), and unit cost per robot disclosed in SEC filings (hardware learning curve). The trajectory of those three numbers over four quarters will tell you more about the humanoid robot market than any analyst forecast.

If you work in a warehouse: Humanoid robots are not replacing experienced warehouse workers in 2026 or 2027. Current economics don't support it. What they are doing is filling shifts that can't be filled, particularly third shifts and weekend slots that warehouses struggle to staff. The near-term impact is more about alleviating chronic labor shortages than about eliminating existing positions. That will change as throughput improves, but the timeline is measured in years, not quarters.

The Bottom Line

Agility Robotics is not going public because the economics of humanoid labor have arrived. It is going public because the economics are visible on the horizon, and the company needs capital to reach them. At current performance, a Digit costs 32% more per unit of work than a human warehouse employee. Running the robot three shifts instead of two closes the gap entirely. Doubling throughput, which is within reach for the next-generation Digit v5, flips the economics decisively. The $2.5 billion valuation is a bet that 65,000 hours of real-world deployment data, a factory capable of producing 10,000 robots per year, and $300 million in secured orders constitute enough momentum to cross the payback threshold before the money runs out. In humanoid robotics, Agility has done something none of its competitors have managed: it has put a number on the gap between where robots are and where they need to be. For investors, the question is no longer whether humanoid robots can work. It is whether 32% is a chasm or a rounding error.
