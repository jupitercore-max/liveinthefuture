Tesla Shipped 150 Robots Last Year. It Just Converted the Factory That Made the Model S to Build a Million.

On May 10, 2026, the last Model S rolled off the Fremont assembly line after 14 years. The line now builds Optimus Gen 3 humanoid robots. Tesla's target is one million units per year. Last year, the entire United States shipped 450 humanoid robots. China shipped 18,000.

By Zara Osman May 17, 2026

One hundred and fifty. That is how many Optimus humanoid robots Tesla shipped in all of 2025. The company's target for that year was 10,000 units. It missed by 98.5%.

On May 10, 2026, the last Tesla Model S sedan rolled off the Fremont, California assembly line, ending a 14-year production run that began with the car that made the world believe electric vehicles could be desirable. The Model X followed it into retirement after 11 years. Three hundred and fifty Signature Edition farewell units were produced. Deliveries were postponed, sparking customer backlash.

The lines that built those cars now build humanoid robots. Tesla says those lines will produce one million Optimus units per year. Getting from 150 to one million requires a 6,667-fold increase in production volume. No manufacturer in history has attempted a ramp of that magnitude in a two-year window.

The Math Nobody Ran

Tesla's own history provides the benchmark. The company delivered its first Model S in June 2012 and crossed one million cumulative vehicle deliveries in early 2020, roughly eight years later. Annual production didn't reach one million until sometime in 2022, a full decade after launch.

Consider the EV ramp: 2,650 vehicles in 2012, then 22,477, then 31,655, then 50,580, then 76,230. Each year roughly doubled or tripled the last, but the base was growing. Going from 150 to 1,000,000 is not an S-curve. It is a vertical line.

There is a more generous way to read the numbers. Tesla currently produces roughly 1,000 Optimus units per month at Fremont, up from near-zero in early 2025. If that rate holds, annual output would be 12,000, not one million. To hit one million by the end of 2027, Tesla needs to increase monthly production from 1,000 to roughly 83,000 within 18 months. Even if the lines ramped at 3x per quarter, the math produces 81,000 per month by Q4 2027. Tight but not physically impossible, assuming the supply chain cooperates.

The Production Gap: 40 to 1

Zoom out, and Tesla's bet looks less like hubris and more like desperation. In 2025, the entire United States shipped approximately 450 humanoid robots. Tesla accounted for 150 of those. Figure AI shipped roughly 150 more. Agility Robotics contributed the balance.

China shipped approximately 18,000. Chinese vendors generated $440 million in humanoid robot revenue in 2025, with Unitree and AgiBot each moving between 5,000 and 10,000 units. Put differently: China produced 40 humanoid robots for every one the United States shipped.

In electric vehicles, the United States never fell this far behind. Tesla was the global EV sales leader from 2018 through 2022, and even after BYD overtook it, American EV production remained competitive. In humanoid robots, the race started with the US already in a deep deficit. Chinese manufacturers have a domestic actuator supply chain, lower labor costs for assembly, and government subsidies specifically targeting humanoid robot production, including a projected 94% production growth target for 2025 alone.

The Revenue Substitution

Here is a calculation that Tesla's investor relations has not published. The Model S and Model X lines at Fremont produced an estimated 15,000 to 20,000 vehicles per year in their final years, down from a peak of roughly 50,000 combined. At an average transaction price of approximately $85,000 for the Model S and $95,000 for the Model X, those lines generated somewhere between $1.3 billion and $1.8 billion in annual revenue.

If Tesla hits its one-million-unit Optimus target at the company's stated price range of $20,000 to $30,000 per unit, the same factory floor would generate $20 billion to $30 billion in annual revenue. That is a 14x to 17x increase from the same physical space.

Piper Sandler analyst Alexander Potter values the Optimus business at approximately $100 per Tesla share, arguing that at the current stock price of around $420, investors are effectively getting the entire robot division for free. His base case assigns $400 per share to Tesla's 17 existing product lines. The robot is a free option with, in his view, $15 trillion in total addressable market upside.

Margin is the missing variable. Cars have gross margins that Tesla reports quarterly (hovering around 17-18% in recent periods). Robot margins are unknown because no company has mass-produced humanoid robots at scale. The bill of materials for Optimus Gen 3 includes 50 actuators per hand, a custom AI5 chip, the same 4680 battery cells used in Tesla vehicles, and the full FSD neural network stack. Whether those components can be assembled profitably at $20,000 per unit is an open question that can only be answered after the lines are running.

The Competition Is Not Waiting

Figure AI, the Sunnyvale-based humanoid startup, has built over 350 Figure 03 robots and 9,000 actuators at its BotQ factory. In January 2026, it produced one robot per day. By May, it produces one per hour, a 24x increase in under 120 days. The Helix-02 system completed an autonomous eight-hour warehouse shift without human intervention, handling sorting, packing, and storage tasks.

Germany's Schaeffler, a $14 billion automotive supplier, signed a deal with Humanoid Inc. to deploy 1,000 to 2,000 humanoid robots across its manufacturing sites by 2032, with pilots beginning in 2026-2027. The contract includes a five-year actuator supply agreement, making Schaeffler both a customer and a component supplier. This is the first long-term robot-as-a-service contract between a major industrial manufacturer and a humanoid robot maker.

Norwegian startup 1X offers its NEO robot at $20,000 or $499 per month on a lease, establishing a consumer price point that undercuts Musk's stated $20,000-$30,000 range for Optimus. IDTechEx projects average humanoid robot costs will fall from $114,700 in 2024 to $37,000 by 2030, driven by actuator commoditization and shared AI platforms.

Strongest Counterargument

The strongest case against Tesla's robot bet is Tesla's own track record. Elon Musk promised 10,000 Optimus units in 2025 and delivered 150. He promised Full Self-Driving would be feature-complete by the end of 2020, then 2021, then 2022. The Semi was unveiled in 2017 with deliveries promised for 2019; the first units shipped in late 2022. The Cybertruck was announced in 2019 for late 2021 delivery; production began in late 2023.

A pattern holds: Musk announces a target, misses it by years, and eventually delivers a version of the product. If that pattern holds, one million Optimus units per year could arrive not in 2027 but closer to 2030 or 2031. That timeline would still represent the fastest ramp in manufacturing history, and it would still make the Fremont conversion a rational decision. But it would also give Chinese manufacturers four to five more years of production experience, potentially widening the 40:1 gap before Tesla can close it.

Converting a car line to a robot line is also not a simple retooling. Cars and robots share some supply chain overlap (batteries, wiring harnesses, control boards) but diverge sharply on the components that matter most: actuators, multi-axis joints, and vision/tactile sensor arrays have no automotive analog. Tesla is building the actuator supply chain from scratch while Figure AI and Chinese competitors are sourcing from an existing ecosystem of industrial servo and motor manufacturers.

Limitations

Tesla does not break out Model S and Model X production volumes separately from the Cybertruck in its "Other Models" reporting category, so the 15,000-20,000 annual unit estimate for the S/X lines in their final years is derived from industry trackers and registration data, not Tesla's own filings. The $20,000-$30,000 Optimus price target comes from Musk's public statements and analyst models, not from a published price list. China's 2025 humanoid shipment figures vary by source between 13,000 and 18,000 units depending on whether wheeled humanoids are included in the count. Figure AI's production rate is self-reported and has not been independently audited. Margin projections for Optimus at scale remain entirely speculative, as no humanoid robot has been mass-produced in volumes sufficient to establish real unit economics.

What You Can Do

If you work in manufacturing, logistics, or warehousing, the relevant question is not whether humanoid robots arrive but when and at what cost per hour. Schaeffler's robot-as-a-service contract is the first template for how these deals will be structured. Ask your company's operations team whether they have evaluated pilot programs from Figure, Agility, or Tesla's internal deployment pipeline. Prior LITF analysis calculated the break-even cost at $1.48 per hour for current-generation humanoids against warehouse labor. If your facility runs two shifts, the economics are already close to parity.

If you are an investor, track three metrics: Tesla's quarterly Optimus production disclosures (due in Q2 2026 earnings in July), Figure AI's BotQ output rate (currently one per hour, targeting four per hour by year-end), and China's monthly humanoid export data from Chinese customs. The spread between Tesla's stated targets and actual shipments is the single best indicator of whether the factory conversion is working.

If you are a policymaker in a manufacturing-dependent district, the Fremont conversion is a preview. Tesla's Fremont factory employs over 22,000 people. Model 3 and Model Y lines continue operating, so the S/X conversion affects a subset of those workers, but the strategic direction is clear: the same factory that needed thousands of assembly workers to build cars will eventually need fewer workers to build the robots that replace assembly workers elsewhere. That recursive loop has no precedent in American manufacturing, and workforce transition planning for it has not started in any state.

The Bottom Line

On June 22, 2012, the first Model S rolled off the Fremont line and changed what the world thought an electric car could be. On May 10, 2026, the last one rolled off and was replaced by a bipedal robot with 50 actuators per hand. Tesla is betting that the second event will prove more consequential than the first. Getting from 150 robots to one million requires a ramp 17.7 times steeper than the one Tesla pulled off with cars, attempted in one-fifth of the time, using a supply chain that does not yet exist, against competitors who already ship 40 units for every one the United States produces. Every number in that sentence should make you uncomfortable. The question is whether it makes you uncomfortable enough to pay attention.
