The US Government Bought Equity in 9 Quantum Companies. Their Combined Revenue Wouldn't Cover One F-35 Engine.

The Commerce Department is investing $2.013 billion from the CHIPS and Science Act in quantum computing, taking minority equity stakes in nine companies whose combined annual revenue totals roughly $169 million. At a sector-wide price-to-sales ratio of 334x, these stocks trade at 48 times the NASDAQ's valuation at the peak of the dot-com bubble. An original analysis of the allocation math, the IBM revolving door, and the announcement-day amplifier effect.

Kai Nakamura Compute & Infrastructure July 1, 2026

$169 million. Stop there. That number should hold your attention, because it is the combined annual revenue of the four publicly traded pure-play quantum computing companies in the United States: IonQ ($130M), D-Wave ($24.6M), Rigetti ($7.1M), and Quantinuum (estimated $7M pre-IPO). Their combined market capitalization, as of July 1, 2026, is $56.5 billion, which gives the sector a collective price-to-sales ratio of 334, a number so far removed from historical precedent that comparing it to the dot-com bubble actually undersells the absurdity, because at the peak of that bubble in March 2000, the entire NASDAQ Composite traded at roughly 7x sales. Cisco, the poster child of that era's excess, peaked at 40x. The quantum sector blows past all of them: it occupies a valuation regime that has never existed in the history of American public markets, and the US government just bought in.

On May 21, 2026, the Department of Commerce announced letters of intent to provide $2.013 billion in federal incentives to nine quantum computing companies under the CHIPS and Science Act. In exchange, the government will receive minority, non-controlling equity stakes in each company. This is not a grant or a subsidy. It is the United States government becoming a shareholder in an industry that generates less annual revenue than a single Pratt & Whitney F135 engine costs to manufacture ($27.9 million per unit, per GAO procurement data), multiplied across the portfolio. Think about that.

Two numbers dominate the table, and the first is the one nobody in the quantum hype cycle wants to discuss. First: 68% of the funding ($1.375 billion) goes to two foundry companies, IBM and GlobalFoundries. Alan Gorenstein, a deep-tech analyst, nailed it on LinkedIn: "Everyone is reading the $2B quantum announcement wrong. It is not a bet on qubits. It is a bet on foundries." The seven qubit companies split $638 million. At $38-100 million each, that is meaningful runway extension but not transformative capital for any single company.

IonQ got nothing, despite generating more revenue than the other three public quantum pure-plays combined and holding the title of largest quantum pure-play by market cap until Quantinuum's $1.68 billion IPO three weeks ago, was left out entirely. Barron's reported that Quantinuum received the trapped-ion slot instead. IonQ had already announced its own $100 million facility in Boulder days before the CHIPS announcement.

The Revolving Door Problem

IBM received $1 billion of the $2 billion total — half. Half the entire quantum portfolio, directed to one company, to build a subsidiary called Anderon that will operate as a "state-of-the-art 300-millimeter quantum wafer foundry" in Albany, New York, a facility that IBM will match with a billion of its own cash plus intellectual property and personnel transfers, creating a $2 billion venture in a market where the entire sector's combined annual revenue is smaller than the amount IBM will spend furnishing the lobby.

Darío Gil oversees the Department of Energy's role in quantum strategy, and before joining the DoE as Under Secretary for Science in September 2025, he spent 22 years at IBM, rising to Senior Vice President and Director of Research, the exact division whose commercial successor is now receiving the largest share of the program he influences. He was, by his own description in a public letter, "strongly associated with the creation of the quantum computing industry" at IBM. Under his leadership, IBM built the first programmable quantum computers available through the cloud.

Rep. Zoe Lofgren (D-CA) raised the conflict directly: Gil "was involved in discussions surrounding the awards while IBM emerged as the largest beneficiary." She also challenged the legal basis for the program, arguing that Section 102 of the CHIPS Act authorized microelectronics research, not quantum manufacturing incentives or government equity stakes. "The Congressional record is quite clear that Congress did not want the government taking equity stakes in companies using these funds," Lofgren said.

A straightforward counterargument deserves its full weight, because it is not wrong. IBM's quantum program is genuinely among the most advanced on Earth: the company has demonstrated processors with 1,121 qubits (Condor, 2023) and 133-qubit error-corrected systems (Heron), and its roadmap to a fault-tolerant quantum computer by 2029 is the most detailed public timeline from any major player in the field. A foundry capable of fabricating quantum wafers at 300mm scale would serve the entire US quantum industry, not just IBM, and the foundry model (open manufacturing infrastructure that benefits competitors and collaborators alike) is exactly the kind of pre-competitive investment that government does well. Gil is qualified because he built the thing the government is trying to fund. But the optics of one man's former employer receiving 50% of a program he helped shape demand, at minimum, the kind of formal recusal disclosure that no public record yet shows.

Rigetti trades at 901 times its annual revenue, a number that would have gotten a dot-com-era analyst laughed out of any conference room on Sand Hill Road. It generated $7.1 million in sales last year, down from $10.8 million the year before, while losing $216 million in the process. D-Wave's Q1 2026 revenue was $2.9 million on a $18.4 million loss; its saving grace is a record $33.4 million in bookings, but $20 million of that came from a single university system sale, the kind of lumpy, non-recurring deal that flatters a quarterly headline while revealing almost nothing about demand trajectory. None of this constitutes a sustainable business model. Not yet, anyway — these are bets on a future that may arrive in three years or thirty, and the market has priced them as though the answer is three.

Combined market cap of the four public quantum pure-plays: $56.5 billion. Combined annual revenue is roughly $169 million, a figure that would not cover the $178 million the Department of Defense pays for a single F-35A fighter jet, delivery included, and is less than the annual budget of the National Zoo. Absorb that.

In 2025 alone, these same companies collectively diluted shareholders by issuing $4.15 billion in new stock and warrants: IonQ raised $2 billion, Quantum Computing Inc. raised $1.25 billion, D-Wave raised $550 million, and Rigetti raised $350 million. At this stage, these companies' primary product is equity, not computation, and Washington has now officially joined that customer base, checkbook open, asking no questions about when the product will ship.

The Announcement-Day Amplifier

Something remarkable happened on May 21, the day Commerce announced the awards: D-Wave surged 30%, Rigetti surged 30%, Infleqtion jumped 24%, and IBM climbed 12%. Three of the smaller quantum pure-plays, D-Wave, Rigetti, and Infleqtion, collectively received $300 million in proposed awards. Their combined market capitalization increased by approximately $5 billion in a single trading session.

That is $16.70 in market value created for every $1 the government committed to spend. Remarkable? Absurd? Both.

For comparison, SoftBank's Vision Fund was widely criticized for paying 5-10x premiums on speculative tech investments during 2019-2021. Quantum stocks responded 1.7x more generously than SoftBank at its most aggressive. Commerce's announcement functioned less like an investment and more like an IV drip of institutional legitimacy into companies whose share prices are primarily driven by narrative, not revenue.

Five Modalities Walk Into a Bar

CHIPS quantum money hedges across five distinct hardware approaches to building a quantum computer: superconducting circuits (IBM, D-Wave, Rigetti), trapped ions (Quantinuum), neutral atoms (Atom Computing, Infleqtion), photonics (PsiQuantum), and silicon spin (Diraq). History strongly suggests that one or two modalities will ultimately dominate commercial quantum computing, much as CMOS silicon won the semiconductor wars of the 1980s while gallium arsenide, germanium, and silicon-on-sapphire faded into niches. Washington is betting on all five horses simultaneously, ensuring that at least some of its $638 million in qubit-company investments will fund architectures that end up as footnotes.

A portfolio approach has a reasonable defense: nobody knows which modality will achieve fault tolerance first, so diversification across all of them minimizes the risk of missing the winner. This is what the CHIPS R&D Office's Bill Frauenhofer means when he talks about addressing "discrete technological problems of genuine consequence" across "multiple quantum modalities at once." Nobody knows which approach wins, and that honest admission is the implicit text of every allocation in the portfolio.

What the Government Is Really Buying

Strip away the hype and the conflict-of-interest questions, and something interesting remains. This $2 billion quantum CHIPS allocation is not, primarily, a bet on any of these companies' stock prices. It is a bet on the same thesis that drove the Human Genome Project and DARPA's ARPANET: that early government investment in pre-commercial fundamental technology generates outsized returns on time horizons that no private investor would tolerate.

History supports the thesis, and the numbers are striking. HGP cost $3.8 billion over 13 years (1990-2003). A 2013 study by the Battelle Memorial Institute, commissioned by NHGRI, found it generated $796 billion in economic activity, a 209:1 return. DARPA's internet investments, approximately $4.5 billion in inflation-adjusted dollars, spawned an economy measured in trillions. Even the Manhattan Project, at roughly $28 billion (inflation-adjusted), created a nuclear energy industry worth over $1 trillion cumulatively.

At the Human Genome Project's 209:1 ratio, $2 billion in quantum investment would produce $418 billion in economic value. IBM estimates quantum computing will generate $850 billion in economic value by 2040. If even a quarter of IBM's projection materializes and the government's equity stakes track the sector proportionally, the taxpayer return could be significant. At 10:1, a modest showing by historical government-bet standards, the return is still $20 billion.

But the comparison has limits that matter more than the parallels. HGP funded basic science at universities and national labs, not equity stakes in publicly traded companies. DARPA funded research that created entirely new industries, not $100 million positions in companies that already trade at 334x sales. Commerce's equity stakes in quantum companies are closer in structure to the $8.9 billion CHIPS Act investment in Intel, where Commerce took a 9.9% stake, than to the open-ended research grants that historically produced triple-digit ROI multipliers. That structure optimizes for financial returns, not scientific breakthroughs, and history's biggest government tech wins came from optimizing for the opposite.

The Strongest Case Against

Most powerfully, the objection is not that quantum computing is overhyped, because the physics is real and the engineering progress over the past three years has been genuine. Quantinuum's Helios system achieved 99.92% two-qubit gate fidelity. Google's Willow chip demonstrated below-threshold error correction, and IBM's roadmap is on schedule. Every machine works, and the engineering momentum is genuine.

It is the financial structure that breaks down. Commerce is buying equity in companies at valuations that already price in success. If quantum computing reaches commercial viability by 2030 and these companies' stock prices triple, the government earns a modest return on paper. If quantum computing reaches viability and these companies' stock prices do not triple, because the value was already priced in at 334x sales, the government's equity stakes produce zero incremental return. Only one scenario lets the equity structure clearly outperform a traditional research grant: quantum reaches viability AND these specific companies' valuations continue to climb from already-historic levels. That requires both the technology thesis and the equity thesis to be simultaneously correct, which is a compound bet that the government has no particular edge in making.

What You Can Do

If you are a retail investor holding quantum computing stocks, understand what you own. These are not technology companies in the traditional sense. They are call options on a physical phenomenon that may or may not produce commercially viable products within this decade. A 334x P/S ratio means the market has already priced in a version of success that most of these companies will never deliver. CHIPS funding de-risks the companies' immediate survival but does nothing to de-risk their long-term commercial viability. Size your position to what you can afford to lose entirely.

If you work in quantum computing, CHIPS funding is unambiguously good news for the field's near-term employment prospects. But track which modality your employer pursues. A five-way bet will narrow, and companies built on the losing architecture will not survive the consolidation regardless of government funding. Understand your employer's moat in the context of the specific engineering problem the CHIPS award targets, and whether solving that problem leads to a commercially defensible position.

If you follow federal technology policy, the Darío Gil question deserves scrutiny. Formal recusal standards for appointed officials under 18 U.S.C. § 208 require disqualification from matters that could benefit a former employer. Gil's involvement in quantum policy discussions while IBM received 50% of the quantum CHIPS allocation is at minimum a fact pattern that warrants investigation, regardless of whether the selection was technically meritorious.

Limitations

This analysis relies on publicly disclosed revenue figures and market capitalizations as of June 30-July 1, 2026. Quantinuum's pre-IPO revenue is estimated from limited Honeywell disclosures and may understate actual sales. Three of the nine CHIPS recipients (Atom Computing, PsiQuantum, and Diraq) are private companies with no public financial data, making full-portfolio analysis impossible. Implied equity stake percentages are calculated from current market caps and award amounts; the actual negotiated terms remain confidential. China's quantum spending figure ($10 billion) originates from a 2022 BCG estimate and may be significantly understated given limited transparency into PRC research budgets. Finally, the letters of intent are not finalized agreements and remain contingent on milestone-based disbursement, so the actual flow of funds may differ from announced allocations.

The Bottom Line

The United States government is now a shareholder in nine quantum computing companies through a $2 billion program of uncertain legal provenance, overseen in part by a former executive of the program's largest beneficiary, investing in an industry that trades at 48 times the dot-com bubble's valuation peak. On paper, it looks reckless, but the government's track record on pre-commercial technology bets is 3-for-3 on the ones that mattered most, and the $2 billion price tag is modest by the standard of those precedents. What matters is not whether quantum computing will work; it is whether buying equity in companies at 334x sales is the right structure for a bet that only pays off on a 15-year timeline, a question that the architects of this program have conspicuously declined to answer. HGP did not need to buy stock in Celera Genomics to decode the genome, and DARPA did not take a board seat at Netscape. History's best government technology investments were structured as research, not as trades. This one is structured as a trade, and the exit strategy remains conspicuously undefined.
