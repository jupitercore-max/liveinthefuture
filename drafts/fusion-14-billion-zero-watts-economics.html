Fusion Energy Has Raised $14.2 Billion. It Has Produced Zero Commercial Watts. We Ran the $/Watt Math.

Commonwealth Fusion Systems announced $1 billion in new funding on July 30, bringing its total to $4 billion and the global fusion industry to $14.2 billion raised since 2021. We calculated the cost per projected watt across every major fusion startup, compared it to proven energy technologies, and ran the numbers on whether fusion can arrive in time for AI's power crisis. The short answer: it cannot.

By Viktor Holm · Physics & Energy · July 30, 2026 · ☕ 10 min read

July 30, 2026

Four billion dollars. That is how much Commonwealth Fusion Systems has now raised, after announcing a $1 billion equity round on Wednesday. The money came from pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and industrial companies whose names CFS declined to share. The MIT spinout has absorbed 28% of every private dollar the global fusion industry has attracted since 2021, hired the former CFO of Moderna, and signed power purchase agreements with Google and Eni worth well over $1 billion. Its SPARC demonstration reactor in Devens, Massachusetts, is roughly 75% complete. Its commercial ARC plant in Virginia is planned to deliver 400 megawatts to the grid in the early 2030s.

CFS is not alone. Helion raised $465 million in June at a $15.5 billion valuation, nearly tripling its worth in 18 months. TAE Technologies merged with Trump Media in a $6 billion deal. Pacific Fusion materialized with a $900 million Series A. The industry total: $14.2 billion in private capital since 2021, according to Reuters. The money is real. The megawatts are not.

Nobody has systematically calculated what all this investment buys per watt of planned commercial capacity. So we did.

The Vogtle comparison is instructive. Plant Vogtle's Units 3 and 4 were originally budgeted at $14 billion for 2,234 MW of AP1000 capacity: $6.27 per watt. The final cost was $36.8 billion, a 163% overrun that ballooned the cost to $16.47 per watt. Construction began in 2013. Unit 3 reached commercial operation in July 2023. Unit 4 followed in April 2024. Eleven years, start to finish, for a design that had already been licensed and built elsewhere.

CFS's $10 per projected watt is cheaper than Vogtle's actual $16.47. Numerically. But Vogtle's watts generate 33,871 GWh of electricity per year at a 91% capacity factor. CFS's watts generate nothing, because they do not exist. The ARC plant has not broken ground. The SPARC demonstration reactor has not achieved first plasma. Five peer-reviewed papers published in the Journal of Plasma Physics in June, co-authored by 58 scientists from MIT, Columbia, and the Max Planck Institute, provide theoretical confidence that ARC will work. Theoretical confidence is not a grid connection.

The Construction Cost Nobody's Talking About

Here is the number that should worry fusion investors: CFS CEO Bob Mumgaard said the new $1 billion will not go toward SPARC. It is earmarked for ARC. But $1 billion will not build a 400 MW first-of-kind fusion power plant. Vogtle's two AP1000 fission reactors cost $36.8 billion, and those were a proven design with an existing supply chain and regulatory pathway. ARC is none of those things.

CFS has not publicly disclosed ARC's estimated construction cost. But consider the inputs. The reactor requires superconducting magnets made from VIPER high-temperature superconducting tape, a material CFS itself developed. The tokamak vessel must withstand continuous deuterium-tritium plasma at temperatures exceeding 100 million degrees. Tritium breeding blankets, first-wall materials capable of surviving constant neutron bombardment, cryogenic cooling systems, remote-handling robotics for the radioactive interior, and a turbine hall all need to be designed, tested, and built for the first time at commercial scale.

If ARC tracks the Vogtle pattern of first-of-kind nuclear construction, where final costs ran 163% over budget, the total capital requirement could reach $15 to $25 billion. CFS has $4 billion. The gap is large, and it will need to be closed before ARC delivers its first electron.

The AI Power Math: 17 Fusion Plants Nobody Can Build

Fusion's pitch to data center operators is that it provides clean, firm, baseload power without carbon emissions or long-lived nuclear waste. Google has already signed for 200 MW from ARC. Microsoft has a power purchase agreement with Helion for delivery by 2028. These deals are premised on the idea that AI's power appetite is so enormous that fusion will find a ready market the moment it works.

The appetite is real. The International Energy Agency projects that global data center electricity demand will reach 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, up from 415 TWh in 2024. That is a 530 TWh increase, equivalent to adding Japan's entire electricity consumption. In the United States, data center demand could hit 325 to 580 TWh by 2028, according to a Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study cited by the Brookings Institution. Nearly half of all US electricity demand growth by 2030 will come from data centers.

So: can fusion fill any meaningful fraction of that gap?

We calculated ARC's annual output at full capacity. A 400 MW plant running at a 90% capacity factor (optimistic for a first-of-kind reactor, but consistent with mature nuclear fission performance) would produce 3.15 TWh per year. That covers 0.59% of the projected 530 TWh incremental data center demand. Less than one percent.

To supply 10% of that incremental demand, you would need roughly 17 ARC-scale plants operating simultaneously. At $15 to $25 billion each, that is $255 to $425 billion in construction capital alone. The entire global fusion industry has raised $14.2 billion. The construction cost for meaningful data center relevance exceeds the industry's lifetime fundraising by a factor of 18 to 30.

Helion's math is worse. Orion, its first plant under construction in Malaga, Washington, targets 50 MW. At an 85% capacity factor, that generates 0.37 TWh per year. To cover 10% of incremental data center demand, Helion would need 143 Orion-scale plants. The company has raised $1.5 billion.

The timeline compounds the problem. ARC will not deliver power until the early 2030s at the earliest. Building 17 plants after that would require a construction cadence faster than any nuclear program in history, including China's, which took roughly three years per reactor using standardized Hualong One designs and a mature domestic supply chain. A reasonable estimate for 17 first-generation fusion plants: the 2040s, optimistically. Data centers need the power now.

The Counterargument Fusion Bulls Deserve

Comparing fusion's $/projected-watt to solar's $/installed-watt is inherently unfair, and fusion investors know it. The strongest version of their case goes like this: solar photovoltaic panels cost $76 per watt in 1977. Today, the installed cost is around $1 per watt, a 98.7% decline driven by manufacturing scale, learning curves, and decades of deployment. Fusion is where solar was 50 years ago. The capital being raised today is R&D spending, not construction budgets, and it would be just as misleading to calculate Bell Labs' R&D costs per transistor in 1948 as it is to calculate CFS's R&D costs per projected watt in 2026.

This argument has real force. Fusion, if it works, offers something no other clean energy source provides: firm, dispatchable, baseload power with effectively unlimited fuel. Deuterium is extracted from seawater. Lithium for tritium breeding is abundant. There is no intermittency problem, no seasonal variation, no geographic constraint. A working fusion grid would be the most transformative energy development since fossil fuels.

The CFS peer-reviewed papers are significant precisely because they move the conversation from "can it work?" toward "can it be built?" Brandon Sorbom, CFS co-founder and chief science officer, told Reuters the papers provide "global agreement" that ARC can "continuously deliver 400 megawatts of clean firm base load net energy electricity to the grid." That is a statement about physics. The harder question is about engineering, procurement, construction timelines, and cost. Every nuclear technology in history has answered the physics question before slamming into the engineering one.

What This Analysis Did Not Prove

Our $/projected-watt metric has meaningful limitations. First, it conflates R&D capital with construction capital. A significant fraction of CFS's $4 billion went to developing VIPER superconducting magnets, building SPARC, and advancing plasma physics. Only a portion will fund ARC construction. A fair cost-per-watt calculation would separate these, but CFS does not disclose the breakdown.

Second, we assumed capacity factors of 85-90% for fusion reactors based on mature fission performance. No commercial fusion reactor has ever operated. Actual capacity factors could be substantially lower during the first decade of operation, which would increase the effective cost per delivered MWh.

Third, the Vogtle cost analogy may overstate fusion construction costs. If CFS achieves modular manufacturing of its tokamak components rather than Vogtle-style one-off construction, costs could fall dramatically on units two through ten. CFS itself has gestured at this: Mumgaard has said ARC "will be the first of many that's backed by a supply chain primed for scale." We have no data to evaluate that claim.

Fourth, the Trump Media/TAE merger complicates dollar comparisons. The $6 billion deal value is largely in stock, not deployable capital for reactor construction. We used TAE's $1.79 billion in direct investment, which underestimates total resources available but reflects actual capital deployed.

Finally, fusion's long-term cost trajectory is unknowable at this stage. If the technology works and scales, 2050 costs could look nothing like 2026 costs. Our analysis describes the current state, not the asymptotic one.

The Bottom Line

Fusion energy has attracted $14.2 billion in private capital. It has produced zero commercial watts. The best-funded company on Earth, CFS, is spending $10 per projected watt on capacity that will not exist for at least five years and probably needs another $10 to $20 billion to build. The AI power crisis requires hundreds of terawatt-hours of new generation by 2030. Fusion, under even the most optimistic scenarios, will supply less than 1% of that demand by the time it arrives.

None of this means fusion is a bad investment. It means fusion is a generational bet, not a near-term energy solution. The physics may well be right. The five CFS papers and Helion's 150-million-degree plasma tests suggest it is. But the gap between "the physics works" and "electrons reach the grid at competitive cost" has swallowed nuclear projects before. Vogtle proved that even a licensed, understood, previously-built reactor design can triple in cost and take a decade longer than planned.

What You Can Do

If you are a utility or data center operator shopping for clean firm power by 2030, buy nuclear fission. The four US microreactors that achieved criticality this summer prove the technology is accelerating. NuScale, X-energy, and Kairos Power all have NRC-engaged designs. Fusion will not help you before 2035 at the absolute earliest.

If you are an investor, understand what you are buying. Fusion equity is a call option on a post-2035 energy transition, not a claim on near-term cash flows. CFS is the furthest along, with the most credible physics validation, the strongest power purchase agreements, and the former Moderna CFO preparing what may be an eventual IPO. But the ARC construction funding gap is the critical variable. Watch for the next capital raise, which will need to be substantially larger than $1 billion.

If you are a policymaker, stop pretending fusion is relevant to the 2030 climate target. It is not. The DOE's Reactor Pilot Program and the fusion industry's $14.2 billion in private capital represent important long-term bets. But every dollar framed as a near-term climate solution is a dollar of political attention diverted from technologies that work today: fission, solar, wind, storage, and demand response. The honest framing is 2040. Use it.
