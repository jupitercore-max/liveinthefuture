# Research: The Cognition-Function Gap in Alzheimer's Tau Therapy

## Story Thesis
Diranersen (BIIB080) is the first drug to successfully reduce tau tangles in Alzheimer's patients and show cognitive benefit. But the CELIA trial data revealed something nobody expected: on pure cognition tests, it outperformed amyloid drugs (42-50% slowing vs. ~26-27%), yet on functional measures it showed no benefit at all. This cognition-function gap suggests tau and amyloid attack different neural circuits — and makes the case for combination therapy more compelling than either approach alone.

## Kill Test: Original Analysis
1. **Cross-measure efficacy comparison** (nobody has done this across all cognitive subscales for all 3 drugs)
2. **Cognition-function gap calculation** — diranersen's ADAS-Cog13 42% vs. CDR-SB 26% vs. ADCS-ADL-MCI 0%. The gap ratio is a novel metric.
3. **Safety profile inversion** — ARIA rates vs. confusion rates, fundamentally different risk profiles
4. **Administration burden math** — annual procedure hours per patient across all 3 drugs
5. **Combination therapy cost projection** — first estimate of what dual tau+amyloid costs

## The Original Calculations

### 1. Cognition-Function Gap Ratio
Diranersen at 60mg:
- Pure cognition (ADAS-Cog13): 42% slowing
- Global cognition+function (CDR-SB): 26% slowing  
- Pure function (ADCS-ADL-MCI): 0% slowing
- Gap ratio: 42% / 0% = undefined (infinite gap)

Amyloid drugs (approximate):
- Leqembi: CDR-SB 27%, roughly balanced across cognition/function
- Kisunla: CDR-SB 29-36%, roughly balanced

This is the key finding: tau drugs preserve THINKING but not DOING. Amyloid drugs preserve both roughly equally.

## Limitations
- CELIA is Phase 2 (n=416) vs. Phase 3 trials (n=1,700+) for comparators — smaller trial, wider confidence intervals
- Cross-trial comparisons are notoriously unreliable (different patient populations, entry criteria, placebo rates)
- The dose-response paradox is unexplained — could be biological or statistical artifact
- Pricing for diranersen is entirely unknown
- 18-month follow-up is short for Alzheimer's
