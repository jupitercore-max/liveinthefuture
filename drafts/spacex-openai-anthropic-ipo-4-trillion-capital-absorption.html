Three Companies Worth $3.7 Trillion Just Filed for U.S. IPOs. SpaceX Wants $75 Billion on Friday Alone.

Between June 1 and June 12, Anthropic, OpenAI, and SpaceX each filed paperwork to go public, with a combined valuation of $3.7 trillion. SpaceX’s $75 billion raise is the largest IPO in history by a factor of three. We calculated what happens when three companies try to absorb more capital in one quarter than the entire U.S. IPO market has ever produced in a year.

Tomás Reyes · AI Infrastructure · June 9, 2026

Seventy-five billion dollars on a single Friday.

That is what SpaceX plans to raise when it begins trading on Nasdaq this week under the ticker SPCX, at $135 per share, at an implied valuation of $1.77 trillion. The offering is nearly triple Saudi Aramco’s $25.6 billion record from 2019, a gap so large it redefines the category rather than merely topping the leaderboard. MSCI confirmed Monday that SpaceX clears the thresholds for early inclusion in its Global Standard Indexes, meaning passive funds tracking $5.79 trillion in assets will eventually be compelled to buy shares of a company that lost $4.94 billion last year.

SpaceX is not the only company testing the public market’s appetite this month, and the queue behind it makes the scale problem exponentially worse.

On June 1, Anthropic confidentially submitted its S-1 to the SEC, following a $65 billion funding round that valued the company at $965 billion. It is reportedly close to its first quarterly profit. One week later, on June 8, OpenAI announced its own confidential S-1 filing, targeting a valuation of up to $1 trillion. “We expect it to leak so we’re just announcing it,” the company said on X, adding that it had “not decided on timing yet.”

In 11 days, three companies worth a combined $3.73 trillion submitted paperwork to enter the U.S. public equity market, a concentration of private-to-public capital transfer with no precedent in the modern era of finance.

SpaceX by the Numbers

SpaceX reported $18.7 billion in 2025 revenue, a 33% year-over-year increase, but posted a net loss of $4.94 billion for the year and $4.27 billion in the first quarter of 2026 alone. Capex hit $20.7 billion, exceeding total revenue for the fiscal year. At $1.77 trillion, the implied multiple is roughly 95× trailing revenue, a figure that places SpaceX in the company of the most aggressively valued IPOs in market history and raises the question of what exactly passive index funds are being asked to own.

Only about 7% of shares will float at launch, creating a supply constraint that could amplify early volatility. S&P Global declined to change its profitability requirement, shutting SpaceX out of the S&P 500 and creating a $1.77 trillion public company that sits outside the benchmark most retirement accounts track. Morningstar has estimated fair value at roughly $780 billion, less than half the IPO price, while up to 30% of shares may be reserved for individual investors and Elon Musk faces a 366-day lock-up on his holdings.

What OpenAI and Anthropic Signal

Neither OpenAI nor Anthropic has disclosed a raise amount or a listing date. OpenAI, which generated $2 billion in monthly revenue as of March with 900 million weekly active users and 50 million paying subscribers, cautioned that a public offering “may be a while,” though earlier Wall Street Journal reporting placed a potential listing as early as September, with Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs advising on the process.

Anthropic has revealed fewer details about its timeline. Its $965 billion valuation has since risen to roughly $1 trillion on the Forge secondary market, propelled by demand for Claude Code. If both companies eventually raise capital at scales proportional to their valuations, the combined demand from all three offerings could reach $155 billion to $235 billion, a range that would swallow the best full-year IPO market the United States has ever produced.

SpaceX’s confirmed $75 billion raise exceeds the combined U.S. IPO proceeds for 2022, 2023, and 2024: $58.1 billion across roughly 330 offerings over 36 months. One company, one day, more capital than three entire calendar years of public listings produced collectively.

If all three companies eventually raise at the midpoint of our estimated range ($195 billion), that figure exceeds the 2021 all-time record ($142.4 billion) by 37%, accomplished not by 397 companies over 12 months but by three companies in a matter of weeks, each of which would rank among the largest technology offerings ever priced.

33,000 Companies in the Queue

Capital markets have a waiting room, and it is dangerously overcrowded. An estimated 33,000 private equity-backed companies are sitting in an exit backlog that has accumulated since the 2022 rate shock froze the IPO window. That window reopened in 2025 and 2026 is running at a 163.7% pace over the prior year, with 68 IPOs raising $34.2 billion before any of these three companies entered the picture.

But institutional allocators operate with finite capital budgets, and the displacement arithmetic is unforgiving. A pension fund that commits $500 million to the SpaceX offering has $500 million less for a Series D follow-on or a mid-cap tech debut. BCA Research has warned that the convergence “may dampen forward market returns and mute further multiple expansion,” and Barron’s described the emerging dynamic as an “impending chokepoint” for the broader listing pipeline.

For those 33,000 companies, the question is not whether the IPO window is open. It is whether three companies are blocking the door.

Strongest Counterargument

Marquee IPOs do not necessarily drain capital; they can expand it. Saudi Aramco’s $25.6 billion raise attracted sovereign and institutional pools that would not have entered equity markets otherwise. During the 2021 SPAC boom, record IPO activity coincided with record venture funding and all-time highs in public equity valuations, suggesting that large listings create their own gravitational pull, drawing in capital that would otherwise sit in fixed income or cash equivalents. High-profile listings generate media coverage, retail enthusiasm, and fresh allocations that grow total market liquidity rather than carving existing liquidity into smaller slices.

There is real evidence for this today: the 2026 IPO market is already running 163.7% ahead of 2025, and the three filings themselves may be generating the momentum that lifts the entire market. If SpaceX trades well on Friday, Anthropic and OpenAI benefit from the sentiment.

Limitations

Our capital demand estimates for OpenAI and Anthropic are projections, not disclosed figures. Both companies filed confidentially and have not announced raise amounts or listing dates. OpenAI has explicitly stated timing is undecided. Historical U.S. IPO totals are sourced from Renaissance Capital and may vary by inclusion criteria for SPACs and direct listings. Per-year IPO counts for 2022–2024 are approximations. SpaceX data is from confirmed S-1 filings and regulatory documents. If OpenAI and Anthropic delay listings into 2027, the single-quarter absorption thesis does not apply, though the annual absorption problem persists.

The Bottom Line

One company is raising more capital on Friday than the entire U.S. IPO market produced in three consecutive years. Two more filed the same month. Whether these offerings land weeks apart or months apart, the aggregate demand for public capital from AI companies alone now exceeds any precedent in market history.

If you manage institutional capital: Model for allocation displacement. Every dollar committed to these three companies is a dollar unavailable for the 33,000 PE-backed companies waiting in line. Spillover effects on Series C/D pricing power should emerge within 90 days of the SpaceX listing.

If you are considering the SpaceX IPO as a retail investor: Understand what 95× trailing revenue and a 7% float mean. Morningstar values the company at less than half the IPO price. Historical megacap IPOs at comparable revenue multiples have delivered negative returns in their first 18 months. Wait for the lock-up expiration before assuming the price reflects stable supply and demand.

If you work at or sell to a PE-backed company waiting to go public: Your exit window may not function as advertised this year. Three companies are absorbing the oxygen, and the practical effect for everyone behind them in line is that the IPO market may look open from a distance while being functionally congested up close. Consider whether secondary-market transactions or structured sales offer a faster path to liquidity than waiting in a queue that just got 37% longer.
