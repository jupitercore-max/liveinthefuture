Earth's Orbit Had 164 Days of Collision Margin. Now It Has 5.5.

Astrophysicists created a countdown clock for orbital catastrophe. In 2018, the buffer between routine satellite operations and the first catastrophic collision was 164 days. By mid-2025, it had collapsed to 5.5 days. Starlink alone performs one collision avoidance maneuver every 1.8 minutes. An original trend extrapolation shows the sub-1-day threshold could arrive before this decade ends.

Anya Volkov · Space · May 18, 2026 · ☕ 11 min read

Every 1.8 minutes.

That is how often a Starlink satellite fires its thrusters to dodge something, according to a University of British Columbia analysis of SpaceX's own FCC filings, which report that each of the constellation's 9,000-plus satellites performs an average of 44 collision avoidance maneuvers per year. Spread across the entire fleet, that works out to roughly one evasive action every 108 seconds, around the clock, every single day of the year, a ceaseless orbital dance where a single missed step could scatter wreckage across a shell of space that GPS satellites, weather instruments, the International Space Station, and 3.56 billion daily users of Meta's apps depend on for communication and navigation.

Astrophysicist Sarah Thiele wanted to answer a simple and terrifying question: what happens when the dodging stops?

The CRASH Clock

In their paper "An Orbital House of Cards," Thiele and her co-author Aaron Boley introduced a metric they call the CRASH Clock, short for Collision Realization and Significant Harm. It measures the expected time between a system-wide loss of satellite maneuvering capability and the first catastrophic collision in low Earth orbit. Think of it as a stress test: if every satellite in LEO suddenly froze in its current path, unable to dodge, how long before two of them collide?

In January 2018, the answer was 164 days. You could lose all collision avoidance for five and a half months and still, statistically, avoid disaster.

By June 2025, the CRASH Clock read 5.5 days.

That is a 97% reduction in orbital safety margin in seven years. It captures something intuitive but previously unquantified: low Earth orbit has become so congested that the entire system depends on continuous, flawless maneuvering by thousands of independent operators with different software, different risk thresholds, and different communication protocols. "The CRASH Clock is intended to be used as an indicator for how reliant we are on operations in orbit being executed without error," Thiele told the Weather Network, "and how much stress we are placing on the orbital environment."

Aaron Rosengren, a mechanical and aerospace engineer at the University of California, San Diego, who was not involved in the study, put it more bluntly to Scientific American: "The exact number matters less than the trend."

The Arithmetic of Congestion

The trend is driven by one operator above all others. SpaceX's Starlink constellation now comprises more than 9,000 active satellites, roughly two-thirds of every functioning spacecraft in orbit around Earth. Those satellites occupy a narrow band of altitudes between 480 and 560 kilometers, where close approaches within one kilometer now occur approximately every 22 seconds across the full population of tracked objects in LEO, and where Starlink satellites individually encounter another object in their proximity zone about every 11 minutes.

SpaceX has responded to this density by adopting the most conservative collision avoidance threshold in the industry. According to the company's filings with the Federal Communications Commission, Starlink satellites initiate evasive maneuvers when the probability of collision exceeds 3 in 10 million, a standard roughly two orders of magnitude more sensitive than the industry norm of 1 in 10,000. Between December 2024 and May 2025 alone, the constellation executed 144,404 collision avoidance maneuvers.

Consider what those numbers mean in operational terms. Each maneuver consumes propellant. Propellant is finite. When a satellite exhausts its fuel reserves, it can no longer dodge anything and must be deorbited, ending its revenue-generating life prematurely. At an estimated manufacturing and launch cost of $250,000 to $500,000 per satellite, the "dodging tax" is not trivial: 396,000 maneuvers per year across 9,000 satellites means accelerated propellant consumption across the entire fleet, shortening average operational lifespans and increasing the replacement cadence that SpaceX must sustain through its already-furious launch schedule. Over a five-year satellite life with an average of 44 maneuvers per year, each Starlink satellite performs roughly 220 avoidance burns. No other satellite operator in history has managed anything close to this operational tempo. Nobody knows exactly how much it costs because SpaceX does not publish fuel-budget breakdowns, but the structural pressure on constellation economics is real and growing with every new satellite added to the orbital population.

What Could Stop the Dodging

Solar storms.

Solar activity follows an 11-year cycle, and the current Solar Cycle 25 peaked in 2024-2025 with levels of activity that surprised even veteran heliophysicists. Consider the Gannon Storm of May 2024, when a series of coronal mass ejections battered Earth's magnetosphere hard enough that more than half of all satellites in low Earth orbit had to adjust their trajectories, not because they were about to collide with each other, but because the storm expanded Earth's upper atmosphere, increasing drag on every object below 600 kilometers and throwing orbital predictions into disarray.

Solar storms attack satellite operations through two mechanisms simultaneously. Atmospheric expansion creates additional drag, forcing satellites to burn fuel to maintain altitude at exactly the moment when positional uncertainty makes collision avoidance calculations unreliable. And electromagnetic interference can disrupt the communication links between ground stations and satellites, degrading the telemetry and command signals that operators need to execute maneuvers in the first place.

In February 2022, a moderate geomagnetic storm destroyed 40 newly launched Starlink satellites outright. They never reached their operational orbits. A single severe geomagnetic event, according to industry estimates, could cost the satellite sector $40 billion.

Thiele's CRASH Clock analysis puts a finer point on the risk. A 24-hour loss of maneuvering control across LEO, the kind of disruption a major solar storm could cause, now carries a 30% probability of triggering at least one catastrophic collision. Solar storms typically provide one to two days of warning. That leaves operators with a window roughly the same length as the disruption itself to prepare, execute contingency maneuvers, and pray the models hold.

Not Just Weather: The Military Variable

On April 28, 2026, two Russian military satellites passed within three meters of each other at 17,000 miles per hour in low Earth orbit. COSMOS 2581 and COSMOS 2583, tracked by the commercial space situational awareness firm COMSPOC, executed what analysts described as a coordinated proximity operation, part of a broader inspection campaign that may involve testing anti-satellite capabilities.

Three meters. At orbital velocity, the margin between a successful rendezvous and a debris-generating collision is measured in milliseconds of timing and centimeters of trajectory accuracy. Russia has not disclosed the purpose of these operations. Western analysts note the obvious dual-use implications: a satellite that can approach another spacecraft within ten feet for "inspection" can also approach it for destruction.

Military considerations compound the CRASH Clock problem in a way the metric's designers explicitly acknowledge but cannot model. Its calculations assume a uniform, simultaneous loss of control across all satellites. A targeted attack on one operator's ground infrastructure, a scenario that is not hypothetical given Russia's demonstrated anti-satellite capabilities and China's 2007 kinetic kill test that generated over 3,500 pieces of tracked debris still circling the planet, would not trigger the same system-wide freeze but could create a localized debris cascade in exactly the orbital shells where the most traffic concentrates.

Communication failures between operators make the picture worse. In 2019, a European Space Agency science satellite had to execute an emergency maneuver to avoid a Starlink satellite after a bug in the communication system between ESA and SpaceX prevented the normal coordination process from functioning. It worked. Crisis averted. But the incident demonstrated that the orbital safety net has single points of failure that do not require solar storms or military action to exploit.

The Pipeline of Congestion

Starlink is not the only mega-constellation in the pipeline. Amazon's Project Kuiper holds FCC authorization for 3,236 satellites and has begun launching its first operational units. China's state-backed Guowang constellation has filed for more than 13,000 satellites. The European Union's IRIS² program, though much smaller, adds to the orbital population. OneWeb, Telesat, and a growing roster of national and commercial operators are deploying or planning their own constellations.

In a single 90-day period in early 2026, five companies filed applications to place over 1.1 million satellite data center nodes in orbit, a number that dwarfs the approximately 16,000 satellites currently active. Even if a fraction of those applications proceed, the arithmetic of orbital density is heading in one direction.

Extrapolating the CRASH Clock trend is crude but instructive. The metric fell from 164 days in January 2018 to 5.5 days by June 2025, an average decline of roughly 21 days per year. But the decline is not linear. It correlates with the pace of mega-constellation deployment, which itself is accelerating: SpaceX launched over 2,000 Starlink satellites in 2024 alone. If Amazon Kuiper, Guowang, and the planned commercial constellations deploy on anything close to their announced schedules, the CRASH Clock will not decline at 21 days per year because it no longer has 21 days to lose. The question is when the metric crosses below one day, the threshold at which a solar storm's warning window is shorter than the time needed to execute system-wide contingency plans, and a reasonable projection based on announced deployment timelines suggests that point could arrive before 2030.

SpaceX's Response

SpaceX has not ignored the problem. In early 2026, the company began lowering 4,400 Starlink satellites from their original 550-kilometer orbit to 480 kilometers, a 70-kilometer drop that serves multiple purposes. Lower orbits mean faster natural deorbiting for satellites that lose propulsion: at 480 kilometers, a dead satellite reenters Earth's atmosphere in roughly two to three years instead of the five to eight years it would take from 550 kilometers. Shorter debris residence times reduce the long-term collision risk.

The move was partly triggered by a near-miss with a newly launched Chinese satellite that SpaceX disclosed to the FCC in late 2025. It also improves signal quality for ground users by reducing the distance radio signals must travel, shrinking each satellite's coverage beam by approximately 24% and concentrating signal strength. But smaller beams mean more frequent handoffs between satellites as users move, introducing new service-quality tradeoffs.

The orbit reduction is a genuine structural improvement, not a cosmetic one. It is also an acknowledgment that the original altitude was no longer tenable given the traffic density. SpaceX is, in effect, retreating to a lower orbit where the physics of atmospheric drag provide a natural cleanup mechanism for failures. The question is whether other operators will follow, or whether the vacated altitude bands will simply fill with competitors' hardware.

Limitations

Several caveats apply to this analysis. The CRASH Clock is a statistical expectation, not a countdown timer. It assumes simplified orbital distributions and uniform loss of control, which is not how real disruptions work. SpaceX's conservative collision avoidance threshold means the actual collision probability during normal operations is much lower than the clock implies, and no Starlink-on-Starlink collision has occurred despite years of dense operations. The $40 billion single-storm damage estimate is an industry projection, not an actuarial figure, and relies on assumptions about satellite replacement costs and service interruption that are not publicly verifiable.

The propellant cost calculations in this article use estimated satellite costs ($250,000-$500,000 per unit) because SpaceX does not publish production cost data. The trend extrapolation to a sub-1-day CRASH Clock before 2030 assumes constellation deployment proceeds roughly on announced schedules, which historically has not been the case; Amazon's Kuiper, for instance, is significantly behind its originally announced timeline. If deployment slows, the timeline extends.

The military risk assessment is inherently speculative. Russia's proximity operations and China's debris-generating ASAT test are facts; the specific scenarios described are informed projections, not intelligence assessments.

The Strongest Case Against Alarm

The most compelling counterargument to orbital crisis rhetoric comes from SpaceX's own track record. The company operates the densest constellation in history and has not produced a single catastrophic collision. Its avoidance threshold is 333 times more conservative than the industry standard. It proactively lowered orbits when traffic density increased. It maintains a launch cadence sufficient to replace aging satellites before they become uncontrollable debris. By the most relevant operational metric, collisions per satellite-year, Starlink's safety record is unblemished.

The CRASH Clock, moreover, measures a scenario that has never occurred: total, simultaneous loss of maneuvering across all operators. Real disruptions are partial, affecting some satellites and some ground stations while others continue functioning normally. The 2022 storm destroyed 40 satellites out of thousands. The Gannon Storm forced widespread maneuvering but did not disable ground control. The system bent but did not break, and reasonable people can argue that the CRASH Clock overstates risk by modeling a worst case that the actual space environment has never produced.

This argument is correct on every factual point and still misses the structural issue. The Kessler-Cour-Palais Syndrome scenario, where one collision generates debris that causes another collision in a self-sustaining cascade, does not require total system failure. It requires one collision in the wrong place at the wrong time, in an orbital shell dense enough that fragments encounter other objects before they deorbit naturally. The 5.5-day CRASH Clock does not predict when that collision will happen. It measures how little room the system has left to absorb surprises.

The Bottom Line

Low Earth orbit has become the most congested transportation network humans have ever built, a system where 9,000 satellites from a single operator dodge obstacles every 108 seconds, where the margin between routine operations and the first catastrophic collision has collapsed from five and a half months to five and a half days in less than a decade, and where the pipeline of planned launches could push that margin below 24 hours before the current solar cycle ends. The benefits of mega-constellations are real: Starlink provides internet access to communities that fiber and cellular towers will never reach, and the economics of low-orbit broadband are transforming global connectivity. But the CRASH Clock reveals that those benefits now ride on a system with almost no tolerance for the kind of disruption that solar weather, military provocation, or a single software bug could deliver.

What You Can Do

If you work in space policy or regulation: push for mandatory, standardized conjunction data sharing between operators. The current system relies on voluntary exchange through the 18th Space Defense Squadron's public catalog and ad-hoc operator-to-operator agreements. A real-time, shared situational awareness layer, analogous to air traffic control, does not exist for LEO and is the single highest-leverage intervention available.

If you manage satellite operations: adopt SpaceX's 3-in-10-million collision probability threshold as a minimum standard, not because regulators require it but because the orbital density now demands it. The legacy 1-in-10,000 threshold was calibrated for an era when the CRASH Clock read in months, not days.

If you invest in or insure space assets: demand CRASH Clock reporting from constellation operators the same way credit markets demand stress test results from banks. A 5.5-day CRASH Clock is the orbital equivalent of a bank holding capital reserves that cover less than a week of outflows.

If you are a citizen: understand that the GPS in your phone, the weather forecast on your screen, and the emergency communications your government relies on during natural disasters all pass through an orbital environment that is 97% more fragile than it was when most of these systems were designed. The satellites are still dodging. The question is what happens when they cannot.
