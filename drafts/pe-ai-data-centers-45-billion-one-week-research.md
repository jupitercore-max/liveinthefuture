# Research: $45 Billion in Private Equity Committed to AI Data Centers in One Week

## Core Thesis
In a single week (June 9-12, 2026), two PE-backed deals committed $45 billion to AI data center infrastructure. This represents a structural shift: private equity is becoming the financial backbone of AI infrastructure, filling a capital gap hyperscalers cannot close alone. The original calculation: at $35B per GW, scaling AI XPV's 20GW target implies ~$700B in total PE-financed infrastructure — roughly 13% of Goldman Sachs' $5.3T hyperscaler capex estimate through 2030.

## Deal 1: Broadcom AI XPV Platform (June 9)
- **Capital:** $35B initial tranche
- **Investors:** Apollo Global Management (lead), Blackstone Credit & Insurance
- **First customer:** Anthropic — 1GW expansion at Fluidstack-based sites, starting mid-2026
- **Also named:** OpenAI as initial customer
- **Target:** 20GW compute capacity by 2028
- **Technology:** Broadcom XPUs + networking (not Nvidia GPUs)
- **Quote:** Hock Tan (Broadcom CEO): "once-in-a-generation opportunity"
- **Quote:** Jim Zelter (Apollo President): conviction in Broadcom tech + Anthropic roadmap
- **Quote:** Jon Gray (Blackstone President): "compute demand is too big to ignore"
- **Sources:** Reuters, WSJ, TradingView, Insider Monkey, ainvest.com

## Deal 2: Helix Digital Infrastructure (June 12)
- **Capital:** $10B+ committed long-duration capital
- **Investors:** KKR (lead), Kuwait Investment Authority, Nvidia, Vistra
- **CEO:** Adam Selipsky (ex-AWS CEO, stepped down May 2024)
- **CIO:** Waldemar Szlezak (KKR's global head of digital infrastructure)
- **Technology:** Nvidia DSX factory-aligned infrastructure
- **Power:** Vistra as preferred power partner
- **Scope:** Hyperscale DC development + ops, power generation, transmission/distribution, fiber/connectivity
- **Quote:** Selipsky: "Large users of digital infrastructure have an urgent need to reduce complexity and unlock new capacity"
- **Context:** KKR infra platform manages $100B+ in assets, including $70B+ across digital and power
- **Sources:** Reuters, WSJ, Barron's, IndexBox

## Original Calculation: Implied $/GW and Total Financing Need
- AI XPV first tranche: $35B for Anthropic's 1GW initial expansion = $35B/GW
- But the platform targets 20GW by 2028. If subsequent tranches maintain similar $/GW: 20GW × $35B/GW = $700B
- Reality check: early GW cost is likely higher (greenfield + first customer premium). Scale should compress cost. Maybe $15-25B/GW at scale, implying $300-500B for 20GW.
- Helix: $10B committed, no published GW target. At comparable $15-25B/GW, that's 0.4-0.7 GW.
- Comparison: hyperscaler self-financed DCs cost roughly $8-12B/GW (Meta's Richland Parish: $27B for 2GW = $13.5B/GW including power generation and transmission).
- PE-financed implies a premium: PE needs returns (typically 15-20% IRR), meaning hyperscalers are paying a capital cost premium for speed and risk transfer.

## Strongest Counterargument
PE solves a genuine capital gap. Hyperscalers face public bond market saturation (Goldman), and their credit issuance already dominates IG indices. PE provides off-balance-sheet financing at a time when hyperscalers are guiding $700B+ in 2026 capex collectively. Without PE, the buildout slows, inference costs don't fall, and AI's economic productivity gains stall. The premium PE extracts is the cost of speed. Meta's Richland Parish deal shows the alternative: direct financing with utility rate-basing, which shifts risk to ratepayers anyway. At least PE investors are sophisticated and consenting.

## Risk Analysis
- **Dependency:** If PE becomes the financing backbone, hyperscalers lose leverage. PE firms can charge premium returns because they control a bottleneck.
- **Duration mismatch:** AI demand may be cyclical; DC assets are 20-30 year bets. If AI model efficiency improves (more output per GPU), demand for raw compute could plateau.
- **Concentration:** Blackstone + Apollo + KKR now control a significant fraction of U.S. DC infrastructure financing. Three firms as chokepoint.
- **Risk transfer:** Meta's 4-year exit clause is the template. PE structures systematically shift long-duration risk to limited partners, ratepayers, or utility customers. Who bears the loss if AI demand falters?
- **Historical parallel:** Banks in 2005-2007 created CDO structures to distribute mortgage risk widely. Nobody individually held enough to feel the pain — until they all did. PE-financed DCs are not CDOs, but the risk-distribution logic rhymes.
