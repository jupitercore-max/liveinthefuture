Kimi K3 Delivers 3.17× More Intelligence Per Dollar Than Fable 5. Export Controls Made It Possible.

Moonshot AI’s 2.8-trillion-parameter model scores a 57 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, three points behind Anthropic’s Fable 5 at 60. But at $15 per million output tokens versus $50, each dollar buys more than three times the capability. An enterprise burning 1 billion output tokens per day would save $12.78 million a year by switching. The distillation accusations don’t weaken the story. They complete it.

By Marcus Chen · Computing & AI · July 28, 2026 · ☕ 9 min read

$0.263. That is the cost of one million output tokens per intelligence index point from Moonshot AI’s Kimi K3, the 2.8-trillion-parameter model Beijing released on July 16. For Anthropic’s Fable 5, the same metric reads $0.833, and for OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol it comes in at $0.508, which means K3 trails both American models in raw capability while crushing them in capability per dollar spent. Nobody in Silicon Valley wants to talk about what that means, because the implications land squarely on the policy they lobbied hardest to preserve.

The Intelligence-Per-Dollar Calculation

The Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index is a composite score built from nine standardized benchmarks, run identically across models, and while it is imperfect in the way all composite scores are, it remains the closest thing the industry has to a consistent ruler.

K3 delivers 3.17 times more intelligence per dollar than Fable 5, and against Sol the ratio is 1.93, which means the gap is not marginal but structural. A company spending $50,000 a day on Fable 5 API calls for an enterprise coding pipeline could get 95% of the capability for $15,000, saving $12.78 million annually. Most of that remaining 5% intelligence gap exists in broad general evaluations, not in the task-specific benchmarks where enterprise customers actually spend; on Terminal-Bench 2.1, a real-world coding benchmark, K3 scores 88.3 to Sol’s 88.8 and Fable 5’s 90.0, and on the Front-end Code Arena K3 is ranked first outright.

Factor in caching and the picture worsens for American labs. Moonshot prices cached input tokens at $0.30 per million, ten times cheaper than its standard input rate, and the company reports cache hit rates above 90% for sustained coding workloads. The Valuation Gap Is Even Wider

Moonshot is reportedly seeking a $50 billion valuation and may go public this year, while Anthropic is valued at $965 billion and OpenAI sits at $852 billion. Divide each valuation by the company’s Intelligence Index score:

Markets value each point of Anthropic’s intelligence at 18.3 times what they assign to Moonshot’s, and for OpenAI the multiple is 16.4 times, embedding assumptions about moats, safety infrastructure, enterprise contracts, regulatory positioning, and the staying power of export controls that would reprice violently if any of them weakened.

Yes, They Probably Stole Some of It

In February 2026, Anthropic publicly accused Moonshot, DeepSeek, and MiniMax of operating 24,000 fake Claude accounts and generating more than 16 million interactions to extract reasoning capabilities through distillation. Moonshot’s share was 3.4 million of those exchanges, focused on agentic reasoning, tool use, and coding. By June, OSTP Director Michael Kratsios escalated further, accusing Moonshot of building a “sophisticated internal platform” to systematically access Fable’s capabilities, while Treasury Secretary Bessent warned of sanctions and the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security opened an investigation into chip access violations.

Moonshot denied everything, Beijing called the allegations “AI hegemonism,” and China’s Ministry of Commerce countered that American firms had also trained on Chinese-generated model outputs, calling it standard industry practice.

Here is the part that should concern policymakers on both sides: even if every distillation allegation is true, it does not change the cost math. It makes it worse.

Why Theft Makes the Export Control Problem Harder

Export controls rest on a delay thesis: deny China access to cutting-edge chips, and Chinese labs will fall further behind American ones in each successive generation of models. The gap was supposed to widen. Instead, Kimi K3 sits three index points behind Fable 5, narrower than the gap a year ago, and charges 70% less for the privilege.

If K3 was built partly on distilled Fable intelligence, China found a way to convert approximately $10 million worth of stolen API access into a model seeking a $50 billion valuation, a return on distillation investment so staggering that it undermines the entire enforcement architecture. And the open-weight release means the stolen intelligence, however much of it exists in K3’s parameters, is now downloadable by anyone. You cannot un-distill a model that has already been published.

Contrast this with Anthropic’s response. On July 24, the company released Opus 5, explicitly pitched as delivering near-Fable capabilities at half the cost. Anthropic’s own Chief Revenue Officer, Dianne Penn, framed it as a commitment to “consistently deliver frontier intelligence and bring that as accessibly as possible.” The price pressure from Chinese competitors is already forcing American labs to discount their own products, and export controls did not prevent this dynamic but may have accelerated it by giving Chinese labs a reason to invest heavily in efficiency-maximizing architectures and distillation techniques that American labs, comfortably charging premium prices, had less incentive to pursue.

The Architecture Tells a Story Too

K3 activates 16 of its 896 experts per request. That is a 1.8% activation rate across 2.8 trillion parameters, meaning each query uses a fraction of the model’s total capacity while the rest remains dormant. Kimi Delta Attention delivers 6.3 times faster decoding in million-token contexts. Attention Residuals improve training efficiency by roughly 25% at less than 2% additional compute cost. Both techniques were published as open research before K3 launched.

This matters because it undermines the narrative that K3 is purely a product of theft. Distillation can extract behavioral capabilities from a target model, but it cannot invent a new attention mechanism or a novel training efficiency technique. Those architectural innovations are Moonshot’s own, and they are the primary reason K3 can price itself at $15 per million output tokens while operating profitably enough to seek a public listing. Demand was so strong that Moonshot suspended new subscriptions within two days of launch after traffic overwhelmed their compute infrastructure.

Limitations

This analysis relies on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, a single composite benchmark. Models vary significantly in performance across different real-world tasks, and no single score captures the full picture. K3’s API pricing may not be sustainable long-term; Moonshot’s revenue figures are not public, and the company could be pricing below cost to build market share. Self-hosting K3’s open weights requires GPU clusters capable of handling 2.8 trillion parameters, which limits the open-weight advantage to well-resourced organizations. Distillation allegations remain officially unproven, though Anthropic’s evidence is detailed and specific. We use Moonshot’s seeking price of $50 billion for the valuation comparison, not a confirmed post-money number.

The Strongest Case Against This Thesis

The best counterargument is straightforward: cheap copies are not the same thing as independent innovation. If Moonshot trained K3 on distilled Fable outputs, then K3’s low cost reflects intellectual property theft, not engineering superiority. In this reading, the intelligence-per-dollar metric flatters a lab that free-rode on billions of dollars in American research spending. Factor in Anthropic’s actual training costs, amortized across K3’s true development inputs, and the real price of K3’s intelligence is far higher than $15 per million tokens. But K3’s customers don’t pay Anthropic’s amortized costs, and in a market, the price on the invoice is the only one that matters.

What You Can Do

If you run AI infrastructure: Benchmark K3 against your current provider on your actual workloads, not public leaderboards. A 3-point index gap may be larger or smaller for your specific use case, but if you are spending more than $500,000 per year on API calls, the potential savings justify a serious evaluation sprint. Dual-provider architectures that route non-critical tasks to cheaper models and reserve frontier models for high-stakes decisions are becoming standard practice for good reason.

If you invest in AI companies: Ask what happens to valuation multiples if the cost floor for frontier-class intelligence drops 60-70% every 18 months, because the $16 billion per intelligence point that markets assign to Anthropic assumes pricing power that Chinese open-weight competitors are actively eroding. Consider the DeepSeek V4 Pro to K3 progression: an index jump from 44 to 57 in three months shows the Chinese frontier advancing faster than the American one.

If you work in policy: The export control framework assumed that restricting chip access would slow Chinese AI progress relative to American progress. Evidence from K3 suggests the opposite may be occurring: restrictions are incentivizing architectural efficiency and distillation techniques that reduce dependence on brute-force compute. A planned US-China AI safety dialogue in September is reportedly at risk over the IP theft accusations. Canceling it would remove one of the few remaining channels for coordinating on shared risks while doing nothing to close the cost gap.

The Bottom Line

American AI labs remain ahead on raw capability. Fable 5 scores 60 to K3’s 57. Sol scores 59. Those leads are real. But the era in which capability leadership automatically translated to market dominance is ending, because the gap is shrinking faster than prices can be cut to compensate. Anthropic already launched Opus 5 as a discount product. OpenAI has been slashing prices for two years. Whether Chinese models can compete on quality is no longer the question; whether American models can compete on cost is, and at $0.833 per intelligence point against $0.263, the math says not yet.
