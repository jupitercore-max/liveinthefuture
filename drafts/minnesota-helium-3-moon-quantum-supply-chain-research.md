# Research Notes: Minnesota's Helium-3 vs. the Moon

## Story Angle
Three U.S. government labs (WHOI, USGS, LLNL) independently confirmed that a well in northeastern Minnesota contains helium-3 at concentrations matching lunar regolith — while Interlune plans billion-dollar Moon missions and DARPA scrambles for alternatives. The economics of terrestrial extraction obliterate lunar mining on cost.

## Original Calculation: Topaz He-3 Output vs. Quantum Demand

### He-3 production from single Topaz well:
- Jetstream #1 peak compressed flow: 1.3 MMcf/d = 36,812 m³/d at STP
- He-3 at 12 ppb by volume in total gas stream
- He-3 volume: 36,812 × 12 × 10⁻⁹ = 4.42 × 10⁻⁴ m³/d = 0.442 L/d
- **Annual per well: ~161 L/year**
- At $2,500/L: **~$403,000/year in He-3 per well**
- 10-well program: ~1,610 L/yr = **5-7% of global tritium-derived supply**

### He-4 revenue (the primary product):
- He-4 at 8% of 1.3 MMcf/d: 104 Mcf/d per well
- At $550/Mcf contract price: $57,200/day = **$20.9M/year per well in He-4**
- He-3 is a super-premium byproduct at marginal cost near zero

### Supply-demand gap:
- Current global He-3 supply (tritium decay): 22,000-30,000 L/yr ≈ 3-4 kg/yr
- Current dilution fridge demand: ~50 L each × hundreds of units
- Million-qubit era demand: 10,000-50,000 L per machine
- Projected 1,000+ quantum computers: 50,000-50,000,000 L/yr
- **Gap is 3-4 orders of magnitude if quantum scales as projected**

### Cost comparison (per liter of He-3):
| Source | Cost/liter | Status | Bottleneck |
|--------|-----------|--------|------------|
| Tritium decay | ~$2,500 | Current | Nuclear weapons maintenance, 3-4 kg/yr max |
| Topaz (byproduct) | Near-zero marginal | 2027-2028? | He-4 pays all costs; He-3 extraction is TBD |
| Interlune (Moon) | ~$3,000 | 2028-2029? | Robotic lunar lander + excavator + return vehicle |

### The insight:
A kilogram of lunar He-3 requires: a rocket to the Moon ($100M+), autonomous excavators processing thousands of tons of regolith, gas extraction at 1/6 Earth gravity, hermetic storage, and a return mission. A kilogram of Topaz He-3 requires: separating an isotope from a gas stream you're already producing. The marginal cost difference is not incremental — it's civilizational.

## Strongest Counterargument
Topaz's He-3 output is impressive for a terrestrial source but physically incapable of solving the scale problem. At 161 L/well/year from 10 wells = 1,610 L/yr. The quantum computing industry projects needing 100,000+ L/yr within a decade. Even if the entire Topaz reservoir produced at maximum theoretical rates, it cannot close the gap. Lunar regolith holds a billion kg. Topaz buys time; the Moon is the only source with sufficient inventory.

Additionally, DARPA and multiple research groups are pursuing He-3-free alternatives (adiabatic demagnetization, on-chip electronic coolers), which could make the supply question moot. The Finnish company Bluefors is already developing modular platforms. If alternative cooling succeeds, neither Topaz nor the Moon matters.

## Limitations
- Pulsar Helium is a junior exploration company (market cap ~$248M, no revenue)
- No commercial He-3 extraction infrastructure at Topaz — isotope separation at ppb concentrations is non-trivial
- No production timeline or resource estimate filed (still in appraisal)
- He-3 ppb figures verified, but conversion to commercial product requires technology that doesn't exist at this site yet
- All Interlune timelines (2027 test, 2030 commercial) are aspirational; no lunar missions completed
