## 2. Novel Contribution

### The Regulatory Gap
- ~45 fusion companies worldwide have raised approximately $8-9B in private capital
- Only ONE has regulatory licenses for a power plant
- Fission licensing via NRC: typically 5-10 years (10 CFR Part 50/52 process)
- Fusion licensing via Agreement State (WA DOH): ~2-3 years of engagement for Helion
- This represents a 60-80% reduction in regulatory timeline
- Critical insight: the ADVANCE Act created a two-speed regulatory system where WHERE you build matters as much as WHAT you build
- Not all states are Agreement States — 39 states + DC have agreement status; 11 do not
- Implication for the industry: fusion companies choosing sites in non-Agreement states face longer federal NRC oversight

### PPA Factory Math
- Microsoft PPA: 50MW from Orion
- At competitive wholesale rates (~$50/MWh, 85% capacity factor): ~$18.6M/year revenue
- Reported OpenAI deal: 5GW by 2030
- 5GW ÷ 50MW per plant = 100 Orion-class plants
- Timeline: 2026-2030 = 4 years → 25 plants per year
- No fusion company has built even one. Helion is proposing to build the first AND scale to 25/year within the same decade.
- That is not a physics problem. It is a manufacturing and construction logistics problem — and it is why Helion employs 600 people weighted toward technicians, not scientists.

## 4. Strongest Counterargument
John Slough (Helion co-founder, now critic) argues FRC confinement at Helion's compression speeds produces "catastrophic" flux loss. Karl Lackner's Max Planck group published formal critique in Journal of Fusion Energy (Feb 2026) arguing Helion's ion-electron temperature assumptions are "much more demanding" than their analysis suggests. Troy Carter (Oak Ridge) notes Helion "doesn't publish" enough for independent evaluation. Helion missed its own 2024 net-electricity deadline. No published results confirm net power generation from Polaris. A regulatory license is not a physics result — it confirms safety programs and personnel, not that fusion works at commercial scale.
