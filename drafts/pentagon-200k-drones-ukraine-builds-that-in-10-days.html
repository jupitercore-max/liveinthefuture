The Pentagon Ordered 200,000 Drones. Ukraine Builds That Many Every 10 Days.

On July 27, the Pentagon’s own drone chief admitted the U.S. is “still years” from matching Ukraine’s wartime output. Ukraine produces 19,178 attack drones per day. The Pentagon’s $1.1 billion Drone Dominance Program has ordered 200,000 total. Run the math three ways on that $1.1 billion and the gap stops being about money. It’s about industrial architecture.

By Tomás Reyes · Defense & National Security · July 28, 2026 · ☕ 10 min read

Seven million drones a year. That is what Ukraine will produce in 2026, according to Travis Metz, the Defense Innovation Unit’s deputy director who runs the Pentagon’s Drone Dominance Program. He shared the figure in a Reuters interview published July 27, alongside the American counterpart: just under 200,000 drones ordered cumulatively through February.

Do the division. Ukraine’s 7 million drones per year works out to 19,178 per day. At that rate, Ukraine matches the Pentagon’s entire cumulative order in 10.4 days. The Gauntlet II follow-on order of 60,000 drones, the one the Pentagon calls a dramatic scale-up? Ukraine builds that in three.

Metz, to his credit, did not pretend the gap was small. “It’s much harder to get from zero to 200,000” than it will be to scale further, he said, and he expects the U.S. to “eventually rival” Ukraine’s output. But what he described next should concern anyone tracking the program: on July 23, four days before the interview, the Pentagon released a revised supply chain framework mandating a “wholly domestic” small unmanned aircraft system supply chain. Chinese-made brushless motors and battery packs are now banned from any drone competing in the August Gauntlet II test at Fort Carson, Colorado.

The problem is that the “vast majority” of drones ordered in the program’s first phase used those exact Chinese motors. Metz’s own words.

The Gauntlet Model: Competition at Scale

The Drone Dominance Program is structurally unusual for the Pentagon. Instead of a decade-long development contract with a prime, it runs live-fire competitions called Gauntlets. Forty-nine companies showed up at Camp Grayling, Michigan, in June. Nineteen survived to advance to Gauntlet II. Each of those nineteen must now deliver 120 drones equipped with lethality payloads within five weeks just to remain eligible, a production benchmark that deliberately filters out companies that can build an impressive prototype but cannot manufacture at volume.

Gauntlet I, held at Fort Benning in February and March, produced roughly $150 million in orders for 30,000 one-way attack drones. That price works out to $5,000 per drone. Gauntlet II targets 50,000 to 60,000 additional drones from the best performers. The program’s stated price ambition is $5,000 per unit in the near term, falling to $2,000 as competition matures.

For context, the first American one-way attack drone used in combat, a system called LUCAS built by Arizona-based SpektreWorks, cost $35,000 per unit. The Gauntlet program is explicitly trying to undercut that by an order of magnitude.

And who topped the first Gauntlet leaderboard? Not an American company. Skycutter, a London-based firm partnered with Ukrainian manufacturer SkyFall, scored 99.3 out of 100 with its Shrike 10 Fiber, a fiber-optic FPV drone designed on the Ukrainian battlefield. Ukrainian Defense Drones finished sixth with 72.9 points. The Pentagon’s own evaluation ranked a Ukrainian-designed drone above every American entrant.

The $1.1 Billion Question: Three Ways to Spend It

The Drone Dominance Program has a two-year budget of $1.1 billion. What does that buy?

Option A — the Pentagon’s approach. Order drones at $5,000 each through competitive Gauntlets. Result: 220,000 drones. This is, roughly, what the program is doing.

Option B — buy at Ukrainian prices. The average Ukrainian FPV attack drone costs between $331 and $443, depending on quality. Call it $400. At that price, $1.1 billion buys 2.75 million drones, more than twelve times the Pentagon’s cumulative order, and enough to equip every active-duty U.S. soldier with two.

Option C — build factories instead of buying drones. Finnish defense company Sensofusion sells what it calls a Tactical Drone Factory: a standard 20-foot shipping container fitted with industrial 3D printers, an electronics assembly station, and enough spares to run around the clock. Crew of three. Output: 50 interceptor drones per day, each costing under $580. The price of one factory: $2.4 million.

At $2.4 million per factory, $1.1 billion buys 458 of them. Each produces 50 drones per day, or 18,250 per year. Multiply: 458 factories × 18,250 drones per year = 8.36 million drones annually. That is more than Ukraine’s entire projected 2026 output of seven million.

Why Industrial Architecture Matters More Than Money

Ukraine did not get to seven million drones a year by writing large procurement contracts. It got there by distributing production across more than 160 small manufacturers, operating from warehouses, converted barns, and basements. A volunteer army of builders, coordinated through Telegram channels and Zoom tutorials, cranked out airframes alongside established defense firms. General David Petraeus, visiting Ukrainian drone manufacturers in person, reported that a single Ukrainian company told him it alone would produce three million drones in 2026.

The trajectory is instructive. Ukraine had essentially no domestic drone industry in 2022. By 2024, it produced roughly two million. By 2025, about 3.5 million. By 2026, six to seven million. That is a compound annual growth rate of approximately 90% sustained over four years, achieved under constant bombardment of its energy grid, factories, and logistics infrastructure.

The United States, by contrast, has 19 companies advancing through a structured Gauntlet competition, supported by the world’s largest defense budget. Its domestic drone production across all sectors runs somewhere between 300,000 and 400,000 units per year. Ukraine launches more than that every month.

The Chinese Component Paradox

Senator Roger Wicker, chairman of the Senate Armed Services Committee, testified earlier this year that China controls more than 90% of the global market for small, non-military drones through “years of state subsidies and aggressive pricing.” American-made drones cost five to 25 times more than Chinese equivalents.

That cost gap creates a paradox the Pentagon is now walking straight into. Banning Chinese components is a sound national security decision. But doing it while simultaneously trying to scale drone production to match a country building seven million units a year means the Pentagon is asking American manufacturers to replace 90% of their supply chain while multiplying output by orders of magnitude, both at once.

Ukraine faced the same challenge and solved it under fire. Ukrainian manufacturers now produce their own flight controllers, circuit boards, and radio control systems domestically. The cost of their domestically sourced drones actually went down as they localized, according to a GlobalData analysis of the Ukrainian drone industry. Ukraine cut its dependency on Chinese components and reduced per-unit cost simultaneously.

The American trajectory runs the other direction. Firestorm Labs, the most-tested U.S. portable drone factory, produces about 50 drones per month from its xCell system, a two-container setup. Finland’s Sensofusion hits the same number per day from a single container with three workers. The American factory is 30 times slower than its Finnish competitor for the same category of output.

The Ramp Rate Problem

Assume the Pentagon’s Gauntlet program continues to scale. Gauntlet I ordered 30,000 drones. Gauntlet II targets 60,000. At this pace, the program is ordering roughly 180,000 drones per year, if it runs two Gauntlets annually. Ukraine’s current output is 39 times that rate.

Even if the Pentagon doubled its Gauntlet orders every year, an aggressive ramp by any defense procurement standard, the math looks like this:

The Strongest Case for the Status Quo

The obvious rebuttal: these are not the same drones. Pentagon tactical UAS carry encrypted communications, anti-jamming capabilities, thermal optics, extended range, and reusable airframes designed for reconnaissance missions that an $400 FPV kamikaze drone cannot perform. The $5,000 Gauntlet price tag buys a weapon system that must survive weapons qualification, testing, and compliance regimes that a Ukrainian volunteer assembling airframes in a basement does not face. A Ukrainian FPV has a flight time of roughly ten minutes and a range measured in single-digit kilometers. It is a one-way munition optimized for a single task: delivering a warhead to a target it can see.

This is a real distinction, and it matters. The $5,000 drone and the $400 drone serve different operational needs, and pretending otherwise is dishonest.

But here is where the argument bends: the Pentagon’s own evaluation ranked a Ukrainian-designed FPV drone above every American competitor. The Shrike 10 Fiber, a battlefield-proven design from SkyFall, scored 99.3 out of 100 in Gauntlet I, evaluated on strike accuracy at 2.5 to 10 kilometers, urban operational performance, multi-target engagement, ease of operator use, and production scalability. The Pentagon tested its criteria and the Ukrainian design dominated them. If the Gauntlet’s scoring reflects what the military actually wants, the gap is not a product of differing requirements. It is a production capacity problem.

What We Did Not Prove

Several caveats limit this analysis. The Sensofusion factory output of 50 drones per day is a claimed figure from the manufacturer, not independently verified sustained production data. Our ramp-rate projections assume consistent doubling, which historically no Pentagon program has sustained for five consecutive years. Ukrainian production figures from General Petraeus and other public accounts may run higher than Kyiv’s official tallies, which are not publicly available in full. The per-drone cost comparison excludes integration, training, logistics, and lifecycle maintenance costs that inflate the Pentagon’s real cost but do not appear in unit pricing. And some portion of Ukraine’s production volume relies on volunteer labor and wartime urgency that cannot be assumed in a peacetime industrial base.

We also do not know the actual per-drone cost of Gauntlet orders. The $5,000 figure derives from dividing Gauntlet I’s $150 million in orders by 30,000 drones. The Pentagon has not confirmed this math, and the actual contract values are classified.

What You Can Do

If you work in defense acquisition: Study Ukraine’s distributed manufacturing model before writing the next request for proposals. The Sensofusion factory-in-a-container approach, whether from Finland or an American competitor, represents a fundamentally different procurement philosophy: buy capability to produce, not units produced. The Pentagon’s own $1.1 billion budget, redeployed toward portable factory infrastructure, could create permanent domestic capacity that exceeds Ukraine’s output.

If you run a drone company competing in Gauntlet: The 120-drones-in-five-weeks production requirement for Gauntlet II entry is the Pentagon telling you, in plain terms, that manufacturing speed is a selection criterion, not just performance. The companies that solve domestic supply chain for motors and batteries fastest will own this market, not the ones with the best airframes.

If you invest in defense tech: Track the companies building production infrastructure, not just platforms. Sensofusion, Firestorm Labs, and the Ukrainian JV partners localizing in Ohio and New Hampshire are worth watching for the same reason contract manufacturers are more valuable than most hardware startups: they are the picks and shovels of a drone gold rush.

If you follow national security policy: The Pentagon’s July 23 supply chain framework is directionally correct on security but creates a manufacturing headwind that compounds the production gap. The critical signal to watch is whether Gauntlet II drone costs rise or fall compared to Gauntlet I. If they rise, the domestic supply chain mandate is making the gap worse, and parity moves further out.

The Bottom Line

The Pentagon has a $1.1 billion program designed to close a drone production gap with a country whose GDP is one-twelfth of the U.S. defense budget. Ukraine, under active bombardment, scaled from zero to seven million drones in four years using distributed manufacturing, volunteer labor, and an iterative design cycle compressed by the fact that its operators are dying. The United States, with every material advantage, has ordered 200,000 drones total and just banned the components most of them were built with.

The gap is not about money. At $2.4 million per portable factory, the Pentagon’s existing budget could fund 458 factories producing 8.36 million drones per year, more than Ukraine’s current output. The gap is about how the money gets spent: on units ordered through a competition, or on the infrastructure to produce them indefinitely. Ukraine chose factories. The Pentagon is still choosing purchase orders.

Travis Metz said the United States should become “the world champions” of drone production. At 200,000 drones against seven million, the U.S. is not in the race. The question is whether anyone in the building will notice before it matters.
