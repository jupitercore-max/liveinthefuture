## Thesis
Quaise Energy has raised hundreds of millions, signed a PPA, and asked Congress for $410 million — all on a technology that has been demonstrated to 100 meters. Their target: 5,000 meters. That's a 50× scale gap, the widest in clean energy. If millimeter-wave drilling works at depth, it unlocks 90+ GW of always-on geothermal power anywhere on Earth. If it doesn't, it's the most expensive rock collection in Oregon.

## Key Data Points

- **Drilling depth demonstrated:** 100m (July 2025), ~122m/400ft into granite (Congressional testimony)
- **Drilling depth needed:** ~5,000m (5km, about 3 miles) for superhot rock at Project Obsidian (Tier I site)
- **Gap ratio:** 50× (100m → 5,000m)
- **Technology:** Gyrotron millimeter-wave energy that melts and vaporizes rock. "Where drilling stops, Quaise begins."
- **Project Obsidian (Oregon):** First phase under construction. Two well systems: one targeting 315°C rock, another targeting 365°C.
- **Output target:** 50 MW initially, expandable to 250 MW, long-term 1 GW at same site
- **Footprint:** ~20 acres (vs ~250 acres for equivalent solar)
- **Fundraise:** Seeking $100M Series B + $100M grants/debt = $200M
- **Congressional ask:** $410M FY2027 for geothermal office
- **PPA:** Signed for first 50 MW with undisclosed customer
- **Timeline:** 2030 for first operations
- **Conventional geothermal:** Only 0.4% of US electricity, ~3.7 GW installed (mostly California/Nevada)
- **Republican support:** Geothermal tax credits survived while wind/solar credits revoked
- **Key risks:** Supercritical water conditions at 5km (>374°C, >220 bar), beam coherence over 5km of bore, unknown impurities in produced water, steam vs liquid water behavior unknown

## Strongest Counterargument
Quaise has raised $200M and asked for $410M from Congress on the strength of a 100-meter drilling demonstration. The gap between 100m and 5,000m isn't linear — it's exponential in difficulty. At 5km, pressures exceed 1,000 bar, temperatures pass supercritical water conditions (374°C), and the millimeter-wave beam must maintain coherence through 5km of bore filled with vaporized rock. Conventional drill bits fail at these conditions; there's no guarantee an energy beam won't too. Every deep drilling project in history has encountered surprises below 3km — unexpected rock formations, fluid behavior changes, equipment failures. Quaise's confirmation well in Oregon will be drilled conventionally first, meaning the millimeter-wave system won't even be tested at depth until AFTER they've already spent most of the $200M. Investors are essentially pre-funding a science experiment.

## Limitations
- Quaise is private; financial details beyond fundraise targets are not public
- The 10× power per well claim comes from Quaise's own analysis presented at Stanford workshop, not independently verified
- DOE's 90-300 GW estimate assumes cost reductions that haven't been demonstrated
- Comparison to ITER/fusion is illustrative, not exact — different technology, timelines, and failure modes
- We cannot independently verify the drilling depth claims; relying on company disclosures

## Actionable Takeaways
1. For policymakers: The $410M ask is 1/60th of ITER's cost and targets 90+ GW of potential. Even as a hedge, the expected value is asymmetric.
2. For energy investors: Watch the confirmation well results (late 2026). That's the binary event.
3. For data center operators signing nuclear PPAs: Superhot geothermal could deliver baseload power on similar timelines (2030) at potentially lower cost. Don't sign decade-long nuclear commitments without at least evaluating geothermal alternatives.
4. For the geothermal industry: Quaise's success or failure affects the entire sector's credibility. A blowout failure at Project Obsidian would set back all next-gen geothermal funding.
