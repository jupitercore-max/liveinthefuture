China Now Has Three Mass-Production Robotaxi Programs. The U.S. Has Two.

XPeng rolled the first mass-produced vision-only robotaxi off its Guangzhou line on May 18, packing 3,000 TOPS of in-house silicon and zero LiDAR sensors. Geely unveiled a 43-sensor purpose-built cab with no steering wheel. Baidu already runs 250,000 paid rides a week across 20 cities. Three companies, three fundamentally different architectural bets, one market. No other country is running this experiment.

By Kai Nakamura May 21, 2026

Three. That is the number of distinct, mass-production-capable robotaxi architectures now competing in China's autonomous vehicle market as of this week, each representing a fundamentally different engineering philosophy about how self-driving cars should see, think, and be built. The United States, despite its head start in commercial robotaxi operations, has exactly two.

On May 18, XPeng rolled the first unit of its mass-produced robotaxi off the production line in Guangzhou. It was the first time a Chinese automaker had achieved full-stack, in-house robotaxi mass production: the silicon, the AI model, the vehicle platform, the manufacturing. Four proprietary Turing AI chips deliver 3,000 TOPS of computing power. There is no LiDAR. No high-definition mapping. Just cameras and an end-to-end vision model called VLA 2.0 that XPeng claims compresses response latency to under 80 milliseconds.

Days earlier at Auto China 2026, Geely had pulled the cloth off the EVA Cab, its own robotaxi built on the opposite premise. No steering wheel. No pedals. No provision for a human driver at all. Dual Nvidia Drive Thor-U processors deliver 1,400 TOPS. Forty-three sensors bristle from its body, LiDAR prominent among them. Deployment is planned for 2027 through CaoCao Mobility's existing 60-city ride-hailing network.

And then there is Baidu, which skipped the manufacturing debate entirely. Its Apollo Go service already operates more than 250,000 paid robotaxi rides per week across 20-plus Chinese cities, using third-party vehicles outfitted with its autonomous driving software. Baidu's bet: the fleet is the product, not the car.

The Architecture Matrix

What makes China's robotaxi moment structurally interesting is not that three companies are building self-driving taxis. It is that they have placed three orthogonal bets on the most contested questions in autonomous vehicle design, and all three will be testable against real passengers, in the same cities, under the same regulatory regime, within 18 months.

For comparison, the U.S. market runs on two architectures. Waymo operates the most mature commercial service globally: hundreds of thousands of weekly rides across San Francisco, Los Angeles, Phoenix, and Austin, using Jaguar I-PACE vehicles loaded with LiDAR and radar. Tesla launched its robotaxi service in Austin in June 2025 with safety monitors, began unsupervised operations in January 2026, and has since expanded to Dallas and Houston while ramping Cybercab production at Giga Texas. Both use purpose-built or heavily modified vehicles. Neither shares its platform with a consumer car line in the way XPeng does.

The Compute Economics Nobody Ran

Here is the calculation that reveals the sharpest divergence between these bets. XPeng packs 3,000 TOPS into its robotaxi using four proprietary Turing AI chips. That is 750 TOPS per chip, manufactured and designed in-house, meaning XPeng pays the wafer cost and packaging cost, but not the margin that Nvidia charges OEMs for its silicon.

Geely buys two Nvidia Drive Thor-U processors at 700 TOPS each for 1,400 TOPS total. At Nvidia's volume pricing for automotive-grade chips, each Thor-U module is estimated to cost between $2,000 and $3,000. Geely pays roughly $4,000 to $6,000 per vehicle in compute silicon alone, before factoring in the LiDAR and 41 additional sensors.

XPeng's in-house silicon cost is opaque, but the economics of chip design at scale follow a well-documented curve. McKinsey estimates that fabless chip companies achieve 55 to 65% gross margins on mature-node designs when shipping millions of units. XPeng's consumer GX SUV uses the same four Turing chips, meaning the silicon R&D amortizes across both the consumer and robotaxi product lines. If XPeng ships 100,000 GX vehicles per year (consumer plus robotaxi combined), the per-unit R&D allocation drops to roughly $800 to $1,200, with wafer and packaging costs adding perhaps $1,500 to $2,000. Total compute cost per vehicle: approximately $2,300 to $3,200.

That gives XPeng 2.1 times Geely's compute headroom at 58% to 75% of the silicon cost. The tradeoff: XPeng's Turing chips are unproven in L4 edge cases at scale, while Nvidia's Thor has the entire global automotive supply chain validating its silicon across dozens of OEMs.

What the Orders Say

Demand data from Pony AI provides a real-time signal on consumer adoption. During China's Labor Day holiday (May 1 to 5), Pony AI's average daily paid robotaxi orders surged 544% year-over-year, with a 155% increase over the New Year holiday period. The company deployed hundreds of seventh-generation robotaxis to transport 3,000 concertgoers during the break and targets more than 3,000 vehicles on the road by the end of 2026. It launched Europe's first commercial robotaxi service in Zagreb.

Pony AI's numbers matter because they demonstrate that Chinese consumers are adopting robotaxis not as novelties but as routine transport. A 544% year-over-year surge in a market that barely existed 24 months ago is the growth curve of a category being born, not a pilot being tolerated.

The Platform-Sharing Wager

XPeng's most contrarian bet is not its vision-only approach. Tesla already validated that strategy directionally by operating unsupervised vehicles in Austin without LiDAR. The real gamble is platform sharing.

The GX robotaxi rides on the same architecture as the $58,000 GX consumer SUV that XPeng launched at Auto China 2026. Same four Turing chips at 3,000 TOPS. Same VLA 2.0 autonomous driving stack. Same Bosch steer-by-wire and six-layer safety redundancy. The robotaxi variant strips out the driver-focused layout and installs gravity seats, privacy glass, and independent rear displays in 5-seat, 6-seat, and 7-seat configurations.

This mirrors Tesla's early strategy of using Model 3 and Model Y to gather driving data while developing the Cybercab. But XPeng goes further: its robotaxi is literally the same car, on the same line, with a different interior. The consumer GX generates revenue today while validating every sensor, every chip, and every meter of the autonomous stack in the real world.

Geely's EVA Cab, by contrast, is a clean-sheet robotaxi with no consumer revenue stream. Every dollar invested in its development is pure R&D cost until the first commercial ride. The advantage: design optimization unconstrained by the compromises of a consumer vehicle. The disadvantage: CaoCao Mobility must absorb the full development cost without any offset from consumer vehicle sales.

Strongest Counterargument

Production is not deployment. XPeng has rolled exactly one robotaxi off its assembly line and logged exactly zero L4 operational miles with paying passengers. Baidu has years of ride data. Waymo has years. Tesla has months of unsupervised operations across three Texas cities. Geely's EVA Cab remains an unveil, not a service.

The hardest part of the robotaxi business is not building the car. It is surviving the ten-millionth edge case on a rain-slicked turn in Shenzhen at 11 PM when a delivery scooter runs a red light from behind a parked bus. Manufacturing capability and deployment capability are separated by an enormous, unforgiving canyon of real-world validation. XPeng's pilot operations have not even started. Its "fully unsupervised by early 2027" target is aggressive in the technical sense and aspirational in the operational sense.

Limitations

XPeng's claim of "5x better performance than competitors on takeover rates" is self-reported, unverified, and measured on internal benchmarks that have not been disclosed. The sub-80ms latency figure has not been independently benchmarked. Pony AI's 544% year-over-year growth is calculated from a small base during a holiday period and should not be extrapolated as a sustained trend. No Chinese robotaxi operator has disclosed per-ride unit economics. CaoCao Mobility's 60-city figure refers to its entire ride-hailing network, not autonomous-ride coverage. Our compute cost estimates for XPeng's Turing silicon rely on industry-standard fabless margins and amortization models, not XPeng's actual cost structure, which is not public.

The Bottom Line

China is running the most comprehensive architectural experiment in autonomous vehicle history. Three companies with three production-capable programs, each testing a different answer to the three hardest questions in the field: vision-only or sensor fusion for perception, shared platform or purpose-built for the vehicle, and in-house or third-party for the silicon.

If you are an investor, fleet operator, or policymaker in autonomous transport, the next 18 months of Chinese operational data will be more informative than the previous five years of pilot programs anywhere else. Watch for XPeng's H2 2026 pilot results and its takeover rate in dense urban traffic. Watch for Geely's CaoCao deployment throughput and whether its LiDAR investment translates into measurably lower incident rates. Watch for whether Baidu's asset-light model can sustain profitability as its hardware partners demand margin. The answers will not come from whitepapers or investor presentations. They will come from the 544% growth curve of Chinese consumers who have decided that climbing into a driverless car is just how you get across town.
