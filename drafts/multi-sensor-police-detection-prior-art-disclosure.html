One Disclosure Just Killed the Patent on AI Radar Detectors. The Three Companies That Could Build It Won’t.

A year-old public domain disclosure describes fusing AI computer vision, radar/lidar detection, ALPR, radio scanning, and automated crowdsourced spotting into a single in-vehicle police detection system. Combined false positive rate: below 0.1%. No single sensor gets close. The disclosure is CC0. Nobody has built it.

Marcus Cole Defense & Transport June 25, 2026

Zero point one percent. That is the combined false positive rate you get when an AI camera system trained on police vehicles, a Ka-band radar detector, and an automatic license plate reader all flag the same vehicle from the same direction at the same moment. Each sensor alone is mediocre. Vision-only detection produces false positives on 5 to 10 percent of civilian Ford Explorers and Dodge Chargers that look like police fleet vehicles. Ka-band radar alone cries wolf on roughly 30 percent of alerts thanks to automatic door openers, blind-spot monitoring systems, and adaptive cruise control. But stack those independent signals through a Bayesian fusion engine and the error rate collapses by two orders of magnitude. This is not a product. This is a prior art disclosure published exactly one year ago today, dedicated to the public domain, and nobody has built it yet.

The technical disclosure, filed June 25, 2025 under CC0 (Creative Commons Public Domain Dedication), describes a seven-subsystem platform that would represent the most significant leap in driver situational awareness technology since the original Valentine One introduced directional arrows in 1992. The inventor, Jeremy Clawson, published it to ensure no company can patent the concept of fusing AI vision with RF detection for law enforcement spotting. Under 35 U.S.C. §102, it constitutes prior art as of that date.

The Problem With Radar Detectors

The radar detector industry is a $1.2 billion market dominated by three companies: Valentine Research, Escort (formerly Cincinnati Microwave), and Uniden. All three sell fundamentally the same product — a superheterodyne receiver tuned to X, K, and Ka radio bands plus a photodiode for 904 nm lidar. The technology is mature, refined, and at a dead end.

Here is why. Modern police radar systems like the Stalker II and Decatur Genesis II have an instant-on mode. The officer keeps the transmitter in "hold" — electromagnetically silent — until a target vehicle is within measurement range. By the time the detector receives the burst and alerts the driver, the officer already has a speed reading. Reaction time for the driver: 0.5 to 2 seconds. The speed measurement is already complete.

Lidar is worse. A police lidar beam diverges at roughly 3 milliradians. At 1,000 feet, the beam is 3 feet across, aimed at a specific headlight or front plate. Your detector receives only secondary scatter. The speed measurement finishes before enough scattered energy reaches the detector to trigger an alert. Lidar detection is not a warning. It is a post-measurement notification.

Then there are the methods that produce zero electromagnetic emission: pacing, VASCAR, aircraft timing. No detector on the market can sense these because there is nothing to sense. A police vehicle parked in a highway median with radar on hold is invisible to every product from Valentine, Escort, and Uniden.

What The Disclosure Describes

The system inverts the detection paradigm. Instead of RF as the primary sensor, AI computer vision becomes primary, with RF serving as confirmation. A forward-facing camera running a YOLO-class object detection model on a dedicated neural processing unit identifies marked and unmarked police vehicles, police motorcycles, and officers on foot. The model classifies what it sees: vehicle type, livery, agency, whether emergency lights are active, whether the vehicle is positioned in traffic-stop posture behind another car.

This alone would be a meaningful upgrade over RF-only detection. But the disclosure does not stop there. The vision pipeline feeds into a sensor fusion engine alongside the radar detector's structured output (band, signal strength, direction, bogie count), an optional software-defined radio that detects carrier energy — not audio content, just presence — on public safety dispatch frequencies, an ALPR subsystem that reads plates and checks them against a local database of known police vehicles, and a crowdsourced network where vehicles automatically share high-confidence detections with zero user interaction.

The fusion engine combines these heterogeneous inputs using Bayesian inference or Dempster-Shafer evidence theory, producing a single confidence score for each candidate detection. A vision detection alone might produce a likelihood ratio of 50:1 for a clearly marked police vehicle. A Ka-band alert alone produces a noisy 8:1. But when both fire from the same direction at the same time, and ALPR returns a match on a government plate series, the combined likelihood ratio exceeds 1,000:1.

Why Waze Is Dangerous

Waze, owned by Google since 2013, provides crowdsourced police spotting. It requires 3 to 5 manual taps to file a report. At 70 mph, that is 200 to 400 feet of travel with the driver's eyes on a phone screen instead of the road. The manual reporting model is literally more dangerous than the speed enforcement it warns about.

The disclosure's automated crowdsourced spotting eliminates this entirely. When the fusion engine's confidence exceeds a configurable threshold (default: 0.85), the system shares an anonymized report — GPS coordinates, timestamp, detection type, confidence score, contributing sensor modalities. No user interaction. No eyes off the road. Reports expire after 15 minutes with exponential confidence decay. Multiple vehicles corroborating the same detection reinforce confidence. A lightweight reputation system filters adversarial poisoning attempts.

The Edge Computing Imperative

Every inference happens on the device. No camera footage leaves the vehicle. No license plates of civilian vehicles are stored. The system processes frames and outputs only structured metadata: class label, confidence, bounding box coordinates, timestamp. This is architecturally enforced, not a privacy policy that can change.

This matters because the hardware to do it barely existed five years ago. Running a YOLO-class detector at 30 frames per second on a vehicle's 12-volt power supply required a laptop GPU. The Hailo-8 NPU delivers 26 TOPS at 2.5 watts. Google's Coral Edge TPU manages 4 TOPS at 2 watts. The Nvidia Jetson Orin Nano pushes 40 TOPS. These chips made the disclosed system technically feasible. They also made it cheap enough to manufacture at consumer dashcam prices.

Strongest Counterargument

The strongest case against this system is that the radar detector industry has had decades to add sensors and chose not to, which means either they have analyzed it and found it commercially unviable or they are run by hardware engineers with no software capability. Valentine Research is a privately held company with an estimated 40 employees and has not meaningfully updated its detection technology since the V1 Gen2 added Bluetooth. Escort was acquired and restructured multiple times. Uniden is a consumer electronics conglomerate that treats radar detectors as a legacy SKU. None of these companies employ a single machine learning engineer, and building the training pipeline for police vehicle detection — curating images across all 50 states, hard-negative mining against civilian fleet vehicles, quantization-aware training for INT8 edge deployment — requires capabilities that are entirely absent from their organizational DNA.

That means the system, if built, will come from outside the detector industry. The most likely builders are dashcam companies adding AI features (Garmin, BlackVue), aftermarket automotive tech companies with ML expertise, or open-source projects leveraging commodity SDR hardware and smartphone NPUs. The disclosure ensures that whoever builds it cannot prevent competitors from building it too.

What This Analysis Did Not Prove

No working prototype exists. The false positive calculations assume statistical independence between sensor modalities, which is approximately true but not perfectly so. A police vehicle parked at a strip mall with K-band automatic doors would generate correlated false positives across both vision (visible police vehicle shape) and radar (K-band emission), violating the independence assumption and inflating the combined confidence. The disclosure acknowledges this risk through GPS-based lockout databases for known fixed false-positive locations, but this only works after the system has learned the location through repeated exposure.

The ALPR subsystem depends on a database of law enforcement license plates built from FOIA requests and public records. Some states exempt police vehicle registrations from public disclosure. California's "E" exempt plates are identifiable by prefix, but other states use less transparent coding. The database will be geographically uneven.

The crowdsourced spotting network requires critical mass. With fewer than roughly 100 participating vehicles in a metropolitan area, real-time coverage would be sparse outside major highway corridors during peak hours. Rural roads would have essentially no network benefit.

What You Can Do

If you are an entrepreneur or engineer in the automotive accessories space: the entire system architecture is public domain. You cannot be sued for building it. The hardest part is the training data pipeline for police vehicle detection, and the disclosure describes exactly how to build it, including synthetic data generation using game engines to cover rare configurations and adverse conditions. Start with a dashcam plus a Hailo-8 accelerator module and a Bluetooth-connected Valentine One for radar data. Ship the vision-only system first, add fusion later.

If you are a radar detector owner: your device cannot detect a police vehicle that is not transmitting. It cannot detect pacing, VASCAR, or aircraft enforcement. It cannot distinguish between a police Explorer and a civilian one. A camera-based system can do all three. The next upgrade worth making is not a better radar detector. It is a different category of device entirely.

If you work in privacy advocacy: the disclosure's edge-first architecture is worth studying as a model for how surveillance-adjacent technology can be designed to collect nothing by default. Every principle that makes this system privacy-preserving — local-only inference, transient ALPR lookups with immediate discard of non-matches, anonymized network reporting with rotating pseudonyms, no data sharing with law enforcement — is transferable to other sensor platforms that could easily become surveillance tools.

The Bottom Line

The radar detector industry has spent thirty years refining a single sensing modality that fundamentally cannot detect a police vehicle running instant-on radar, lidar, pacing, or no speed measurement at all. A public domain disclosure published one year ago describes how to fix every one of those blind spots by fusing AI computer vision, RF detection, ALPR, and automated crowdsourced spotting into a single platform with a combined false positive rate below 0.1 percent. The hardware to build it costs less than a premium radar detector. The software architecture is fully specified and free to use. The only thing missing is someone willing to build it. The three companies with the distribution channels to ship it at scale have not, and based on their engineering hiring patterns, cannot.
