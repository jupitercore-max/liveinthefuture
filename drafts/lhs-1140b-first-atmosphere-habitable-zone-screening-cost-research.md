## Core News Event
On July 16, 2026, a paper published in *Science* by Collin Cherubim et al. (Harvard) reported the first observationally confirmed atmosphere on a rocky exoplanet in the habitable zone: **LHS 1140 b**. The detection used the Magellan Clay telescope (ground-based, 6.5m) at Las Campanas Observatory, Chile — not JWST.

## Original Contribution (Kill Test)
**The Atmospheric Screening Cost Hierarchy** — a calculation showing what it costs to answer increasingly harder questions about exoplanet habitability, applied to the full catalog of 45 known rocky habitable-zone planets:

| Level | Question | Method | Cost per planet | Total for 45 planets |
|-------|----------|--------|----------------|---------------------|
| 1 | Does atmosphere exist? | Ground-based helium spectroscopy (1083nm) | ~$10K | ~$450K |
| 2 | What's it made of? | JWST transmission spectroscopy (20+ hrs) | ~$500K–$2M | ~$22–90M |
| 3 | Are there biosignatures? | ELT/HWO (hundreds of hours) | ~$5–50M | Only top ~5–10 targets feasible |
| 4 | Is it actually habitable? | Direct imaging, next-gen missions | Unknown | Decades away |

Each level is 10–50× more expensive than the last. The bottleneck isn't finding planets — it's characterizing them.

Secondary original analysis: **The ELT scaling calculation** — the ELT (39m, first light ~2028) has 36× the collecting area of Magellan Clay (6.5m). For the same SNR in the same integration time, it could detect helium on stars √36 ≈ 6× fainter, extending the screening horizon from ~50 ly to ~300 ly. The known rocky habitable-zone catalog would grow from 45 to potentially hundreds of targets.

## Primary Sources

### Source 1: Cherubim et al. 2026 (Science)
- Published July 16, 2026 in *Science*
- Lead author: Collin Cherubim, Harvard (recently earned PhD in Earth and Planetary Sciences)
- Co-author: Robin Wordsworth (Harvard)
- Telescope: Magellan Clay (6.5m), Las Campanas Observatory, Chile
- Method: Near-infrared absorption spectroscopy targeting metastable helium at 1083nm
- Observation: 6.5 hours total, once in 2024 and once in 2025
- Result: Helium escaping from atmosphere detected in 2024 data; NOT detected in 2025 data
- Interpretation: First confirmed atmosphere on a rocky planet in the habitable zone; consistent with Cherubim's prediction of a "helium world" — a new class of planet
- Key quote (Cherubim): "This is the first time anyone has found an atmosphere on a rocky planet in the habitable zone of another star."
- Key quote (Wordsworth): "Twenty years ago we wondered whether other terrestrial-type planets even existed. Then we learned they're common, and found some in the habitable zone. The next question was whether any of them had managed to keep an atmosphere. Now we know at least one has."

### Source 2: Cadieux et al. 2024 (JWST/NIRISS)
- JWST NIRISS transmission spectroscopy (0.65–2.7μm), 2 transits
- Ruled out hydrogen-rich atmosphere for LHS 1140 b
- Found tentative evidence of N2-dominated atmosphere (or possibly CO2-dominated)
- Also consistent with water world scenario with large water-mass fraction

### Source 3: Damiano et al. 2024 (JWST/NIRSpec)
- JWST NIRSpec transmission spectroscopy (1.7–5.2μm), 2 transits
- Program ID: 2334, PI: M. Damiano
- Also ruled out H2-rich atmosphere
- Favored high mean molecular weight atmosphere (possibly N2 with H2O and CO2)
- Predicted CO2 absorption at 4.2μm with ~20 ppm signal

### Source 4: Bohl et al. 2026 (Cornell/Kaltenegger)
- "Probing the Limits of Habitability: A Catalogue of Rocky Exoplanets in the Habitable Zone"
- Published March 19, 2026 in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society
- Used Gaia DR3 + NASA Exoplanet Archive
- Result: **45 rocky worlds in the empirical habitable zone, 24 in narrower 3D-HZ**
- Prioritized targets for JWST, ELT, HWO, and LIFE
- LHS 1140 b is one of these 45

### Source 5: NASA Exoplanet Archive
- As of July 2, 2026: **6,316 confirmed exoplanets**
- LHS 1140 b: discovered 2017 by Dittmann et al.

## Planet Properties (LHS 1140 b)
- Radius: 1.73 R_Earth (70% larger than Earth)
- Mass: 5.6 M_Earth
- Density: 5.9 g/cm³ (Earth: 5.5 g/cm³) — rocky composition
- Orbital period: 24.74 days
- Semi-major axis: 0.0946 AU
- Equilibrium temperature: ~226K (with A=0.3)
- Distance: ~49 light-years (~15 parsecs), constellation Cetus (the Whale)
- Tidally locked ("Eyeball planet" — one hemisphere always faces star)
- Bolometric irradiance: ~0.43 S_Earth
- Discovered: 2017 (Dittmann et al.)
- **In the canonical liquid water habitable zone**
- Possible liquid ocean on illuminated hemisphere; mostly frozen water if present

## Star Properties (LHS 1140)
- Type: M4.5V red dwarf
- 5× smaller than the Sun
- ~0.5% as bright as the Sun
- Most common star type in the galaxy
- High X-ray/extreme UV radiation relative to total energy (strips atmospheres)
- One of the closest known stars hosting a habitable-zone rocky planet

## Key Scientific Context

### The vanishing helium mystery
- Helium detected in 2024, NOT detected in 2025 (same telescope, same method)
- Separately confirmed: independent team reanalyzed all data, result held
- Not unheard of: helium escape measurements on other planets have shown variability
- Possible explanations: stellar activity shifts, outflow geometry changes, helium still there but not in excited state
- Quote (Cherubim): "When you make a measurement of escaping helium and then you go back and look at it months and years later, it is usually different and sometimes it isn't there — and we don't understand it."

### "Helium world" — a new class of planet
- Cherubim's model predicts helium-dominated atmosphere with water, CO, CO2, small amounts of O2
- Not like anything in our solar system
- Could be more similar to a gas giant than to Earth/Mars/Venus in atmospheric structure
- Quote (Cherubim): "The new data from my study are consistent with my prediction that it's a new class of planet: a helium world."
- Quote (Sukrit Ranjan, U. Arizona): "It's exciting on its own merits, not as a new Earth. It might be habitable, but not in a conventional sense."
- Quote (Natalie Allen, Johns Hopkins): "Earth and Venus are very similar in size and both have atmospheres, but Venus's surface isn't habitable. I think to have a real picture of habitability on this planet, we need to understand the bulk atmosphere."

### M-dwarf atmospheric stripping
- M dwarfs emit much higher X-ray/EUV radiation relative to luminosity
- This strips atmospheres from orbiting rocky planets
- Major open question: CAN rocky planets retain atmospheres around M dwarfs?
- TRAPPIST-1 planets (also M-dwarf): JWST has found no clear atmospheres
- LHS 1140 b's atmosphere detection is significant partly because it survived M-dwarf radiation

### Previous work on LHS 1140 b
- 2017: Discovery (Dittmann et al.)
- 2024: JWST/NIRISS — ruled out H2-rich, tentative N2 (Cadieux et al.)
- 2024: JWST/NIRSpec — water world scenario favored (Damiano et al.)
- 2025: Rochon et al. — LHS 1140 c (different planet in same system) has NO atmosphere
- 2026: Cherubim et al. — first confirmed atmosphere via ground-based helium detection

### Unpublished JWST data
- As-yet-unpublished JWST data on LHS 1140 b exists that "could help clear up" bulk atmosphere composition (per Natalie Allen, New Scientist)

## Original Calculations

### 1. The Screening Hierarchy Cost
**Level 1: Atmosphere existence (ground-based helium 1083nm)**
- Magellan Clay: 6.5 hours observation time
- Large ground-based telescope time ≈ $1,000–$2,000/hour (consortium rates)
- Total cost: ~$6,500–$13,000 per planet
- Conservative estimate: $10,000 per planet
- For all 45 rocky HZ planets: 45 × $10,000 = **$450,000**
- That's less than a single JWST orbit's amortized cost

**Level 2: Bulk atmospheric composition (JWST transmission spectroscopy)**
- JWST annual operating cost: ~$170M/year
- Science time: ~6,500 hours/year
- Cost per hour: ~$26,000 (operating only), ~$77,000 (lifecycle amortized over 20 years)
- LHS 1140 b required at least 4 transit observations (2 NIRISS + 2 NIRSpec) = 20–32 hours
- Cost per planet: ~$520,000–$2.5M
- For top 10 rocky HZ targets: **$5–25M**
- Note: Not all 45 targets are bright enough for JWST transmission spectroscopy

**Level 3: Biosignature detection (O2, O3, CH4)**
- Requires much higher SNR than Level 2
- Atmospheric scale height on LHS 1140 b: H = kT/(μg)
  - T ≈ 226K, μ ≈ 28 amu (N2-dominated), g ≈ 19 m/s²
  - H ≈ 3,530 m ≈ 3.5 km (Earth: 8.5 km)
- Expected O2 signal: ~10–20 ppm (vs. ~100–200 ppm transit depth)
- Would require dozens to hundreds of transits with JWST
- Each transit: ~75 minutes of in-transit time, every 24.74 days
- 100 transits = 100 × 25 days = ~7 years of monitoring just for one planet
- Only feasible for ~5–10 nearest/brightest targets even with next-gen telescopes

### 2. ELT Scaling
- ELT mirror: 39.3m diameter
- Magellan Clay: 6.5m diameter
- Collecting area ratio: (39.3/6.5)² = 36.5×
- For same integration time and target brightness: SNR improves by √36.5 ≈ 6×
- For same SNR: can observe stars (6)² = 36× fainter → ~6× more distant
- Current helium screening reach: ~50 ly (LHS 1140 b is at 49 ly)
- ELT reach: ~300 ly
- Volume of space scales as distance³: (300/50)³ = 216×
- Approximate M-dwarf density in solar neighborhood: ~0.07 per cubic parsec
- Within 15 pc: ~1,000 M-dwarfs; within 90 pc: ~48,000 M-dwarfs
- At Kepler occurrence rate ~0.16 rocky HZ planets per M-dwarf
- Current catalog (within ~50 ly): 45 rocky HZ planets known
- With ELT + better surveys: potentially 200–1,000+ targets accessible
- ELT first light: ~2028; ANDES spectrograph: ~2030
- GMT (25.4m) first light: ~2029
- TMT (30m): timeline uncertain

### 3. Cost-per-answer comparison
- **Does atmosphere exist?** $10K on existing telescope (proven July 2026)
- **What's it made of?** $500K–$2M on JWST (partial answers so far for LHS 1140 b)
- **Are there biosignatures?** $5–50M equivalent, 7+ years of monitoring, only for nearest targets
- **Is it habitable?** Unknown cost, likely requires space-based direct imaging missions (HWO: $11B estimated)
- The ratio: Level 1 is 50–200× cheaper than Level 2, which is 10–25× cheaper than Level 3
- **The gap**: We can screen for existence cheaply, but characterizing what we find is the bottleneck

## Limitations & Counterarguments
1. **Single detection, non-reproducible**: Helium appeared in 2024 but not 2025. A single positive detection (even if robust) isn't the same as a repeated, replicable result.
2. **Helium ≠ habitability**: Knowing helium is escaping tells you an atmosphere exists, but says nothing about whether conditions at the surface support life. Venus has an atmosphere too.
3. **"Helium world" may not be habitable**: If the atmosphere is helium-dominated, surface pressures could be extreme (like ocean floors). Not conventionally habitable.
4. **M-dwarf bias**: Most rocky HZ planets we know orbit M-dwarfs (because they're easier to detect). This biases our catalog toward planets that may be atypical due to tidal locking, stellar activity, etc.
5. **Cost estimates are rough**: Telescope time costs vary enormously by consortium, allocation method, and accounting (marginal vs. full lifecycle). The $10K vs. $500K comparison is illustrative, not precise.
6. **Not all 45 are screenable**: Some rocky HZ planets orbit stars too faint or too distant for current helium detection. The $450K total is theoretical.
