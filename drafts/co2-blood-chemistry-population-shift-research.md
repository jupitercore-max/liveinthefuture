# Research Notes: CO2 Blood Chemistry Population Shift

## Story Angle
CO2 isn't just warming the planet. For the first time, a peer-reviewed study analyzing 21 years of U.S. population blood data shows atmospheric CO2 is detectably changing human blood chemistry at the population level. The compounding factor: indoor CO2 levels are already 2-4x outdoor, meaning the cognitive and physiological effects are arriving faster than the atmosphere alone would suggest.

## Primary Sources

### Source 1: Larcombe & Bierwirth (2026)
- **Title:** "Carbon dioxide overload, detected in human blood, suggests a potentially toxic atmosphere within 50 years"
- **Journal:** Air Quality, Atmosphere & Health (Springer Nature)
- **DOI:** 10.1007/s11869-026-01918-5
- **Published:** February 2026
- **Authors:** Alexander N. Larcombe (The Kids Research Institute Australia, Curtin University) & Phil N. Bierwirth (ANU)
- **Method:** Analyzed NHANES data 1999-2020, ~7,000 people every 2 years
- **Key findings:**
  - Serum bicarbonate (HCO3-) rose ~7% since 1999
  - Blood calcium and phosphorus DECLINED over same period
  - Changes parallel atmospheric CO2 rise (369 ppm in 2000 → 420+ ppm now)
  - At current trends: bicarbonate at limit of healthy range in ~50 years
  - Ca and P at limit of healthy ranges by end of century
- **Quote (Larcombe):** "What we're seeing is a gradual shift in blood chemistry that mirrors the rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide which is driving climate change."
- **Quote (Bierwirth):** "I actually think that what we are seeing is because our bodies are not adapting... As CO2 in the air is now higher than humans have ever experienced, it appears to be building up in our bodies."
- **Context:** Humans evolved at 280-300 ppm CO2. Current: 420+ ppm. Annual increase: ~2.6 ppm/yr (2024 saw 3.5 ppm rise).

### Source 2: Allen et al. (2016) — Harvard COGfx Study
- **Title:** "Associations of Cognitive Function Scores with Carbon Dioxide, Ventilation, and Volatile Organic Compound Exposures in Office Workers"
- **Journal:** Environmental Health Perspectives, Vol. 124, No. 6
- **DOI:** 10.1289/ehp.1510037
- **Key findings:** Cognitive function scores were significantly lower at 1,000 ppm CO2 compared to 600 ppm. Crisis response, information usage, and strategy scores all declined.
- **Relevance:** Indoor CO2 levels are already routinely 800-1200 ppm in offices and schools. This is the cognitive threshold data.

### Source 3: Scripps/NOAA Mauna Loa CO2 Data
- Seasonal peak exceeded 430 ppm (2025)
- Annual average: ~425 ppm (2025-2026)
- Average annual increase: 2.6 ppm/year over past decade
- 2024 recorded 3.5 ppm rise (highest annual jump)
- At 2.6 ppm/yr: hits 450 ppm by ~2036, 500 ppm by ~2055

### Source 4: GRL Warming Acceleration Study (March 2026)
- Published in Geophysical Research Letters
- Global warming rate nearly doubled: 0.2°C → 0.35°C per decade since 2015
- 1.5°C threshold could be breached before 2030
- Supports: CO2 rise isn't slowing, it's accelerating

### Source 5: Harvard Global CogFx Study (2021)
- 302 office workers in 6 countries
- 0.8-0.9% slower response times for every 10 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5
- Reduced throughput (speed and accuracy) with decreased ventilation
- Shows cognitive effects are measurable and global

## Original Calculation: The Indoor Amplification Problem

### Outdoor → Indoor CO2 Amplification
- Outdoor CO2 (2026): ~425 ppm
- Typical indoor CO2: outdoor + 400-800 ppm (depending on occupancy and ventilation)
  - Well-ventilated office: outdoor + 200-400 ppm = 625-825 ppm
  - Poorly ventilated office: outdoor + 600-800 ppm = 1025-1225 ppm
  - Classroom with 30 students: outdoor + 800-1200 ppm = 1225-1625 ppm
  - Bedroom (closed windows): outdoor + 600-1000 ppm = 1025-1425 ppm

### Projection to 2050
- Outdoor CO2 (2050): ~490 ppm at 2.6 ppm/yr
- Well-ventilated office (2050): 690-890 ppm
- Poorly ventilated office (2050): 1090-1290 ppm (above cognitive decline threshold)
- Classroom (2050): 1290-1690 ppm (well above threshold)
- Bedroom (2050): 1090-1490 ppm

### The Key Insight
Indoor environments where people spend 90% of their time are already at or near cognitive decline thresholds today. By 2050, even well-ventilated buildings will approach the threshold. Poorly ventilated spaces — schools, apartments, developing world offices — will chronically exceed it. The blood chemistry data from Larcombe shows this isn't just about acute cognitive effects; the body is accumulating CO2 at a population level.

## Strongest Counterargument
The body has robust buffering mechanisms. Kidneys compensate for acid-base shifts. The 7% bicarbonate rise could be within normal adaptive capacity. Correlation ≠ causation: other factors (diet changes, obesity trends, medication use) could explain part of the NHANES shift. The study authors themselves note they "do not prove direct causation."

## Limitations
- NHANES is U.S.-only; other populations may show different trends
- No individual-level atmospheric exposure data — population-level correlation
- Indoor CO2 amplification estimates use published ranges, not measured NHANES-participant data
- The 50-year projection assumes linear trends; policy action could change trajectory
- Cognitive decline data (Allen 2016) measured acute exposure, not chronic adaptation
