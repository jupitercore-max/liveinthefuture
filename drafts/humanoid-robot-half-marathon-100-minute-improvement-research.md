## Novel Contribution
**Original calculation nobody has run:** Compare the annual improvement rate of humanoid robot half-marathon performance against the century-long improvement rate of human half-marathon world records.
- Robot: 2:40:42 (2025) → 50:26 (2026) = 68.7% pace improvement in 1 year
- Human men's half-marathon WR: ~67:00 (1920s era) → 57:20 (Kiplimo, 2021) = ~14.4% improvement over ~100 years
- Human men's half-marathon WR recent: 58:01 (Kamworor, 2019) → 57:20 (Kiplimo, 2021) = 1.2% improvement in 2 years
- The robot's single-year improvement exceeds a century of human progress by 4.8×

**Second calculation:** Cost-per-unit analysis — Honor reportedly spent $10B over 5 years (Alpha Plan), and Unitree ships humanoids for $16,000 (G1). If a smartphone company can build a world-class locomotion system in 12 months, what does that mean for the incumbent robotics companies burning $100M+ per year?

## Strongest Counterargument
Running fast on flat pavement is a solved problem for wheeled and quadruped robots. Bipedal locomotion on pavement is an engineering demonstration, not a commercially useful capability. The real test is unstructured environments — stairs, rubble, uneven terrain. Lightning's LiDAR-based navigation and stripped-down design (no head, hands, or eyes) shows it was optimized for this single benchmark. The improvement rate is impressive for marathon times but doesn't map cleanly to factory or delivery tasks where manipulation, not locomotion, is the bottleneck.

## Limitations
- No independent verification of autonomous status (self-reported by race organizers)
- No technical specs published (battery capacity, motor torque, total energy consumption)
- No cost data for Lightning specifically
- Human WR comparison is inexact (different eras, different sport maturity, biological vs mechanical)
- 2025 comparison is partially unfair (2025 was the first-ever event, 2026 had 14× more entries and institutional investment)
