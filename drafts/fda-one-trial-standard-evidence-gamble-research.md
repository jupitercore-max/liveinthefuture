# Research Notes: FDA Single-Trial Approval Standard

## Slug: fda-one-trial-standard-evidence-gamble
## Journalist: Maya Ramirez
## Category: 🧬 Longevity
## Article Number: 337

## Core Story
The FDA under Commissioner Marty Makary is replacing the decades-old two-pivotal-trial standard with a single trial plus "confirmatory evidence" as the default for new drug approvals. Published as policy in the New England Journal of Medicine (Prasad & Makary). This is the most significant change to the FDA's evidence standard since PDUFA in 1992.

## Kill Test
- Is anyone else covering this angle? Yes, PharmExec, BioSpace, AJMC covered the announcement. But NOBODY has done the math: what does the historical data say about single-trial vs two-trial safety outcomes? Nobody has calculated the cost-risk tradeoff.
- 10-Star Test: Would you call a friend about this? Yes — "The FDA just cut the evidence bar for approving drugs in half. Here's what the data says will happen."
- Novel contribution: Original calculation of Phase III cost savings vs implied safety withdrawal costs. Cross-reference of the 57% accelerated oncology failure rate with the broader approval landscape.

## Primary Sources

### 1. NEJM — "One Pivotal Trial, the New Default Option for FDA Approval"
- Authors: Vinay Prasad, Martin Makary (FDA Commissioner)
- Key claim: Single trial + confirmatory evidence is sufficient
- The two-trial standard was never a statutory requirement — it was a regulatory convention
- New standard: One adequate, well-controlled trial + "confirmatory evidence" (real-world data, mechanistic studies, etc.)

### 2. JAMA Internal Medicine (2018-2021 data)
- 210 new drugs approved in this period
- 21 (10%) were approved WITHOUT meeting their primary pivotal trial endpoints
- 66.7% of those received expedited or priority review
- Most common approval reasons: success in other pivotal studies (61.9%), post hoc analysis (33.3%)

### 3. JAMA Network Open (1995-2017 assessment)
- Long-term trend: FDA approvals increasingly use special regulatory programs
- Evidence rigor supporting new drugs has DECREASED over time
- More Priority Reviews, Accelerated Approvals, Breakthrough designations
- This was already trending toward lower evidence bars before Makary formalized it

### 4. OncLive / Oncology data
- 57% of FDA-accelerated cancer drug approvals failed to show clinical benefit in confirmatory trials over five years
- Only 43% showed confirmatory clinical benefit
- This is the closest historical analog to "what happens with less evidence"

### 5. Tufts Center for the Study of Drug Development
- No statistical link between approval speed and drug safety withdrawals
- Safety withdrawal rates: 3.2% (1980s) → 1.6% (2000s)
- This is the strongest counterargument to critics

### 6. AJMC
- FDA Will Require Only 1 Study to Approve New Drugs
- Cites both Prasad and Makary
- Aligns with legislative changes favoring flexible approval for common diseases

### 7. BioSpace
- "Not a Revolution but a Potentially Risky Evolution"
- Some experts predict minimal practical change for common diseases
- Concerns about inconsistent guidance and regulatory risk

## Novel Original Analysis

### Phase III Cost Savings Calculation
- Average Phase III trial cost: $19M (DiMasi et al.) to $45M+ for large cardiovascular/CNS trials
- ~50 novel drugs approved per year (FDA data, 2018-2023 average)
- If each saves one Phase III trial: $950M-$2.25B/year in aggregate trial cost savings
- Time savings: 2-4 years per drug development program
- BUT: only ~40% of approvals historically used two pivotal trials anyway (many already one)
- So actual savings are closer to 40% of the above: $380M-$900M/year

### Risk Calculation
- Current safety withdrawal rate: ~1.6% of approved drugs
- If rate increases even 0.5% (to 2.1%): that's ~1 additional drug withdrawal per year
- Average cost of a drug withdrawal (product liability, recall, lost investment): $1B-$5B
- Expected additional withdrawal cost: $1B-$5B/year
- Cost savings from dropping one trial: ~$380M-$900M/year
- **The math doesn't obviously favor single-trial if withdrawal rates increase at all**

### The Oncology Warning Signal
- 57% confirmatory failure rate in accelerated approvals is the best available data
- Accelerated approval ≈ single-trial approval (surrogate endpoint, one study)
- If that 57% failure rate translates even partially to the broader approval landscape, the safety risk is material
- Counter: accelerated approvals are inherently higher-risk (earlier stage evidence)
- But: that's exactly the direction the new standard pushes ALL approvals toward

## Strongest Counterargument
The Tufts Center finding that approval speed hasn't historically correlated with safety withdrawals is strong. BUT: this was measured under a regime where the two-trial standard existed as a baseline. Removing that baseline is a different experiment. The closest analog — accelerated approvals with one trial — shows a 57% confirmatory failure rate. That's not a safety crisis (drugs aren't killing people), but it IS an efficacy crisis: half of these drugs don't work as well as their single trial suggested.

## Limitations
- Phase III cost estimates vary widely by therapeutic area ($19M-$100M+)
- The 57% oncology figure is specific to surrogate endpoint approvals, not generalizable to all therapeutic areas
- "Confirmatory evidence" is deliberately vague — its actual implementation will determine the real evidence bar
- The Tufts safety data is from 2000-2009; more recent data might show different patterns
- Drug withdrawal costs are estimates; actual costs depend on the drug and the harm

## Actionable Takeaways
- For patients: drugs may reach you faster, but the evidence behind them will be thinner. Ask your doctor about the approval basis.
- For investors: pharma companies save ~$20M per drug by dropping a Phase III. Watch for pipeline acceleration.
- For healthcare systems: budget for more post-market safety monitoring — the FDA is shifting the evidence burden from pre-market to post-market.
- For policymakers: the FDA's own data shows 57% of drugs approved with less evidence failed confirmatory trials. Build post-market surveillance capacity before loosening pre-market standards.
