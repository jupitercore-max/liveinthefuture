# Research Notes: $30M Startup Spacecraft vs $2B Government Program

## Core Story
On July 3, 2026, at 4:36 AM ET, Katalyst Space Technologies launched LINK — a 399-kilogram robotic spacecraft — aboard a Northrop Grumman Pegasus XL rocket from Kwajalein Atoll in the Marshall Islands. The mission: rescue NASA's $500 million Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory from an uncontrolled atmospheric reentry. 

This is the first time a commercial robotic spacecraft has attempted to capture an uncrewed government satellite not designed for servicing.

Meanwhile, NASA's own in-house satellite servicing program (OSAM-1/Restore-L) spent $2 billion over 10 years and was cancelled in March 2024 without ever launching.

## Primary Sources

### Source 1: NASA contract award (Sept 2025)
- NASA.gov announcement: $30M SBIR Phase III contract to Katalyst Space Technologies
- Purpose: develop and launch LINK to dock with Swift and boost its orbit
- Competitors beaten: Starfish Space; joint venture of Cambrian Works and Astroscale
- Quote from NASA official Shawn Domagal-Goldman: "Given how quickly Swift's orbit is decaying, we are in a race against the clock"
- "This is a forward-leaning, risk-tolerant approach for NASA."

### Source 2: OSAM-1 cancellation (March 2024)
- NASA cancelled after "continued technical, cost, and schedule challenges"
- Original name: Restore-L (started ~2014)
- Original cost estimate: $626-753 million
- Final cost at cancellation: over $2 billion (per NASA OIG October 2023 report)
- Workers: ~450 NASA employees and contractors at Goddard Space Flight Center
- Congress appropriated ~$1.5 billion; total invested ~$2 billion
- Original launch target: 2020; slipped to at least 2026 before cancellation
- NASA Inspector General blamed poor performance by contractor Maxar

### Source 3: Reuters launch coverage (July 3, 2026)
- LINK successfully launched, reached orbit, communications confirmed
- 880 pounds (399 kg), about 1/3 of Swift's 3,200 lbs (1,452 kg)
- 5 feet tall, ~20 feet of solar panels, 3 robotic arms with hand-like grippers
- Month-long voyage to Swift vicinity
- By late July: fly to within 6 miles (9.6 km) of Swift, then final approach
- ~1 week for rendezvous and capture using robotic arms
- ~60 days to tow from ~210 miles to ~373 miles (600 km)
- Design, build, test in 9 months

### Source 4: Wikipedia - Swift rescue mission
- LINK launched July 3, 2026 (confirmed)
- First commercial spacecraft to dock with government-owned spacecraft not designed for servicing
- Swift has no propulsion, no docking interface, 20+ years of insulation degradation
- Swift's orbit decayed from 600 km to ~338 km since 2004 launch
- Solar maximum 2024 accelerated decay
- Mission cost is $30 million, fully funded by contract including launch

### Source 5: Northrop Grumman MEV-1/MEV-2 data
- MEV-1 docked with Intelsat IS-901 in 2020 (first commercial in-orbit docking)
- Intelsat paid ~$13 million/year for life extension service
- $65M total for 5 years of service
- Replacement satellite would cost $300M+
- MEV-2 docked with Intelsat 10-02 in 2021 directly in GEO
- MEVs designed for up to 15 years of service, can be reused

### Source 6: On-orbit satellite servicing market data
- Market size: $2.79B (2025) → $3.09B (2026) → $5.79B by 2032 (11% CAGR)
- Alternative estimate: $2.4B (2023) → $5.1B by 2030 (11.5% CAGR)
- Orbital repair sub-market: $1.81B (2025) → $4.39B by 2030 (19.3% CAGR)
- Key players: Northrop Grumman (only proven GEO provider), Astroscale, Starfish Space, ClearSpace, Orbit Fab, D-Orbit
- Active satellites: 9,000+ (2024) → expected 60,000+ by 2030

### Source 7: Swift Observatory value and science
- $500M total mission cost (build + launch + operations since 2004)
- Key gamma-ray burst observatory — discovers ~100 GRBs/year
- No equivalent capability exists; no replacement mission planned
- Science operations suspended since February 2026
- If rescue succeeds, science could resume fall 2026 with decades more life

## Original Calculations

### 1. OSAM-1 vs LINK Cost Efficiency
- OSAM-1: $2,000M spent, 0 missions completed
- LINK: $30M, 1 mission launched
- **Cost ratio: 66.7×** — NASA's in-house program was 67× more expensive before cancellation
- OSAM-1 cost overrun: grew from $626-753M estimate to $2B+ = **2.7-3.2× original budget**

### 2. Time Compression
- OSAM-1: ~10 years (2014-2024), never launched
- LINK: 9 months from contract (Sept 2025) to launch (July 2026)
- **Speed ratio: 13.3×** faster

### 3. Cost-to-Save Ratio (Asset Preservation Efficiency)
- LINK: $30M to save $500M asset = **6.0% of asset value**
- MEV-1: $65M to save $300M+ replacement = **21.7% of replacement cost**
- LINK is **3.6× more capital-efficient** than even the proven MEV approach
- But MEV is GEO (higher orbit, more expensive to reach) vs LINK is LEO

### 4. OSAM-1 Cost Per Worker-Year
- $2B / (10 years × 450 workers) = **~$444,000 per worker-year**
- This includes NASA overhead, Maxar contractor costs, Goddard center costs

### 5. What If Servicing Scales?
- 9,000+ active satellites today; 60,000+ by 2030
- Average government satellite replacement cost: $200M-500M (LEO) to $300-500M (GEO)
- If just 0.5% of 60,000 satellites (300 satellites) need servicing per year at $30M average:
  - Market = 300 × $30M = **$9B/year** — higher than most market forecasts
  - But not all missions would be $30M; many would be simpler/cheaper
- At current 9,000 satellites, 1% = 90 satellites × $30M = $2.7B — matches current market size

### 6. The "Tow Truck" Economics
- Swift without propulsion: would have burned up by late 2026
- LINK boost from ~338 km to ~600 km: doubles orbital altitude
- At 600 km, atmospheric drag is minimal — decades more life
- $30M for decades of additional science vs $500M+ for replacement
- **Return on investment: 16.7× to 1** ($500M saved / $30M spent)

## Dual-Use / National Security Angle
- "The U.S. Space Command cares a lot about this" — Katalyst CEO Ghonhee Lee to Reuters
- China demonstrated satellite grappling in 2022 (one satellite grabbed and moved another)
- China demonstrated close-proximity satellite ops in 2025
- LINK proves US commercial capability to approach, capture, and manipulate uncooperative satellites
- This is exactly what you'd need for satellite defense — or offense

## Strongest Counterargument
- Katalyst has never flown a mission (though acquired Atomos Space, which flew a problematic demo in March 2024)
- The hardest part — capture of an uncooperative, deteriorating satellite — hasn't happened yet
- OSAM-1 was more ambitious (refueling + assembly + manufacturing demonstration)
- Comparing a single orbit-boost mission to a multi-capability demonstration isn't entirely apples-to-apples
- But the cost disparity is so extreme (67×) that even adjusting for scope difference, the efficiency gap is enormous

## Limitations
- LINK has only launched; capture and boost are months away and unproven
- OSAM-1's cost included SPIDER robotic assembly payload, Ka-band antenna assembly — broader scope than just orbit boost
- $30M may not be the "real" cost — Katalyst was already building a demo mission and redirected it
- MEV revenue model ($13M/year) is for GEO servicing with more mature technology
- Market size estimates vary enormously ($2.4B to $9B) depending on assumptions

## Key Quote
"We're doing this on a time scale that's kind of crazy by space standards" — Brad Cenko, NASA Goddard, Swift principal investigator
