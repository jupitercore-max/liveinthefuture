# Research: Fusion's Biggest Week — Physics Validated, Billions Raised, and the $22 Billion Question Nobody's Asking

## Thesis
On June 4, 2026, fusion energy hit two milestones that would have been inconceivable five years ago: 58 scientists from MIT, Columbia, and Max Planck peer-reviewed the physics of a commercial fusion reactor and declared it workable, while a separate company's valuation nearly tripled to $15.5 billion on a $465 million raise. But the celebration masks the real question — not whether fusion works (the physics was never the hard part), but whether it can be built on time and on budget. On that question, fusion has a $22 billion cautionary tale named ITER.

## Novel Contribution

**First-of-its-kind comparative cost analysis of fusion vs. existing energy infrastructure:**

CFS claims ARC will cost $2.5B for 400 MW = $6,250/kW. If true, this beats Vogtle-era nuclear fission by 2.5×. But ITER — the world's only large-scale tokamak construction project — went from $5B to $22B (4.4× overrun). If CFS hits even half of ITER's overrun rate (2.2×), ARC becomes $5.5B = $13,750/kW — right back to Vogtle territory.

**The real math nobody's running:** Total private fusion funding is ~$8-9B across all companies. CFS's first plant alone costs $2.5B+. Helion's Orion is separately funded. The industry has collectively raised enough money to build roughly 2-3 first-of-a-kind plants. First-of-a-kind ALWAYS costs more than projected. The fusion industry needs ~$30-50B more before a single commercial MWh is sold. Where does that money come from?

**Physics vs. engineering gap:** CFS published peer-reviewed physics validation. Helion explicitly refuses to publish. Both are valued in billions. The market is pricing fusion based on narrative, not on the only variable that matters: construction risk.

## Strongest Counterargument

The strongest case FOR fusion right now: these are not the same companies as ITER. ITER is a 35-nation government project with 7 domestic agencies, each building components separately and shipping them to France. CFS is one company, one site, one design, one supply chain. ITER's cost overruns are largely attributable to multinational bureaucracy, not physics or engineering difficulty. A private company with $3B and a single Virginia site might genuinely build for $6,250/kW. SpaceX vs. SLS is the analogy fusion advocates use — and they're not entirely wrong.

Also: CFS's HTS magnets are genuinely new. ITER uses low-temperature superconducting magnets that require cooling to 4 Kelvin (−269°C). CFS's magnets operate at ~20 Kelvin using REBCO tape — smaller, cheaper, more powerful. The magnet is the thing that makes a compact commercial tokamak possible, and CFS demonstrated this with their 20-tesla magnet test in 2021.
