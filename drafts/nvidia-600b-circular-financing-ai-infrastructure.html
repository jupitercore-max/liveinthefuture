Nvidia Is Financing Its Own Demand. The $600 Billion Number Should Scare You.

When the chip maker guarantees a quarter-trillion dollars in leases for facilities that exist to buy its chips, the risk isn't leverage — it's correlation.

Kai Nakamura · Tech Economics · July 29, 2026 · ☕ 9 min read

Six hundred billion dollars. That is roughly the combined value of the financing arrangements Nvidia is reportedly discussing with OpenAI for a single data center campus in southern Ohio. A $250 billion guarantee covers the facility's lease and debt. A separate $350 billion package would finance the purchase of Nvidia's own chips. No longer just selling shovels during a gold rush anymore. It is co-signing the mortgage on the mine. That changes the risk calculus for every stakeholder in the AI supply chain, from bondholders to grid operators to the taxpayers whose federal land underlies the project.

Reuters reported on July 26 that Nvidia is in talks to provide roughly $250 billion in financing guarantees for OpenAI as part of the SB Energy project. SoftBank's subsidiary developing what would become one of the largest single-site data centers ever conceived. It sits on the former Portsmouth Gaseous Diffusion Plant in Piketon, Ohio. A Cold War uranium enrichment site where centrifuges once spun weapons-grade uranium, now repurposed to power a different kind of arms race, one measured in floating-point operations per second rather than megatons.

Ten gigawatts is the planned capacity of the full campus, a number so large it strains every analogy available. To understand what that means, consider that the Grand Coulee Dam in Washington state, the largest power plant in the United States, generates 6.8 gigawatts. This single data center project would need 47 percent more power than America's mightiest dam. Ten gigawatts also represents 0.78 percent of the entire installed generating capacity of the United States, according to the Energy Information Administration's 2024 data showing roughly 1,290 gigawatts nationwide.

The Circular Guarantee Problem

Nvidia's annual revenue for the trailing twelve months sits near $130 billion. The $250 billion guarantee alone equals 1.9 times that figure. Add the $350 billion chip financing discussion and you reach $600 billion, about 4.6 times Nvidia's yearly revenue. For context, JPMorgan Chase, the largest bank in the United States, holds approximately $4 trillion in total assets and $700 billion in outstanding loans. Nvidia is not a bank. Not even close. It does not have a bank's capital reserves, stress testing infrastructure, or loss-absorption mechanisms. Yet it is reportedly contemplating bank-scale financial exposure to underwrite demand for its own products.

This is not the first time an industrial company tried this. General Electric built GE Capital into a $500-billion-plus financial arm that existed, in large part, to finance purchases of GE equipment. Jet engines, turbines, medical imaging machines. GE manufactured the product, GE Capital originated the loan, and for two decades the arrangement printed money so reliably that nobody questioned the circular logic underneath. The unit grew into GE's largest profit center. Then the 2008 financial crisis hit. GE Capital's losses nearly destroyed the parent company, forced a government bailout, and ultimately required GE to dismantle the financial unit over the following decade. Jeff Immelt, GE's CEO through the crisis, later called the financial arm "a ticking time bomb."

Why does the parallel matter? Because the risk is correlation, and correlation is the one thing diversification cannot fix. When a chip maker guarantees leases on facilities that exist to buy its chips, the same event — say, an AI revenue shortfall that makes hyperscaler spending cuts rational, triggers both the lease default and the chip demand collapse. Nvidia would be exposed on the guarantee side and the revenue side simultaneously. This is double-correlated risk. Traditional lenders avoid it by diversifying their loan books across uncorrelated industries.

The Power Math Nobody Wants to Do

Set aside the financing structure for a moment. The physical infrastructure demands are staggering on their own. Microsoft projects the U.S. AI sector will need 50 gigawatts of new capacity by 2028. Anthropic has estimated that a single frontier model training run could require five gigawatts by 2027. Current U.S. data center power consumption is roughly 35 gigawatts.

Let me run the arithmetic that makes infrastructure planners uncomfortable. In 2025, the United States added approximately 25 gigawatts of new generation capacity across all sources: solar, wind, natural gas, nuclear. If Microsoft's 50-gigawatt estimate is even half right, the AI industry alone needs to build more capacity in three years than the entire country built in the last two. And Microsoft is just one buyer.

Look at the announced pipeline. Across four Big Tech companies, specifically Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta, or proposed nuclear capacity alone exceeds 10 gigawatts, with collective investment surpassing $50 billion. Microsoft restarted Three Mile Island Unit 1. Google signed the first corporate deal for small modular reactors with Kairos Power, targeting 500 megawatts by 2035. Amazon bought 1.9 gigawatts from Talen Energy's Susquehanna nuclear plant and invested $500 million in X-energy. Meta issued a request for proposals seeking one to four gigawatts of new nuclear generation.

Not speculative commitments but signed contracts and active construction projects that are already breaking ground. The Savannah River Site in South Carolina, a former nuclear weapons complex, is being developed into a one-gigawatt AI data center with two gigawatts of onsite generation, natural gas bridging to nuclear. AMD and Core Scientific announced a partnership covering 2.5 gigawatts of data center capacity just two days ago.

What $600 Billion in Chips Looks Like

At Nvidia's current pricing for its B200 GPUs, roughly $30,000 to $40,000 per unit depending on configuration and volume, $350 billion in chip purchases buys approximately 9 to 11 million GPUs. A 10-gigawatt facility running at three kilowatts per GPU could power roughly 3.3 million GPUs simultaneously. So the chip financing discussion potentially covers three full buildouts of the Ohio campus, or represents deployment across multiple facilities over many years.

These numbers strain credulity until you consider the trajectory. Global AI infrastructure spending is on pace to exceed $700 billion in 2026, according to multiple industry analyses. Nvidia's data center revenue alone hit $35.6 billion in a single quarter earlier this year. The market is genuinely, verifiably that large, which makes the temptation to finance it yourself almost irresistible. But market size and individual credit risk are different questions, and conflating them is precisely how financial disasters begin.

The Japan Connection

Buried in the reporting is a geopolitical detail worth surfacing. Power for the Ohio project is controlled by the U.S. government. Japan is separately funding a $33 billion natural gas plant as part of a recent trade deal. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is involved in deciding which companies get access to the site.

A single AI data center project is now entangled with U.S.-Japan trade diplomacy, federal land policy, energy infrastructure, and semiconductor industrial strategy, all at once. This is no longer a commercial real estate deal. It is a node in the emerging geopolitics of compute, where access to power is becoming as strategically important as access to chips.

What Could Go Wrong, and What Probably Won't

Will the worst case materialize? Probably not, and the reasons are worth examining carefully before concluding that Nvidia has lost its mind. Nvidia holds more than $44 billion in cash. Its gross margins exceed 60 percent. The guarantee structure likely includes collateral: the data center itself, the land, perhaps the chips, meaning Nvidia's actual loss exposure may be substantially lower than the headline number. And if OpenAI generates sufficient revenue from the compute, the guarantee is never called at all. Nvidia might be signing a piece of paper it never has to honor.

OpenAI's revenue trajectory offers some reassurance. The company reached $13.4 billion in annualized revenue in early 2026, growing rapidly. If that growth continues at anything close to its current trajectory, the economics of a $500 billion campus start to look less like a speculative gamble and more like the kind of leveraged infrastructure play that has historically rewarded patient capital in sectors ranging from railroads to telecommunications to cloud computing, each of which required enormous upfront investment before generating returns. Amazon Web Services generates over $100 billion annually from cloud infrastructure, a business that itself seemed ludicrously capital-intensive when Jeff Bezos first proposed renting server capacity to strangers, and that precedent is precisely what gives Nvidia and its backers the confidence to write checks with this many zeros. There is historical precedent for massive infrastructure bets paying off.

But the strongest counterargument is also the narrowest. It works only if AI demand continues accelerating, if OpenAI specifically captures enough of that demand to service its obligations, if power can actually be delivered to a 10-gigawatt campus in Ohio, if construction costs don't overrun, and if no recession or policy shock intervenes during the multi-year buildout. That is a lot of conditionals carrying a lot of zeros.

Limitations of This Analysis

None of these deal terms are final. Guarantee structure, collateral arrangements, and duration remain undisclosed. Nvidia's actual balance sheet exposure may be much lower than the headline figures suggest. Partial guarantees, layered tranches, and shared risk with other parties could all reduce the effective exposure. We also lack clarity on Japan's $33 billion power funding terms and the timeline for the full 10-gigawatt buildout. Phase one targets 800 megawatts by 2028. Everything else could stretch across a decade or more, during which the entire competitive landscape of artificial intelligence may shift in ways nobody can predict today.

The Bottom Line

When an arms dealer starts financing the war, the calculus changes. Nvidia building the best GPUs in the world is one kind of competitive advantage, the kind that earns 60-percent margins and makes Jensen Huang the most celebrated CEO in Silicon Valley, but Nvidia guaranteeing hundreds of billions in debt so its customers can afford to buy those GPUs is a fundamentally different business with fundamentally different risks, the kind that requires not just engineering brilliance but credit underwriting, counterparty assessment, and a willingness to absorb losses that no chip company has ever faced. GE Capital proved that a manufacturing company can become its own financial system, and that the financial system can eat the manufacturer. Nvidia's $44 billion cash pile and 60-percent margins provide a buffer that GE never had, but the correlation problem, where the same event tanks both your guarantee book and your order book, does not care about margins. It cares about one thing only: whether demand shows up in sufficient volume, at sufficient margins, for sufficient years, to service the obligations that Nvidia has underwritten on the assumption that it will. If you are a shareholder, an energy planner, or just someone who likes electricity to be available when you flip a switch, pay attention to this deal. It is not just about chips. It is about whether we have decided, collectively, to bet $600 billion that artificial intelligence will generate enough economic value to pay for the infrastructure to run it. That bet may prove right. But it is a bet, and someone should say so out loud.
