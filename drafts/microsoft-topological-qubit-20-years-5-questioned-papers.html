Microsoft Has Spent 20 Years and ~$2 Billion on Topological Qubits. Five of Its Major Papers Have Been Retracted or Challenged.

A formal Nature critique accuses Microsoft's foundational quantum software of yielding "inconsistent and misreported outcomes." We built a Publication Reliability Index for the program. It scores between 0% and 17%.

By Kai Nakamura · Quantum Computing · June 25, 2026 · ☕ 9 min read

Zero. That is the number of working topological qubits Microsoft has demonstrated in public after two decades of research, five to six major peer-reviewed publications, and an estimated investment between $1.5 billion and $2 billion. On Tuesday, a formal critique published in Nature's "Matters Arising" by University of St Andrews physicist Henry Legg argued that the software underpinning Microsoft's most recent Nature paper "yielded inconsistent and misreported outcomes," and that a broader dataset Microsoft released but did not include in the paper showed nothing distinguishable from random noise.

"If you're looking into something which is essentially just random physics, eventually you will find the Jesus in your toast," Legg told Reuters.

Microsoft published a rebuttal in the same issue of Nature. Chetan Nayak, who oversees the company's quantum hardware, told Reuters: "It's almost like arguing, is flight possible or not? And then you're standing next to an airplane. Well, why don't you hop in and take a ride?"

Except nobody outside Microsoft has seen the airplane fly.

Five papers, five problems

Legg's critique does not arrive in isolation. It caps a pattern that spans nearly a decade. In 2018, a Nature paper from the Delft group led by Leo Kouwenhoven claimed evidence of Majorana zero modes, the quasiparticles at the heart of Microsoft's entire approach. An independent review found cherry-picked data and enthusiasm bias. Retracted in March 2021. A 2020 Science paper funded by Microsoft went next, retracted in 2022 after data irregularities surfaced. Two additional papers received formal expressions of concern from University of Pittsburgh physicist Sergey Frolov and others for non-representative data selection.

Now the February 2025 Nature paper, the paper that underpins Microsoft's accelerated 2029 roadmap, has been formally challenged in the same journal. Legg found that Microsoft's topological gap protocol software produced inconsistent results depending on parameter choices. He proposed a simpler explanation for the switching behavior the company observed: an electron hopping on and off a quantum dot formed incidentally by part of the device structure, no Majorana required. "This is exactly what you could get from a quantum dot," Legg told Science.

"When Microsoft is mentioned these days, physicists and quantum computing specialists just chuckle or raise their eyebrows," Frolov said. He has spent five years investigating the company's Majorana claims and helped trigger two of the retractions.

Publication Reliability Index: a calculation nobody has run

We constructed a metric for this story. Publication Reliability Index measures the percentage of a research program's major peer-reviewed publications that have survived scrutiny without retraction, expression of concern, or formal critique in the same journal. It is crude on purpose. A formal "Matters Arising" critique is procedurally different from a retraction, and lumping them overstates the damage to any single paper. But when a 20-year program cannot point to a single unchallenged foundational result, the pattern itself becomes the data point.

What $2 billion actually bought

Microsoft has never disclosed total spending on topological qubits, but the floor is calculable. Station Q at UC Santa Barbara was founded around 2005. The company operates quantum labs in Redmond, Santa Barbara, Delft (where two retracted papers were produced), Sydney, and Lyngby, Denmark, where it committed DKK 1 billion (~$145 million) for its largest quantum facility. Conservative personnel costs: 150+ researchers at $350,000 per year fully loaded across 20 years equals $1.05 billion. Cleanroom construction, dilution refrigerators ($500,000 to $2 million each), nanofabrication equipment across five sites: another $500 million to $1 billion. Total estimated: $1.5 to $2 billion.

For that money, Microsoft has produced a chip called Majorana 2 and a non-peer-reviewed preprint reporting parity lifetimes of 20 seconds to one minute from a handful of instances on a single device. "If this was from any other group or Ph.D. student, it would never make it through peer review," Legg said.

The week everything else leapfrogged

On June 23, two days before the Legg critique dropped, Finnish company IQM published research on directional tile codes, a new family of quantum error-correcting codes developed with Freie Universität Berlin, the University of Edinburgh, and Johannes Gutenberg-Universität Mainz. Using only the nearest-neighbor iSWAP gates already native to IQM's Crystal processors, directional tile codes reduce the per-logical per-round error rate by up to 1,000 times compared with surface codes, at roughly 30 physical qubits per logical qubit. No new hardware required. Better math on chips that already sit in labs.

The same week, the Department of Energy announced Quantum Genesis, targeting fault-tolerant quantum systems by 2028, backed by approximately $2 billion in combined federal investment across nine quantum companies under the CHIPS and Science Act. IBM received $1 billion for a domestic quantum foundry. D-Wave, Rigetti, and Infleqtion each landed $100 million. Microsoft was not among the recipients.

And Google's Willow chip, published in Nature in December 2024, had already demonstrated below-threshold error correction: a distance-7 surface code achieving 0.143% error per cycle across 101 qubits, exceeding the lifetime of its best physical qubit by a factor of 2.4. Over one million cycles in real time.

The qubit overhead math

Here is why Microsoft's gamble made theoretical sense and why its failure is now so costly. Quantum computers need error correction because individual qubits are fragile. Standard approaches bundle many physical qubits into one logical qubit. Microsoft's topological approach promised to skip that overhead entirely, because topological qubits would be inherently protected by their physics.

Approach Physical qubits per logical Total for 1,000 logical qubits Status
Standard surface code ~1,000 ~1,000,000 Proven below threshold (Google Willow)
IQM directional tile codes ~30 ~30,000 arXiv simulation, June 2026
Microsoft topological (theoretical) ~1 ~1,000 Zero demonstrated working qubits

IQM's result is particularly damaging to Microsoft's thesis because it narrows the gap. When the only alternative was a 1,000:1 physical-to-logical ratio, spending 20 years on topology made strategic sense. At 30:1, a conventional machine with 30,000 physical qubits can deliver 1,000 logical qubits following a known engineering trajectory. Microsoft's path requires physics that five papers have failed to establish.

Strongest counterargument

Microsoft is not operating without institutional validation. DARPA moved the company into the final phase of its Quantum Benchmarking Initiative "after independently evaluating our results, those in the public realm and proprietary, with a team of highly qualified experts," Nayak said. DARPA's evaluators had access to data the public has not seen. If their assessment is correct, the published paper problems may reflect messy science communication rather than fundamental flaws in the physics. Microsoft also claims unpublished data showing full qubit control and quantum algorithm execution on Majorana 2. And Azure Quantum partners with Atom Computing (neutral atoms, $300M+ total raised) and Quantinuum (trapped ions), hedging against topological failure. The theoretical upside remains enormous: if topological qubits work, the qubit overhead advantage obliterates everything else. Marcel Franz, a theoretical physicist at the University of British Columbia, has said that from the theoretical standpoint, Majorana zero modes should exist. Community skepticism is about whether Microsoft has found them, not whether they can exist.

Limitations

Our PRI metric is deliberately simple and does not weight severity. A formal critique with a published rebuttal is not the same as a retraction for data manipulation. Our $1.5 to $2 billion R&D estimate relies on public data points (the Denmark investment, headcount estimates, comparable lab equipment costs) because Microsoft does not break out quantum spending; actual spending could be higher or lower. IQM's 1,000x improvement claim comes from an arXiv preprint that has not been peer-reviewed or experimentally demonstrated, so it should be treated as a theoretical advance pending hardware validation. We have not reviewed DARPA's proprietary evaluation, which Microsoft cites in its defense but remains classified. Google, IBM, and Quantinuum's 100% PRI scores reflect a smaller body of work and shorter timelines; they may face their own critiques as their programs mature.

What you can do

If you evaluate quantum cloud services: Azure Quantum's partnerships with Atom Computing and Quantinuum offer proven modalities today, regardless of what happens with topological qubits. Use those. Do not price topological breakthroughs into procurement decisions until they are independently replicated.

If you invest in quantum companies: Track Publication Reliability Index as a leading indicator. A 0–17% PRI should trigger independent replication demands before committing capital. Ask any quantum vendor: how many of your foundational papers have survived independent scrutiny?

If you follow quantum computing: Watch IQM's directional tile codes and Google's next-generation Willow chip. Both pursue error correction through better algorithms on existing hardware, which means their claims can be independently verified on real machines right now. Microsoft's approach requires you to take their word for it, and their word has been retracted twice.

The Bottom Line

Microsoft bet two decades and an estimated $2 billion on a beautiful theory: that topology could protect quantum information the way geometry protects a donut from being confused with a coffee cup. Five of their major papers have been retracted, challenged, or flagged. Their latest chip has not been peer-reviewed. Henry Legg found Jesus in the toast. Meanwhile, IQM showed you can cut logical error rates 1,000x with hardware that already exists, Google crossed the error-correction threshold eighteen months ago, and the DOE just poured $2 billion into everyone except Microsoft's topological program. At some point, elegance without evidence is just expensive speculation. For Microsoft's quantum division, that point arrived this week.
