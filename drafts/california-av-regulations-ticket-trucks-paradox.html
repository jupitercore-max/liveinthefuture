California Wrote 100 Pages of Rules for Ticketing Robots. The Same Document Lets 80,000-Pound Trucks Drive Themselves.

California's new autonomous vehicle regulations create the nation's first legal framework for issuing traffic citations to driverless cars while simultaneously opening state highways to autonomous freight trucks. An original analysis of the testing requirements reveals a 10x mileage asymmetry between robotaxis and heavy trucks that exposes which industry Sacramento actually trusts, and why.

Maya Ramirez Infrastructure & Energy May 5, 2026

One hundred pages. That is the length of the two regulatory documents the California Department of Motor Vehicles published on April 28, covering testing and deployment of autonomous vehicles on the state's roads. Buried inside are two provisions that point in opposite directions: Article 3.7 creates a mechanism for police to ticket driverless cars, complete with fleet size caps and permit revocation for repeat offenders, while Article 3.8 lifts the longstanding ban on autonomous vehicles weighing over 10,001 pounds, opening California highways to self-driving semi trucks for the first time.

One hand closes a fist while the other opens a door.

The regulations took effect May 1, and the consequences are already rippling through the industry. Daniel Goff, VP of external affairs at Kodiak Robotics, told TechCrunch the company is "already working on the required documentation" to apply for a California heavy-duty testing permit. Aurora Innovation is already running driverless trucks in Texas. The race to bring autonomous freight to the largest state economy in the nation, one that moves $900 billion in goods annually, has officially begun.

The Testing Gap Nobody Is Talking About

Here is the number that matters most, and that nobody in the initial wave of coverage has calculated: the DMV requires 50,000 miles of supervised testing before a light-duty AV company can apply for driverless deployment permits, but it requires 500,000 miles for heavy-duty vehicles, a ratio of ten to one.

What does that asymmetry look like in practice?

Waymo operates approximately 2,500 robotaxis across multiple cities, completing roughly 500,000 rides per week. At an estimated average of 8 miles per ride, that is 4 million fleet miles per week, or approximately 571,000 miles per day. Waymo's entire fleet could accumulate 50,000 miles of California-specific testing data in approximately 2.1 hours of normal operation. Even a single Waymo vehicle averaging 200 miles per day would hit the threshold in 250 days, under 9 months.

Now consider Kodiak, which operates a small testing fleet, likely fewer than 50 trucks. A long-haul truck averaging 500 miles per day across 300 operating days per year covers 150,000 miles annually. At that rate, accumulating 500,000 miles of California-specific testing with safety drivers would take a single truck 3.3 years. Even a fleet of 20 trucks running simultaneously, logging every mile in California, would need approximately 167 days per truck if the miles are pooled across the fleet (the regulations do not clearly specify whether fleet-wide or per-vehicle miles count, which is itself a significant ambiguity).

That 10x mileage gap is not arbitrary. An 80,000-pound truck at highway speed carries roughly 40 times the kinetic energy of a 4,000-pound sedan at city speed. The regulatory logic is sound: higher stakes demand more proof. But the practical effect is that Waymo, Zoox, and other robotaxi companies are functionally grandfathered in, while trucking entrants face a multi-year qualification gauntlet before they can remove a single safety driver from a California cab.

How You Ticket a Car That Has No Driver

The ticketing mechanism is the provision that got all the headlines, and it is simultaneously more sophisticated and less punitive than the coverage suggested. Under the new "Notice of Autonomous Vehicle Noncompliance," law enforcement can cite an AV company when its vehicle commits a moving violation. There is no monetary fine attached to the notice itself. Instead, violations accumulate as data points the DMV can use to impose fleet size restrictions, geographic limitations, speed caps, weather constraints, or outright permit suspension.

"This is the only way you can force a company to improve, through accountability," Ahmed Banafa, an engineering professor at San Jose State University, told NBC. TechCrunch's Kirsten Korosec pressed the same question the data raises: if the goal is accountability, why not attach a dollar amount? The DMV's answer, implicit in the regulatory structure, is that operational restrictions hurt more than fines. A $500 ticket is rounding noise for a company backed by $16 billion in venture capital. A fleet cap that prevents expansion into Sacramento or San Jose is an existential constraint on growth.

The 72-hour reporting mandate is where the real enforcement teeth are. If a company fails to report a violation to the DMV within three days of receiving it from law enforcement, the DMV gains discretion over increasingly severe sanctions. The regulations also replace the long-maligned "disengagement reports," which companies had gamed for years by using inconsistent standards, with a new metric: "dynamic driving task performance relevant system failure." AV insiders told TechCrunch the new metric is clearer but still imperfect, and the word that came up in every industry conversation was "burdensome."

The Emergency Response Revolution

Easily the least-reported and most operationally significant provisions involve first responders. Every AV company must now maintain two-way communication links with a 30-second response time to police, fire, and emergency medical services. If a local emergency official declares a geofenced "do not enter" zone, all AVs must exit within two minutes. Any vehicle that enters the restricted area after the declaration can trigger permit restrictions or suspension.

This provision exists because of specific incidents. Waymo and Cruise robotaxis have stalled in intersections, blocked fire trucks, and created traffic hazards during emergencies in San Francisco, events documented in public records and news reports over the past two years. The 30-second and two-minute windows are aggressive timelines. For context, the average 911 dispatch response time in California is approximately 10 seconds, meaning AV companies must respond to first responders three times slower than a human dispatcher picks up the phone, a reasonable but not trivial standard that requires 24/7 staffed remote operations centers.

The China Divergence

California's regulatory expansion happened the same week China moved in the opposite direction. On April 29, Bloomberg reported that Chinese regulators froze all new autonomous vehicle permits after Baidu's Apollo Go robotaxi fleet suffered a mass outage in Wuhan on April 1. Over 100 vehicles stopped mid-route, stranding passengers and blocking traffic. The freeze halted new fleet expansions and pilot projects for Baidu, Pony.ai, WeRide, and every other Chinese AV company.

What stands out is the contrast. California's response to AV incidents was to write 100 pages of rules that simultaneously tighten enforcement and expand the addressable market. China's response to a single large-scale failure was a blanket moratorium. One regulatory philosophy says "we can manage this risk with better rules." The other says "stop until we understand what went wrong."

Neither approach is obviously superior. California is betting that data reporting, fleet restrictions, and accountability mechanisms can keep pace with an industry growing at 500,000 rides per week. China is betting that a pause-and-investigate model prevents cascading failures in a country where Baidu alone operates thousands of robotaxis across multiple cities. The market, for its part, has placed its bet: AV funding globally has surged to $21.4 billion year-to-date in 2026, with Goldman Sachs projecting a $415 billion global robotaxi market by 2035.

The Strongest Case Against This Regulatory Approach

Senator Dave Cortese of San Jose has introduced Senate Bill 1246, which argues the DMV's framework does not go far enough. His bill would mandate specific ratios for remote operators, geographic restrictions, and requirements that human supervisors understand California traffic law, provisions the AV industry calls "prescriptive" and "rigid." Reason Foundation policy analysts counter that SB 1246 would "disrupt autonomous vehicle regulatory modernization and impose rigid mandates" that conflict with the DMV's newly adopted performance-based approach.

The core tension is real: the DMV chose outcomes-based regulation (report failures, and we will restrict you if we see problems) over prescriptive regulation (you must have one human for every N vehicles at all times). Outcomes-based regulation is cheaper and more flexible, but it only works if the data reporting is honest and timely. SB 1246's strongest argument is that the 72-hour reporting window, the "burdensome" data requirements, and the "dynamic driving task performance relevant system failure" metric still leave companies with significant room to characterize incidents favorably. Every insider TechCrunch spoke with acknowledged the data-sharing provisions were contentious, and few wanted to say so publicly.

What This Analysis Cannot Determine

Both 50,000 and 500,000-mile thresholds are per-phase, meaning companies must accumulate them separately for supervised testing and driverless testing before applying for deployment, but the regulations do not specify whether miles must be California-specific or whether out-of-state testing data transfers. Aurora has logged millions of miles in Texas. If those miles count, the qualification timeline collapses. If they do not, Texas becomes a training ground that California's residents subsidize with their own road risk. The DMV has not clarified this, and no company we identified has received definitive guidance.

Additionally, the "Notice of AV Noncompliance" framework lacks published data on current violation rates. Without knowing how often Waymo vehicles commit moving violations relative to human drivers on the same roads, it is impossible to assess whether the ticketing mechanism will generate enough data to be actionable or so little data that it becomes performative oversight. Waymo has published safety reports showing its vehicles are involved in significantly fewer injury-causing crashes per million miles than human drivers, but crash rates and traffic violation rates measure different things.

The Bottom Line

California just wrote the most comprehensive autonomous vehicle regulations in the country, and the document's internal contradiction is the point. Robotaxis that have been operating on California roads for years now face a formal accountability framework with real enforcement teeth, because the state learned the hard way that a car with no driver is a legal ghost that no one can cite for running a red light. Autonomous trucks that have never turned a wheel in the state now have a permitted pathway, because California realized that banning a technology Texas had already deployed was costing the state's freight industry competitive position without making anyone safer.

If you run an AV company: your compliance costs just increased materially, particularly the 24/7 teleops staffing for 30-second first responder response times and the data infrastructure for real-time failure reporting. Budget for it now. If you work in freight: Kodiak and Aurora are already preparing California applications, so the window to evaluate autonomous trucking for your operations is measured in months, not years, because the 500,000-mile requirement means the first companies to start testing are the first companies to reach deployment. If you are a California policymaker watching SB 1246: the DMV's outcomes-based approach is the right framework, but only if the data reporting requirements are enforced aggressively enough to catch problems before the next Baidu-style mass outage happens in San Francisco instead of Wuhan. If you are a voter in a city where robotaxis operate: you now have a formal channel to hold AV companies accountable through your local police department. Use it.
