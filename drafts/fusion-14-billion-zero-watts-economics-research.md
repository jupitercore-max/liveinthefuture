## Original Calculations

### Calculation 1: Dollars Per Projected Watt
Nobody has run this comparison across all fusion startups. This calculates how much capital has been raised per watt of announced commercial capacity — a measure of how efficiently investment converts to planned output.

| Company | Capital Raised | Announced Capacity | $/Projected Watt |
|---------|---------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| CFS | $4.0B | 400 MW (ARC) | $10.00/W |
| Helion | $1.5B | 50 MW (Orion) | $30.00/W |
| TAE | $1.79B | 50 MW (near-term) | $35.80/W |
| Pacific Fusion | $0.9B | TBD | ∞ (no capacity announced) |
| General Fusion | $0.4B | TBD (mid-2030s) | ∞ |

For context:
- Vogtle fission (actual): $36.8B / 2,234 MW = $16.47/W (and it produces electricity)
- Vogtle fission (original estimate): $14B / 2,234 MW = $6.27/W
- Utility solar PV installed: ~$1.00/W
- Natural gas CC installed: ~$1.10/W
- Onshore wind installed: ~$1.50/W

Key insight: CFS's $10/W is for capacity that DOESN'T EXIST YET. Vogtle's $16.47/W is for capacity that generates 33,871 GWh/year. The fusion dollar buys a plan; the fission dollar (expensive as it is) buys electrons.

### Calculation 2: The Fusion Funding Gap
How much more capital does the industry need?

CFS:
- SPARC demo: estimated ~$500M-1B (under construction)
- ARC commercial: "multibillion-dollar" — if Vogtle-like overruns apply, $10-20B is plausible for a first-of-kind 400 MW fusion plant
- CFS has $4B. ARC probably needs $10-20B more.

Helion:
- Orion (50 MW): under construction, $1.5B raised
- Microsoft 2028 target — extremely aggressive
- Scaling to 500 MW (Nucor): needs multiple plants at $1.5-3B each

TAE/Trump Media:
- $6B merger gives resources, but building 300-500 MW plants requires $5-15B more

Conservative industry estimate: $14.2B raised, probably $50-100B needed before a single commercial fusion electron reaches the grid.

### Calculation 3: Can Fusion Matter for AI Power Demand?
IEA projects 530 TWh incremental data center demand by 2030 (945 - 415 TWh).

CFS ARC at full capacity:
- 400 MW × 0.90 capacity factor × 8,760 hours = 3,153,600 MWh = 3.15 TWh/year
- That's 0.59% of incremental data center demand
- To supply 10% of incremental DC demand: need ~17 ARC plants
- At $10-20B each: $170-340B in construction costs
- Timeline: ARC won't deliver power until early 2030s; scaling 17 plants would take until 2040s+

Helion Orion at full capacity:
- 50 MW × 0.85 × 8,760 = 372,300 MWh = 0.37 TWh/year
- That's 0.07% of incremental demand
- Need 143 Orion-scale plants for 10%

Bottom line: Fusion is structurally irrelevant to the 2030 data center power crunch. The math doesn't work on any timeline shorter than 15-20 years.

## Strongest Counterargument
The comparison to mature technologies is inherently unfair. Early-stage R&D spending shouldn't be measured in $/watt like proven technology installations. CFS's $4B buys something no amount of solar panels can: a potential baseload energy source with effectively unlimited fuel (deuterium from seawater) and no carbon emissions. The real comparison isn't $/watt today — it's $/watt at scale in 2050, where learning curves and mass production could reduce fusion costs dramatically, just as solar fell from $76/W (1977) to $0.20/W today.

## Limitations
- CFS ARC construction cost is not publicly disclosed; I use Vogtle-analogous estimates
- Capacity factors for fusion are theoretical; no commercial fusion plant has operated
- Fusion plants may have different cost structures at scale (modular manufacturing vs. custom construction)
- Some capital raised (especially CFS's earlier rounds) went to R&D, not construction
- Trump Media/TAE merger valuation is complex (stock-based, not pure capital deployment)
- Power purchase agreements (Google, Microsoft, Eni) provide revenue certainty but don't reduce construction costs
