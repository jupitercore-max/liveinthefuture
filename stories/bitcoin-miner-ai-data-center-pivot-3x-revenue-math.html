Bitcoin Miners Found a Customer That Pays 3× More Per Megawatt Than Bitcoin. They're Called AI Companies.

TeraWulf just signed a $19 billion, 20-year lease with Anthropic for 401 MW of AI infrastructure at a former mining site in Kentucky. Our calculation: AI hosting generates $2.37 million per MW per year, versus $797,000 for mining the same power. US bitcoin miners sit on 8.8 gigawatts of infrastructure that AI companies need yesterday.

By Anya Volkov · Energy & Resources July 6, 2026

Nineteen billion dollars. That is the contracted value of the lease TeraWulf signed with Anthropic on Sunday for a purpose-built AI infrastructure campus at its Justified Data site in Hawesville, Kentucky, a facility that, until recently, mined bitcoin. Four hundred and one megawatts of critical IT load, first capacity arriving in the second half of 2027, full build-out by early 2028. TeraWulf stock, already up 85% this year before the announcement, jumped another 16% in premarket trading on a single Sunday morning. Paul Prager, the company's CEO, said what everyone in the mining industry already knows but few will say plainly: "The defining constraint in this market is no longer computing hardware. It is power, transmission infrastructure, and execution certainty."

He is right, and the numbers explain why an entire industry is walking away from bitcoin.

Show Me the Math

Divide $19 billion by 20 years: $950 million annually. Divide that by 401 megawatts. You get $2.37 million per megawatt per year in AI hosting revenue.

Now compare bitcoin mining with current-generation hardware: a Bitmain Antminer S21 produces 200 terahashes per second at 3,500 watts, packing roughly 57,143 terahashes of mining power into each megawatt, and at the current Luxor hashprice of $38.2 per petahash per day, that megawatt generates about $2,183 daily, or $797,000 per year, which sounds reasonable until you put it next to $2.37 million and realize AI hosting pays 2.97 times more.

But the comparison understates the gap, because mining carries costs that hosting sidesteps entirely. At a favorable $0.04/kWh, each megawatt of mining burns through $350,000 in annual power costs, money subtracted from that $797,000 before hardware depreciates or a single technician gets paid, while AI hosting contracts typically pass electricity through to the tenant and deliver operating margins of 80 to 90 percent according to CoinShares' 2026 outlook, compared with margins that have collapsed to near zero for pure miners at current bitcoin prices.

Near zero is generous. CoinShares' Q1 2026 mining report pegs all-in costs per bitcoin at $118,188 for Bitdeer, $144,321 for HIVE Digital, and $160,402 for Hut 8, and bitcoin trades at roughly $62,000. Every single one of these companies loses money on every coin it mines.

Every single one.

The Fastest Path Nobody Planned

Building a new data center from bare ground takes three to five years in the US, a timeline that includes permitting, construction, grid interconnection studies, transmission upgrades, and environmental review, and EIA data shows grid interconnection alone eats 18 to 36 months with the backlog growing every quarter as hyperscalers compete for the same constrained capacity.

Bitcoin miners already solved that problem. They spent a decade cultivating utility relationships, negotiating power purchase agreements, and building substations where land was cheap and megawatts were plentiful, accumulating, according to Needham & Company, approximately 6.3 gigawatts of operational infrastructure with another 2.5 under construction, for a combined 8.8 gigawatts.

How much does AI need? Consensus estimates from Goldman Sachs, Gartner, and IEA cluster around a 45-gigawatt shortfall for US data centers between 2025 and 2028, a number large enough to rival the total generating capacity of mid-sized European nations, and mining's 8.8 gigawatts represent 19.6 percent of that gap, roughly a fifth of the entire deficit, sitting on sites that already have power flowing.

Conversion runs fast because the infrastructure is already 80 percent there, according to presenters at Bitcoin 2026: substations, transformers, high-voltage feeds, fire suppression, physical security, all present and already paid for, with only 20 percent remaining in capital upgrades to liquid cooling, power distribution, and fiber networking. Twelve months. That is TeraWulf's timeline from contract to electrons, versus three to five years for greenfield.

Who Already Pivoted

TeraWulf is not an outlier. By October 2025, miners had collectively announced $65 billion in contracts with tech companies and cloud providers who need megawatts faster than the grid can produce them: Hut 8 signed a $7 billion AI infrastructure agreement with Google, Cipher Mining inked roughly $3 billion with a FluidStack-led consortium, Core Scientific secured up to $1 billion in credit for AI hosting, and even MARA Holdings, a company that built its entire identity around accumulating bitcoin, filed with the SEC to sell its holdings to fund an AI infrastructure pivot.

Revenue composition is shifting at a pace that makes "pivot" feel too gentle, more like a controlled demolition, and CoinShares projects that for companies with AI contracts, mining revenue will plummet from roughly 85 percent of total revenue in early 2025 to less than 20 percent by the end of 2026. That is not diversification; it is replacement.

Run the total addressable math. If all 6.3 operational gigawatts stayed on mining, they would generate about $5 billion per year; convert them to AI hosting at TeraWulf-Anthropic rates, and annual revenue climbs to $14.9 billion, an uplift of nearly $10 billion per year sitting in rural substations across Texas, Kentucky, New York, and Georgia, requiring no new land, no new grid connections, and no new permits.

What Could Go Wrong

Bitcoin's hash rate has already fallen 14.5 percent from its October peak, according to CoinMarketCap data. If enough miners abandon the network, the cost of a 51 percent attack drops, weakening the security model that underpins a $1.2 trillion asset. This is the strongest case against the pivot: a mass exodus could undermine the very network whose early economics built these facilities, creating a feedback loop where declining security erodes bitcoin's price, driving more miners out, eroding security further still.

Adam Back, the cryptographer whose hashcash protocol inspired bitcoin's proof-of-work, argues the network self-corrects. Difficulty adjusts every 2,016 blocks, roughly every two weeks. As weaker miners exit, remaining operators earn proportionally more. He is probably right on the mechanism, though whether "probable equilibrium" is sufficient security for an asset this large remains an open question.

Location mismatch poses a quieter but potentially more stubborn risk, because miners chose their sites for cheap power, not for proximity to fiber backbones or population centers, far from the corridors in Northern Virginia and Dallas where hyperscalers traditionally build. AI training workloads tolerate high latency, which is why Anthropic chose rural Kentucky, but inference requires low-latency networking, and not every mining site can serve that market without substantial fiber investment.

And then there is duration risk, a less visible uncertainty embedded in contracts that stretch two decades into a future nobody can model with confidence. If inference costs drop by an order of magnitude, tenants may need fewer megawatts than contracted, and renegotiation clauses buried in appendices will determine whether $19 billion stays $19 billion.

Limitations

Our revenue-per-megawatt calculations use the TeraWulf-Anthropic deal as the benchmark; one deal is not a market, and smaller miners will negotiate less favorable terms. Hashprice, the metric driving our mining revenue estimate, can swing 30 percent in a single month, making the $797,000 figure a snapshot rather than a reliable constant. "Eighty percent infrastructure transferability" originates from conference presentations, not published engineering assessments. And the 45-gigawatt power gap is a projection, not a measurement, dependent on AI capital expenditure continuing at current pace.

Bottom Line

Bitcoin miners built the largest privately held power portfolio in American history by accident, chasing cheap electricity to run SHA-256 hashes until the April 2024 halving and a bitcoin price 50 percent below most miners' all-in costs killed the economics. What remains is the hard part that everyone else lacks: substations, transformers, utility contracts, and grid connections that take three to five years to replicate. AI companies need 45 gigawatts of new capacity by 2028, and mining infrastructure delivers megawatts now, not in 2031.

What You Can Do

Evaluating AI infrastructure investments? Separate miners with binding hosting contracts (TeraWulf, Core Scientific, Hut 8, Cipher Mining) from pure miners; the market is pricing pivoted companies at 3 to 5 times the enterprise-value-to-megawatt multiple. Bitcoin holders watching network security: monitor hash rate at mempool.space and note that a 14.5 percent decline remains within what the difficulty adjustment has historically absorbed, since the real danger zone starts past 30 to 40 percent. Running AI workloads that need data center capacity in 2027 or 2028? Former mining sites offer 12 to 18 month timelines versus 3 to 5 years for greenfield builds.
