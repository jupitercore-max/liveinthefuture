Medicare Negotiated Weight-Loss Drugs Down to $245 a Month. The Break-Even Price Is $150.

The Bridge Program launched July 1 with a $50 copay for seniors. Two university microsimulation models show Medicare needs the price 39% lower to stop losing money, generic competition arrives five years after the demonstration ends, and when it sunsets in December 2027, two-thirds of patients' weight loss reverses within twelve months.

By Priya Desai · Health Economics · July 4, 2026 · ☕ 8 min read

Three days ago, roughly 20 million Americans became eligible for weight-loss drugs through Medicare for the first time, when the Bridge Program launched as an 18-month CMS demonstration running through December 31, 2027. Beneficiaries with qualifying BMI and comorbidities can now fill prescriptions for Wegovy, Zepbound KwikPen, or Eli Lilly's Foundayo pill at a flat $50 monthly copay, while CMS pays manufacturers the negotiated Most Favored Nation price of $245 per month and absorbs the $195 difference.

Wall Street greeted the launch with projections of $3 billion to $5.5 billion in incremental annual drug revenue for Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly, and CMS director Chris Klomp told reporters the agency expects "single-digit millions" of enrollees in year one. Nobody projected cost neutrality, because two independent analyses from the University of Chicago have already demonstrated that the negotiated price cannot achieve it under any modeled scenario.

Where $245 Breaks and $150 Holds

Both analyses use a validated Diabetes, Obesity, Cardiovascular Disease microsimulation model applied to 30 million cumulative eligible Medicare beneficiaries over a decade, led by Thomas Hwang and colleagues at the University of Chicago, and together they bracket the fiscal math that press coverage of the Bridge launch almost entirely omitted.

Published in JAMA Health Forum in April 2025, the first study modeled fiscal impact at commercial net prices with a 10% discount, finding that at 10% uptake and 40% long-term adherence Medicare would spend $65.9 billion on GLP-1 drugs, recoup $18.2 billion in downstream savings from prevented diabetes and cardiovascular events, and face $47.7 billion in net new spending over 10 years, while even halving uptake to 5% and cutting adherence to 20% still produced $8 billion in net costs across the decade.

A follow-up in JAMA Network Open in March 2026 reran the model at the negotiated $245 MFN price, projecting $17.6 billion in net spending at 30% uptake with 40% adherence and $5 billion at 10% uptake. Drop the monthly price to $150, however, and Medicare achieves net savings at every uptake level tested: $4.5 billion saved at 10% uptake, $11 billion at 30%, $30.5 billion at 90%, a pattern that held uniformly across the model's full three-way sensitivity surface of uptake, adherence, and price.

Per patient, per month, the gap is $95 between the price that was negotiated and the price that works, amounting to $3.4 billion annually at 3 million enrollees and producing net losses for CMS at every price point, adherence level, and enrollment scenario the researchers tested at $245.

Eighteen Months of Cost, a Fraction of the Payoff

Both JAMA analyses modeled ongoing 10-year coverage, but the Bridge is an 18-month demonstration with no guaranteed successor, and that truncated timeline reshapes the return on investment because downstream health care savings from GLP-1 therapy are overwhelmingly back-loaded: diabetes prevention requires years to translate into avoided hospitalizations, and the SELECT trial's demonstrated cardiovascular benefit emerged with the treatment curve separating from placebo at approximately 12 months, meaning Bridge enrollees reach the inflection point where population-scale savings begin materializing just as the demonstration approaches its midpoint.

At $195 in net federal cost per patient per month, 3 million enrollees generate roughly $7 billion per year in drug spending, or approximately $10.5 billion over the Bridge's full 18-month life, while conservative extrapolation suggests 18 months captures only 8 to 12% of the savings a decade of coverage would produce, yielding roughly $1.5 to $2.2 billion in realized offsets against $10.5 billion in costs and a net Bridge price tag of approximately $8 to $9 billion with a savings-to-cost ratio below 0.21.

Month 19 and the Rebound

When the Bridge sunsets, the fiscal picture compounds. In the STEP 1 extension trial, published in Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism, 327 participants who discontinued semaglutide 2.4 mg after 68 weeks of treatment regained 11.6 percentage points of body weight within one year, erasing two-thirds of their prior 17.3% mean weight loss and leaving net retention of just 5.6%, while cardiometabolic improvements in blood pressure, fasting glucose, and lipid panels reverted toward baseline alongside the weight regain.

If 3 million Bridge enrollees discontinue simultaneously in January 2028, the subsequent 12 months produce a population-level metabolic reversal at a scale no clinical trial has modeled, erasing much of the $1.5 to $2.2 billion in health care savings accrued during the demonstration and returning millions of seniors to baseline cardiovascular risk precisely when compound savings were supposed to justify the upfront investment. Balance, the permanent insurer-run Part D benefit designed to follow Bridge, was shelved in April 2026 after CVS and UnitedHealthcare declined to participate, leaving no funded successor program and no landing zone for patients whose prescriptions expire when the demonstration does.

Why Better Adherence Costs More

One counterintuitive finding from the MFN study undermines the standard pharmacoeconomic assumption that higher adherence saves money: at $245 per month with 10% uptake, raising long-term adherence from 40% to 70% triples net costs from $5 billion to $15.8 billion over a decade, because weight loss from GLP-1 drugs plateaus after approximately 12 months while monthly drug costs persist linearly, making every additional month of adherence progressively less cost-effective as maintenance-phase spending outpaces incremental health benefit by ratios of 2:1 to 5:1. For statins, lifetime adherence saves money; for GLP-1s at current prices, the drugs work well enough to keep patients on therapy but cost too much for continued therapy to pay for itself.

Strongest Counterargument

Untreated obesity costs Medicare approximately $170 billion annually in excess medical spending according to CDC estimates, and GLP-1 drugs reduce cardiovascular events by 20% in high-risk populations while cutting Type 2 diabetes incidence by roughly 70% in prediabetic patients, meaning even a fiscally imperfect program reaches a population that pharmaceutical manufacturers, insurers, and Congress have failed to serve for decades. Price convergence toward break-even is plausible: oral formulations will drive manufacturer competition, the Inflation Reduction Act's Medicare Maximum Fair Price framework can force semaglutide negotiations as early as 2027, and loss of exclusivity in 2032 is projected to deliver additional discounts of 32% to 59%. Allowing the perfect fiscal model to block the first meaningful coverage of anti-obesity medication for America's oldest and sickest population is a policy choice with its own body count, and Bridge may function as political infrastructure since creating millions of beneficiaries who demand permanent coverage is a strategy that has worked for every major Medicare benefit since Part D itself.

Limitations

Our $8 to $9 billion net cost estimate for the Bridge's 18-month window uses linear extrapolation from 10-year microsimulation models not designed to isolate short time horizons, and back-loading of savings is inferred from disease progression mechanics rather than directly observed in published simulation output. Uptake assumptions carry wide uncertainty since prior authorization requirements, physician awareness gaps, and patient hesitancy could hold enrollment well below 3 million, which would substantially reduce fiscal exposure. Weight regain projections derive from 327 controlled-discontinuation participants in the STEP 1 extension rather than millions stopping under real-world conditions with varying treatment lengths and comorbidity burdens, and some patients may transition to commercial coverage, employer plans, or oral generics rather than discontinuing entirely.

What You Can Do

If you are a Medicare beneficiary with qualifying BMI, enroll through your Part D plan now, because the fiscal math is CMS's problem and delaying treatment while waiting for a better deal is a clinical gamble unsupported by the cardiovascular data. If you work in health policy, pressure the $150 threshold directly since semaglutide becomes eligible for IRA price negotiation as early as 2027 and the JAMA authors have provided the exact target. If you manage a Part D plan, model your exposure under enrollment scenarios of 5%, 10%, and 15% of your eligible population, then stress-test what happens to your risk pool when the Bridge sunsets and members regain weight simultaneously, because that rebound is not priced into any current actuarial model.

The Bottom Line

Medicare's Bridge Program is a $245-per-month commitment in a $150-per-month fiscal universe, with a per-patient gap of $95 per month, $3.4 billion annually at expected enrollment, no generic competition until 2032, and a program expiration in 2027 that falls five years before the price reductions that would make permanent coverage sustainable. Within its own 18-month window, the demonstration spends approximately $10.5 billion, recovers a fraction in health care savings, and when it ends, two-thirds of patients' weight loss reverses within 12 months while the permanent coverage program meant to follow has already been abandoned by the insurers who were supposed to run it. Clinical evidence for these drugs is settled and the population health need is real, but the fiscal architecture is structured to lose money at every combination of price, adherence, and enrollment that two independent university models have tested at the negotiated rate.
