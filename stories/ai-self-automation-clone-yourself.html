Your Company Will Try to Automate Your Job. Do It First.

BCG found 74% of enterprise AI projects fail to deliver value. McKinsey's own research says 70% of transformations flop. The common thread: someone who doesn't do the job is deciding what AI should replace. The fix costs $600 per employee per year, not $4 million per consulting engagement.

By Nadia Kovac · Labor & AI Policy · April 29, 2026 · ☕ 9 min read

Seventy-four percent of companies are struggling to extract any scaled value from their AI investments, according to BCG's October 2024 survey: not marginal value, not disappointing-but-measurable value, but no value at all beyond isolated pilots that never graduate to production.

McKinsey, the firm most companies hire to fix exactly this kind of problem, has published its own finding: 70% of large-scale transformations fail. The companies paying McKinsey $3 million to $5 million per 12-week engagement to identify "AI automation opportunities" are buying a coin flip with worse odds, and the typical failure mode is always the same: consultants interview employees for a week, build a slide deck mapping job functions to LLM capabilities, and leave, and the slide deck gathers dust because the people who built it never actually did the work they were proposing to automate.

There is a better approach that costs roughly 1/7th as much, succeeds more often, and has a precedent stretching back half a century to the factory floors of post-war Japan.

The Kaizen Principle, Applied to AI

Toyota's production system collects roughly 700,000 employee improvement suggestions per year, with an implementation rate above 90%. General Motors tried the same idea in the 1980s and got almost nothing. The difference was not incentive structures, though those mattered, but something more fundamental: Toyota trusted the people doing the work to understand the work better than anyone observing it from the outside could.

Michael Polanyi named the underlying problem in 1966: "We can know more than we can tell." A machinist knows when a lathe sounds wrong before any sensor detects a deviation; a nurse knows a patient is declining from micro-expressions that no clinical decision rule captures; an engineer knows which code paths are fragile because they remember an incident three years ago that the documentation never recorded. David Autor, the MIT economist, formalized this as Polanyi's Paradox in 2014: if you cannot articulate what you know, you cannot program a machine to replicate it, and the person least equipped to articulate your tacit knowledge is someone who has never done your job.

This is exactly what happens when a consulting firm tries to map your role onto an AI capability matrix. They see the visible 40%: emails written, reports compiled, data pulled. They miss the invisible 60%: judgment calls, relationship management embedded in how you phrase a question, institutional memory you carry without realizing it, and exception-handling that never appears in any process document because it only happens twice a year and requires someone who was there last time.

The Math: Self-Automation vs. Consulting

Consider a mid-size company of 1,000 employees evaluating where AI can augment or replace work. They have two paths.

Path A: Hire a top consulting firm. A typical MBB (McKinsey, Bain, BCG) AI strategy engagement runs 10 to 14 weeks with a team of 4 to 6 consultants, and at published day rates of $6,500 to $12,000 per consultant (per Slideworks' 2026 analysis of MBB pricing), a 12-week engagement costs $3 million to $5 million. The deliverable: a PowerPoint deck identifying automation candidates across 15 to 30 job functions. Timeline to first actionable change: 4 to 6 months, including the engagement itself plus implementation planning. Success rate for scaled value: 26%, per BCG's own numbers.

Path B: Give every employee AI tools and the mandate to self-automate. Cost: $50/month per employee for a Copilot, ChatGPT Team, or Claude Pro license, which comes to an annual cost for 1,000 employees of $600,000. Timeline to first experiment: days. Each employee runs dozens of micro-experiments against their own tasks, quickly discovering what AI handles well and what it butchers, and no slide deck is required because the knowledge stays in the organization when the experimenter is the organization.

The cost ratio alone is stark: Path B runs 5x to 8x cheaper per year than a single Path A engagement, but the real gap is informational. A consultant interviews you for 45 minutes and decides that 30% of your job is "LLM-addressable," while you, after two weeks of actually trying, know that the 30% is really 12% and a different 18% you never would have guessed turns out to be trivially automatable. The consultant's map diverges from the territory immediately.

The Jagged Frontier Problem

Harvard Business School's 2023 study of 758 BCG consultants using GPT-4 revealed what they called a "jagged technological frontier": on tasks within the AI's competence boundary, consultants improved by 40%, while on tasks outside it, performance dropped 23%. The AI didn't just fail to help with hard problems; it actively degraded the work of experienced professionals by anchoring them to confident, wrong outputs that felt authoritative enough to override the consultant's own judgment.

The frontier is jagged, which means it is unpredictable and cannot be drawn on a whiteboard by someone who has never touched the specific tasks in question. No one, including the people who built the models, can tell you in advance exactly which of your specific tasks fall on which side, and the only way to map the frontier for your job is to walk along it yourself, task by task, and see where the ground holds and where it gives way beneath you.

This is why the METR study matters so much here: experienced developers were 19% slower with AI assistance and believed they were 20% faster, a 39-percentage-point perception gap that should terrify anyone relying on self-reported AI productivity gains. If even the people using the tools misjudge their impact this severely, imagine how far off an external observer's assessment will be. The only corrective is repeated empirical testing against real work, performed by the person who actually does the work every day and knows what "good" looks like.

The Shopify Model

Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke laid down the marker in April 2025. In an internal memo shared publicly on X, he declared: "Reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation at Shopify." Teams must demonstrate that a job cannot be done by AI before requesting new headcount.

Note what Lütke did not do: he did not hire McKinsey, and he did not create a top-down "AI Transformation Office." He pushed the automation mandate to the people who actually do the work. The cynical reading: he's making employees dig their own graves. The realistic reading is different: employees who try to automate their own roles discover, faster than any outside analyst could, exactly which parts require human judgment, and they converge iteratively on the irreducible core that makes them valuable while everything else becomes AI-augmented throughput.

The Cynical Case for Doing It Yourself

Set aside idealism. Companies will automate roles regardless of whether employees cooperate: the 150,000 tech layoffs pegged to AI investment happened whether displaced workers wanted them to or not, the entry-level job crisis is accelerating, and Gen Z's excitement about AI collapsed 14 points in a single year.

The employee who proactively discovers the human-AI split for their role holds an enormous strategic advantage: they are not waiting for a restructuring consultant to misunderstand their job and recommend elimination, because they have already run the experiments and can articulate with specificity what they do that AI cannot. "I tried automating X, here is what worked, here is what failed, and here is why the failure cases require me."

That sentence, backed by data from your own experiments, is the single most powerful thing an employee can say in a world where 74% of AI projects are failing and every executive is desperately looking for someone who actually understands what works.

Limitations

The cost comparison uses midpoint estimates for MBB consulting fees, which are not publicly disclosed and vary widely by engagement scope. The 26% "scaled value" rate inverts BCG's 74% struggle figure, which conflates "failed entirely" with "succeeded but couldn't scale," making it a rough proxy. The $50/month per-employee cost assumes a single license; real costs may be higher when including integration and training overhead. Most critically, this argument assumes employees have the psychological safety to experiment with tools that might automate parts of their own role, and not all workplace cultures support that.

The Strongest Counterargument

The best case against employee-led self-automation is that it asks turkeys to vote for Thanksgiving. A rational employee who discovers that 60% of their job is automatable has every incentive to hide that finding rather than report it, because game theory says individuals optimizing for self-preservation will sandbag the results, quietly pocket the productivity gains, and never tell management what they actually learned. This is not hypothetical: it is exactly what happened during the early spreadsheet era, when clerks who could do in an hour what used to take a day simply pretended it still took a day and used the remaining seven hours however they pleased.

The counterargument to the counterargument: the current environment is not the spreadsheet era, because AI capabilities are visible, public, and advancing on a monthly cadence that makes hiding impossible for long. Management already knows automation is possible; the only question is how much and where. The employee who gets ahead of it controls the narrative and retains the irreducible core of their role, while the one who hides gets restructured when a consultant draws the wrong map and no one with actual domain knowledge is left to correct it.

The Playbook

Week 1: Inventory. List every recurring task you perform without filtering: the trivial (formatting reports, scheduling meetings) and the complex (negotiating with a difficult vendor who trusts you specifically, triaging ambiguous edge cases that no runbook covers).

Weeks 2 to 4: Triage and test. Take the 10 most time-consuming tasks and try each one with AI by actually performing the task both ways and comparing the output side by side. Record what worked, what required editing, and what was unsalvageable.

Month 2: Map the frontier. Tasks where AI saved time go into the "augment" bucket; tasks where it failed or actively degraded the work go into "human core." That human core, stated with evidence from your own experiments rather than from an abstract capability analysis, is your value proposition in a restructuring conversation.

Month 3 and beyond: Recalibrate. New model releases happen quarterly, and each one shifts the frontier in ways no one can predict from the changelog alone. Retest the "human core" tasks every time capabilities shift, because your map only stays useful if it stays current.

Optional but powerful: Document everything and share it. If your company supports transparency, publish your findings internally: "I tested AI against 10 of my core tasks, here is what it can do, here is what it can't, and here is why." That document makes you the resident expert on AI applicability in your domain, which is precisely the person no restructuring plan wants to eliminate, because eliminating you means losing the only reliable map of what automation can and cannot do in your corner of the organization.

The Bottom Line

The question is not whether AI will be applied to your job. The question is who runs that experiment: you, with full knowledge of every edge case and judgment call your role demands, or someone from outside who has never done a single day of your work and whose last engagement was in a different industry. Seventy-four percent of the time, the outside approach fails. The inside approach costs less, starts faster, produces more accurate maps of the human-AI frontier, and leaves the employee standing with a clear, evidence-backed understanding of why they are still necessary. The alternative is to wait, hope, and be surprised when the consultants' PowerPoint deck arrives with your job function highlighted in red and no one in the room who can explain why the analysis is wrong.
