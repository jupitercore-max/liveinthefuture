A $20,000 Home Robot Sold 10,000 Units in Five Days. A Human in a VR Headset Still Folds Its Laundry.

1X Technologies just opened the first vertically integrated humanoid robot factory in the United States, a 58,000-square-foot facility in Hayward, California, that winds its own copper motor coils, stress-tests every actuator for 20 million cycles, and dresses finished robots in machine-washable nylon suits. It sold its entire first year of production in under a week. But when NEO encounters a task it cannot handle autonomously, a 1X employee in a VR headset takes remote control with a live camera feed of your home. An original cost analysis shows why that arrangement breaks at 10,000 units.

Priya Desai Robotics May 11, 2026

Two hundred million dollars. That is the approximate retail value of 10,000 humanoid robots at $20,000 each, the quantity that 1X Technologies sold in five days when it opened pre-orders for NEO last October. On April 30, the Norwegian-American company began full-scale production at a new 58,000-square-foot factory in Hayward, California, making it the first company to operate a vertically integrated humanoid robot manufacturing facility on US soil. Robots are walking off the assembly line. Some of them are already working on the factory floor, stocking parts and hauling components, serving double duty as warehouse hands and training-data generators.

Inside the factory, every component is homegrown. 1X designs and manufactures its own motors, actuators, batteries, sensors, and transmissions in-house, a level of vertical integration that CEO Bernt Børnich compares to early Tesla and early SpaceX. Automated lines wind copper coils into custom motors. A reliability lab subjects hardware to more than 20 million cycles of stress testing, and Vikram Kothari, VP of manufacturing and hardware, says the quality control process is modeled on automotive-grade DFMEA standards. Over 200 workers staff the facility, which sits in a Hayward corridor that is quietly becoming a robotics manufacturing hub; Amazon subsidiary Zoox operates a 220,000-square-foot robotaxi plant nearby.

NEO itself is 168 centimeters tall, weighs 30 kilograms, and operates below the decibel level of a modern refrigerator. It uses tendon-driven actuators and 3D-lattice cushioned limbs, runs on the NVIDIA Jetson Thor platform, and ships in a machine-washable nylon knit suit available in tan, gray, or dark brown. Its marketing pitch: a general-purpose domestic helper that folds laundry, loads the dishwasher, and handles household chores while learning on the job.

That learning is where the story gets interesting.

The Person Inside the Machine

When NEO encounters a task it cannot complete autonomously, it does not stop. It calls for backup. A 1X employee wearing a VR headset takes remote control of the robot, gaining a live stereoscopic camera feed from inside the customer's home. Once inside, the operator manipulates NEO's limbs, completes the task, and the entire session is recorded and fed back into 1X's training pipeline to teach the NEO Cortex brain how to handle similar situations autonomously in the future.

This is not a bug in the system. It is the business model.

1X has been transparent about the mechanism in technical discussions, though the consumer marketing emphasizes the "AI-powered" angle. A January 2026 software update reduced the frequency of human interventions, but "less human involvement" is not "no human involvement," and the company has not published a timeline for full autonomy. According to an independent review by Botmarket, early demos suggest teleoperation is triggered frequently enough that the robot's primary value proposition in year one is not autonomous labor but data collection. Botmarket's reviewer put it bluntly: customers are paying $20,000 to participate in a training program that will benefit future buyers more than themselves.

Nothing in consumer electronics compares to this privacy surface. A smart speaker logs voice commands, a robotic vacuum maps your floor plan, and a security camera records a fixed field of view. NEO does all three simultaneously while moving through your home. It builds a continuous, three-dimensional spatial model of your domestic environment, and when a teleoperator takes control, a human being you have never met gains a first-person, real-time view of your kitchen, your bedroom, and whatever happens to be sitting on your countertop at 7 AM on a Tuesday.

What $20,000 Actually Buys

Consider the economics at the unit level. Assume a NEO operates four hours per day of active task time, a generous estimate for early adoption, and that human teleoperators handle 30 percent of task requests, a figure consistent with the limited demo data and 1X's own acknowledgment that the robot defaults to remote control for complex situations.

At $3.91 per autonomous hour, NEO is already more expensive than a Roomba performing autonomous floor cleaning at roughly $0.14 per hour and far less capable at that specific task. It is cheaper than a human housekeeper at $25 to $35 per hour, but only for the 70 percent of tasks it handles without calling for help. For the other 30 percent, 1X is absorbing the cost of a human teleoperator, and at 10,000 deployed robots, that cost balloons to $109.5 million per year in operator wages alone.

This is the math that makes the data-flywheel model essential rather than optional. Every teleoperated session that gets successfully replayed by the autonomous system in the future eliminates one more human intervention and saves $25. Autonomous success rate is not just a product quality metric; it is the variable that determines whether 1X's business model is viable or an expensive training exercise subsidized by early adopters. If the autonomous rate does not climb from 70 percent toward 95 percent within two years, the teleoperator cost curve will destroy the company's unit economics at the 100,000-unit scale it plans to reach by end of 2027.

China shipped 5,500 humanoid robots in 2025 and is targeting 20,000 in 2026, according to production tracking data compiled by industry analyst Jens Peder Kristensen. AGIBOT alone claims 10,000 units produced in three months. Nearly all Chinese volume is enterprise-focused, which means the training data comes from factories and warehouses where environments are structured, repeatable, and privacy-irrelevant. That is a fundamental advantage: industrial data is cheap, abundant, and unencumbered by the regulatory and reputational risks of recording inside private homes.

Tesla's approach is different again. It has deployed over 1,000 Optimus units inside its own factories, retooled portions of its Fremont plant for humanoid production, and benefits from a manufacturing infrastructure that dwarfs 1X's by orders of magnitude. Musk has quoted a target price of $20,000 to $30,000, but Tesla's data advantage lies in scale, not intimacy: it knows how to build hundreds of thousands of complex electromechanical systems per year. 1X's current facility can make 10,000 per year. Tesla's Fremont plant produced 560,000 vehicles last year.

The Tesla FSD Precedent

There is a useful precedent for 1X's approach, and it lives in your neighbor's driveway. Tesla's Full Self-Driving system charges customers $12,000, or $199 per month, for supervised autonomous driving that requires constant human attention. In exchange, those customers generate the billions of miles of real-world driving data that trains Tesla's neural network. Every additional buyer generates more data, Tesla collects more miles, the system improves, and the next sale gets easier. That flywheel has been spinning since 2019.

1X is running the same playbook in a much harder environment. Public roads are complex, but they are public. What Tesla captures on camera consists of feeds from intersections, highway merges, and parking lots, none of which includes the inside of anyone's home. It does not capture what is on your nightstand, who visits at 10 PM, or whether you folded your laundry this week. 1X's data flywheel requires exactly this level of intimate access, and it requires a human teleoperator to be present during the most revealing moments, those in which the robot has failed to complete a task and the customer's domestic environment is exposed in full to a stranger.

Tesla also had a critical mass advantage. By the time FSD reached its current capability, millions of Teslas were on the road generating training data passively through their cameras. 1X will have 10,000 units in homes. That data volume difference spans three orders of magnitude. Whether NEO's learning rate can compensate for that gap, by extracting more value per teleoperated interaction than Tesla extracts per driven mile, is the central technical question the company must answer before the money runs out.

The Strongest Case Against NEO

The International Federation of Robotics has assessed that useful, widely accepted home humanoids may still be 20 years away. The core problem is not hardware; it is the mismatch between robot capabilities and unstructured domestic environments, where a child's toy on the floor, a new arrangement of dishes, or an unfamiliar detergent bottle can derail a task sequence that worked flawlessly in the lab.

Factories work for robots because they are engineered to be predictable. Every cobot on an assembly line operates in a space designed around its limitations, with consistent lighting, known geometries, and fixed positions. Your kitchen is the opposite. Every home is a unique, constantly rearranging puzzle, and the combinatorial space of domestic scenarios dwarfs anything in industrial automation. A used collaborative robot arm from Universal Robots costs $8,000 to $18,000 and delivers fully autonomous, reliable performance for defined tasks without ever calling a teleoperator or recording your home.

1X's bet is that humanoid form factor, the ability to navigate spaces designed for humans and manipulate objects designed for human hands, justifies the autonomy gap. That bet could be right. But it will be proven or disproven not by pre-order numbers or factory tours but by the quarterly decline in teleoperation rates. If those rates do not fall fast enough, 10,000 customers will own a $20,000 device that is part robot, part surveillance system, and part data-collection terminal for a Norwegian AI lab.

What the Data Cannot Show

This analysis relies on several assumptions that could be wrong. The 30 percent teleoperation rate is an estimate based on available demo data and 1X's public statements; the actual rate could be higher, which makes the economics worse, or lower, which makes them better. The $25 per hour burdened teleoperator cost assumes US-adjacent labor markets; if 1X operates its teleoperation center in lower-cost regions, the per-unit cost drops but the privacy implications intensify. The four-hour daily active usage assumption has no peer-reviewed basis because no consumer humanoid has ever been deployed at this scale. Real-world usage patterns in the first year will be the single most important dataset in all of robotics, and 1X will have it exclusively.

What we cannot assess is whether 1X's vertical integration, its custom motors and in-house actuators and tendon-driven limbs, will prove to be an engineering moat or a scaling bottleneck. History offers examples of both: Tesla's Gigafactory rewrote battery economics through vertical integration, while numerous hardware startups drowned in the capital requirements of doing everything themselves. 1X has raised over $125 million and is reportedly seeking $1 billion more at a $10 billion valuation. That money will need to fund both the scaling of production to 100,000 units per year and the AI research necessary to eliminate the human operators who are currently subsidizing every robot's competence.

What You Can Do

If you are considering pre-ordering a NEO, treat it as an investment in an early-access beta program, not a finished household appliance. Understand that teleoperation means a human being will intermittently have live video access to your home, that sessions are recorded and used for AI training, and that the company has not committed to a date for full autonomy. Ask 1X directly what data retention policies apply to teleoperated sessions and whether you can opt out of recording without losing robot functionality. Read the terms of service before your $20,000 robot reads your kitchen.

If you are an investor evaluating humanoid robotics, watch the teleoperator ratio quarterly. A company that claims 10,000 deployed units but still relies on humans for 30 percent of interactions is a data company wearing a robot costume. The metric that matters is the quarter-over-quarter reduction in teleoperation rate, not the number of units shipped. If that ratio does not drop by at least 5 percentage points per quarter in year one, the unit economics will not close before competitors with larger datasets and cheaper manufacturing, Tesla and the Chinese producers, arrive at similar capability levels.

If you work in robotics policy, start drafting domestic-robot privacy frameworks now. The EU's AI Act classifies emotion-recognition AI in workplaces as high-risk, but it has nothing specifically addressing a humanoid robot that teleoperates through your living room while recording spatial data, object inventories, and human activity patterns. NEO will be in 10,000 homes before any regulatory body publishes guidance on what that means for the people living in them.

The Bottom Line

1X Technologies has done something genuinely remarkable: it built the first vertically integrated humanoid robot factory in the United States, sold out its entire first year of production in under a week, and started shipping actual robots off an actual assembly line in an actual building in Hayward, California. That is not vaporware. That is not a render. But the robot that walks off that line is not yet the robot that was advertised. It is a $20,000 data-collection terminal with legs, a VR-headset-wearing human on standby, and a bet, a big one, that the training data flowing from 10,000 American living rooms will be enough to teach it how to fold a towel without calling Norway for help.
