## 1. The Empirical Record: 0 for 162

**Bloomberg Law (March 10, 2026):** In the year since New York amended its WARN Act (effective March 2025) to require companies to disclose whether "technological innovation or automation" contributed to layoffs:
- **162 companies** filed mass termination notices with NY DOL
- **Zero** — not one — attributed layoffs to AI or automation
- **Amazon** filed WARN notices while CEO Andy Jassy publicly stated the company's workforce would shrink as AI takes on more tasks (later walked back: "not really AI-driven. Not right now, at least")
- **Goldman Sachs** filed notices citing "annual talent review" while separately telling media AI was driving cost savings and job reductions
- The law imposes a $500/day/violation penalty

This isn't a measurement problem. It's a **measurement impossibility**.

## 2. The Five Failure Modes of Attestation-Based AI Displacement Disclosure

### Failure Mode 1: The Attribution Ambiguity Problem
AI displacement is almost never a discrete, attributable event. Kevin Frazier (Abundance Institute/Cato) told Bloomberg Law: "the WARN Act is a product of the 1970s, when factory closings were geographically confined and occurred over days or weeks." AI displacement is:
- **Gradual** (Klarna: 3,104 eliminated over 24 months via attrition, not a single layoff event)
- **Multi-causal** (restructuring + AI + market conditions + financial engineering — which fraction is "AI"?)
- **Counterfactual** (hiring freeze: you didn't fire anyone, you just didn't hire the 500 you would have — how do you report what didn't happen?)
- **Pre-emptive** (HBR/Davenport: cutting based on AI's *potential*, not demonstrated performance — the company can't honestly attribute to AI because AI hasn't actually replaced the workers yet)

Amanda Ballantyne (New America, former AFL-CIO Technology Institute): "There are a lot of factors in our economy right now that are driving disruption for workers." This ambiguity isn't temporary — it's structural to how AI displacement works.

### Failure Mode 2: The Perverse Incentive Structure
No company benefits from checking the "AI" box:
- **Legal liability:** Opens potential wrongful termination claims, discrimination lawsuits (Brookings: 86% of "danger zone" workers are women — AI attribution creates disparate impact exposure)
- **Reputational risk:** "We fired people because of AI" alienates remaining workforce, unions, customers
- **Regulatory escalation risk:** Data could justify future levies, restrictions, or mandates
- **Investor paradox:** In Q1 2025, admitting AI-driven cuts hurt stock prices; by Q1 2026, companies *advertise* AI cuts because investors reward efficiency (the "Attribution Flip" from v8.0). But the WARN system doesn't capture this new dynamic because it's still structured as a penalty, not a reporting framework.

As attorney Andi Mazingo told Bloomberg Law: "Companies are using narratives about AI to frame layoffs in ways that are most likely to be well received by shareholders and potential shareholders." The incentive to attribute shifts with the audience — investors hear "AI efficiency," regulators hear "economic conditions."

### Failure Mode 3: The Threshold Problem
WARN laws only trigger on mass layoff events (100+ employees in most states, varies). But the dominant displacement mechanisms are sub-threshold:
- **Klarna:** 3,104 eliminated via attrition + hiring freeze. Zero WARN triggers. Zero unemployment claims.
- **Shadow Agent Proliferation:** 49% of workers using unauthorized AI (BlackFog). Peer displacement. No management decision to report.
- **Invisible displacement (hiring freezes):** Stanford "Canaries" documents 13% entry-level employment decline in AI-exposed occupations. No company decision to report — they simply stopped posting roles.
- **Degradation Pipeline:** FTE→contractor→gig workers. Each transition counts as "employment." No termination event.
- **Strategic misattribution:** Companies attributing AI-driven cuts to "restructuring" or "market conditions" because the WARN form rewards vagueness.

Even if every company reported honestly, WARN captures maybe 10-15% of actual displacement events.

### Failure Mode 4: The Definition Problem
NY DOL Commissioner Roberta Reardon acknowledged in 2025 that "defining an AI-related layoff is challenging." The categories are genuinely ambiguous:
- A company replaces CRM software with an AI-integrated version. The new system needs 40% fewer customer service reps. Was this an "AI layoff" or a "software upgrade"?
- A company automates data entry with an RPA tool trained on ML models. Is RPA "AI"?
- A manager uses Claude to write code, producing work that previously required two junior developers. The company doesn't backfill when a junior dev leaves. Is this AI displacement?
- A company restructures around "AI-first" principles (Atlassian, Block, Shopify). They cite multiple factors. How much is AI?

Warner-Hawley requires reporting "any artificial intelligence-related job impact" — but "AI-related" is as undefinable as "internet-related" would have been in 1998. Every business decision is increasingly AI-related.

### Failure Mode 5: The Temporal Mismatch
WARN is event-triggered (mass layoffs). AI displacement is process-based (gradual capability absorption over months/years). By the time a company crosses the WARN threshold, the displacement happened months or years earlier through invisible mechanisms. It's like measuring ocean acidification by counting fish kills — the damage is done long before the measurable event.

## 3. The Broader Pattern: Self-Reporting Regulation Systematically Undercounts

The NY WARN failure isn't unique. It follows a well-documented pattern across regulatory domains:

### OSHA Workplace Injury Reporting
Multiple GAO reports and academic studies estimate workplace injuries are underreported by **40-70%**. Employers have direct financial incentives (workers' comp premiums, OSHA inspections) to undercount. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' Survey of Occupational Injuries and Illnesses (SOII) systematically undercounts because it depends on employer-maintained logs. States with electronic reporting requirements capture more injuries — not because workplaces are more dangerous, but because reporting friction is lower.

**AI parallel:** AI displacement has even stronger underreporting incentives (legal liability, multi-causal ambiguity, no physical evidence) and no equivalent of workers' comp claims to create a second data stream.

### SEC Financial Disclosure
SEC comment letter analysis found **at least 92 separate comments addressing AI-related disclosures** across 56 companies since 2021 — the SEC is finding companies aren't disclosing material AI risks/impacts even when they discuss AI extensively in earnings calls and investor presentations. Companies discuss AI with investors in one register and report to regulators in another.

**AI parallel:** The same dual-register problem. Investor communications tout AI efficiency; regulatory filings cite "restructuring."

### Environmental Self-Reporting (TRI)
The Toxics Release Inventory, established 1986, relies on facility self-reporting of chemical releases. Studies consistently find underreporting, especially for smaller releases below threshold quantities. The key insight: TRI *improved* over time not because reporting got more honest, but because **third-party monitoring** (satellite imagery, community air monitors, water testing) created accountability that self-reporting alone could not.

**AI parallel:** AI displacement needs third-party measurement infrastructure — not better self-reporting mandates.

### ESG/Climate Disclosure
The EU's CSRD and SEC's climate disclosure rules face the same "forward-looking, non-standardized data" problem identified in academic literature (Columbia Law, PLOS ONE 2024). Companies have discretion in classifying complex data, reducing reliability while creating an illusion of transparency through the mandatory framework.

**AI parallel:** "AI-related job impact" is the ultimate forward-looking, non-standardized data point.

## 4. Why Warner-Hawley Faces the Same Failure Mode

Warner-Hawley AI-Related Job Impacts Clarity Act requires quarterly reporting to DOL of "any artificial intelligence-related job impact" from covered entities (large companies + federal agencies). It's a direct federal analog of what NY attempted.

**Same attribution ambiguity:** Companies will report zero for the same reasons they report zero in NY — multi-causal layoffs, hiring freezes aren't "events," pre-emptive displacement isn't measurable.

**Same threshold problem:** Even if passed, Warner-Hawley captures explicit displacement events. The dominant mechanisms (attrition, hiring freezes, shadow agents, degradation) are invisible to event-triggered reporting.

**Same incentive structure:** No penalty strong enough to overcome the legal/reputational/regulatory costs of honest attribution.

**Same definition problem:** "AI-related" is genuinely undefined.

**Warner-Hawley's value is political, not measurement.** The act's real function is legitimizing the question — creating a federal data collection mandate even if the data is initially garbage. Over time, the infrastructure could be repurposed for structural measurement. But anyone who expects Warner-Hawley to produce accurate displacement data in its first years is building on sand.

## 5. The Alternative: Structural Measurement That Bypasses Self-Reporting

If attestation produces zero data, measurement must shift from **"what companies say"** to **"what companies do."** Four approaches that bypass self-reporting:

### Approach 1: Revenue-Per-Employee Ratio Tracking (the "Klarna Signal")
**Mechanism:** Require publicly-traded companies to report headcount (already required) and revenue (already required) in a standardized format that enables automated RPE tracking.
- Klarna: RPE went from $175K to $1.2M (6.9×) in 24 months. That signal is visible in financial statements without any company attributing anything to AI.
- HRBench 2025 data shows RPE benchmarks by industry. Companies that deviate significantly above industry median are deploying *something* — whether they call it AI or not.

**Advantage:** Already required data, no attestation needed, manipulation is securities fraud (not a $500/day WARN penalty), captures ALL displacement regardless of mechanism.

**Limitation:** Lagging indicator. Revenue growth from non-AI sources (price increases, market expansion) creates noise. Contractor substitution gaming.

**Design:** Track RPE ratio changes by industry over time. Companies whose RPE increases >2 standard deviations above industry median within 2 years trigger enhanced disclosure requirements (not punishment — information gathering).

### Approach 2: Anthropic-Style Observed Exposure (the "Usage Signal")
**Mechanism:** AI providers publish anonymized, aggregated usage data showing which occupational task categories are being automated vs. augmented.
- Anthropic Economic Index already does this: 77% of enterprise API usage is automation, 23% augmentation. Occupation-level granularity available.
- Doesn't depend on company self-reporting — it's measured by the AI provider from actual usage patterns.

**Advantage:** Measures actual behavior, not claimed behavior. High granularity. Real-time.

**Limitation:** Only captures usage of participating AI providers. Companies can switch to non-participating providers. Privacy/competitive concerns limit detail. Anthropic publishes voluntarily — can be revoked.

**Design:** Warner-Hawley could mandate frontier AI companies (above revenue threshold) publish quarterly "Economic Impact Reports" with occupation-level automation/augmentation ratios — similar to how financial exchanges publish market data. This leverages the AI provider's unique position as observer of actual deployment patterns.

### Approach 3: BLS Labor Dynamics Triangulation (the "Structural Signal")
**Mechanism:** Enhance existing BLS surveys to capture displacement signals without asking about AI:
- Track occupation-level employment changes with high frequency (monthly, not annual)
- Track entry-level vs. experienced hiring ratios by occupation (the Dallas Fed experience premium signal)
- Track involuntary part-time/contractor conversion rates by occupation
- Cross-reference with O*NET AI exposure scores

The Stanford "Canaries" methodology already demonstrates this: 13% relative employment decline in AI-exposed occupations for 22-25 year-olds, 20% decline for young software devs — without asking any company about AI.

**Advantage:** Population-level data, no self-reporting, captures all displacement mechanisms including invisible ones, already-existing infrastructure.

**Limitation:** Slow (BLS methodology changes take years). The 2025 benchmark revision (3.2× overcount) shows BLS itself has measurement problems. Can't attribute causation — correlation with AI exposure scores isn't proof.

**Design:** Fund BLS to produce monthly "AI Exposure Employment Dashboard" cross-referencing employment data with O*NET/Anthropic exposure scores. Create automated anomaly detection. This doesn't require new surveys — just new analysis of existing data.

### Approach 4: DisplaceIndex-Style Composite Indicators (the "Market Signal")
**Mechanism:** Third-party composite indices combining multiple data streams to produce real-time displacement pressure scores.
- DisplaceIndex.com already operating: 70% FRED hard data + 30% AI-scored news sentiment, updated every 6 hours, currently at 39.6/100 ("Displacement Pressure")
- JobGoneToAI: 152,797+ verified AI-attributed jobs across 69+ companies
- Programs.com: 130K+ tracked, cross-industry patterns
- Challenger Gray & Christmas: employer-reported (but independent collection, not regulatory compliance)

**Advantage:** Independent, real-time, multi-source, can't be gamed by individual companies.

**Limitation:** Varies in rigor. News-sentiment component introduces noise. No regulatory authority. Can be dismissed as activist metrics.

**Design:** Warner-Hawley could mandate DOL fund and validate independent displacement indices (not build them — validate). Create an "AI Displacement Data Standard" that third-party trackers can certify against. Government blesses the methodology without doing the measurement.

### Approach 5: SEC-Leveraged Human Capital Disclosure (the "Financial Signal")
**Mechanism:** The SEC already requires "human capital" disclosures under Regulation S-K Item 101(c). SEC staff have issued 92+ comment letters requesting companies clarify AI-related disclosures in 10-K filings. Approximately 61% of these comments requested companies explain how AI is being used and its intended impact.

**Advantage:** Existing regulatory infrastructure. Misleading SEC disclosure is securities fraud (meaningful penalty). Investors are the enforcement mechanism (short-sellers, activist investors verify claims).

**Limitation:** Only covers public companies. Quarterly 10-K cycle is slow. Disclosures are narrative, not structured data. "Material" threshold means small-scale displacement goes unreported.

**Design:** SEC rulemaking to require structured, machine-readable human capital data including: headcount by function, contractor/gig ratios, RPE, AI capital expenditure, AI-attributed task changes. This leverages the one reporting framework where companies face material penalties for misleading information.

## 6. The Measurement Stack: A Realistic Design

No single approach works. The answer is a stack that triangulates:

| Layer | What It Measures | Who Reports | Strength | Weakness |
|-------|-----------------|-------------|----------|----------|
| SEC S-K Enhancement | RPE, headcount, AI capex | Public companies (mandatory) | Securities fraud penalties | Public companies only |
| AI Provider Usage Reports | Task-level automation ratios | AI companies (mandatory) | Actual behavior, real-time | Only captures participating providers |
| BLS Enhanced Surveys | Occupation-level employment dynamics | BLS (population surveys) | Population-level, all mechanisms | Slow, correlation not causation |
| Third-Party Indices | Composite displacement pressure | Independent (validated) | Real-time, multi-source | Varies in rigor |
| Warner-Hawley Attestation | Self-reported AI impacts | Companies (mandatory) | Creates legal/political framework | Will produce near-zero useful data |

**Warner-Hawley is Layer 5 — the least useful for data but most useful for politics.** Its value is creating the *mandate* for measurement. Layers 1-4 produce the *actual data*.

The critical design insight: **the measurement system should not depend on companies accurately self-identifying AI displacement — because they can't, even if they wanted to.**

## 7. The Deeper Problem: What Counts as "Displacement"?

Even perfect measurement faces a philosophical problem: what counts?

The NY WARN zero-compliance outcome isn't just about evasion. In many cases, companies genuinely don't know whether cuts are "AI-related." A company restructures 15% of its workforce. AI is one factor among market conditions, competition, management decisions, and strategic pivots. What percentage of those cuts is "AI"? 30%? 60%? 100%? There's no objectively correct answer.

This is why outcome-based measurement (RPE changes, occupation-level employment shifts) is structurally superior to input-based measurement (self-reported causation). You don't need to know *why* displacement happened to know that it happened and to fund transitions.

**The carbon tax analogy is instructive but misleading.** Carbon emissions work as a tax base because CO₂ is physical, measurable, and unambiguously attributable to a specific chimney. "AI displacement" is none of these things. The correct analogy is not carbon tax but **income tax** — you don't tax the *reason* someone made money, you tax the *outcome* (income). Similarly, don't tax the reason someone was displaced; tax the outcome (windfall productivity gains).
