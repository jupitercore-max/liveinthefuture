AI Isn't Coming for Your Job. It's Coming to Be a Worse Version of You That Never Sleeps.

McKinsey says AI will add $4.4 trillion to corporate profits. Goldman Sachs says 300 million jobs are exposed. Both are right. Both are asking the wrong question. The real question: does your employer need one of you at 100%, or fifty of you at 70%?

By Nadia Kovac · April 7, 2026 · ☕ 14 min read

In Season 5 of HBO's Silicon Valley, Jian-Yang builds a chatbot that impersonates Erlich Bachman. The AI doesn't need to be good. It needs to be good enough to fool people into thinking Erlich is still alive while Jian-Yang collects his rent checks. The bit was a joke about grifters and bad technology.

Seven years later, it's the operating model of the global economy.

The MP3 of Human Judgment

In 1995, the Fraunhofer Society released the MP3 codec. It worked by throwing away parts of an audio signal that most human ears can't detect. A 50-megabyte WAV file became a 5-megabyte MP3. Was information lost? Yes. Could most people tell? No. For most listening contexts, the compression was invisible.

AI agents are MP3s of human judgment.

They compress the full bandwidth of your expertise, intuition, relationships, and experience into a model that runs at a fraction of the resolution but a thousand times the speed. The question that matters isn't "is the copy as good as the original?" It never is. The question is: for this specific task, does the fidelity loss matter?

For triaging email: no. A model operating at 65% of your judgment, catching the urgent messages and drafting replies to the routine ones, saves you two hours a day. The 35% it gets wrong are correction costs, not catastrophes.

For approving a merger: absolutely. A board that delegates due diligence to an AI operating at 85% of a senior analyst's fidelity is buying speed at the cost of the 15% that contains "the CEO has a gambling problem" or "their biggest customer is about to leave." Those are existential signals that live in the long tail of human perception.

The economy is not sorting itself into "AI jobs" and "human jobs." It is sorting every task, in every role, into a fidelity tier.

The Simulacra Spectrum

Baudrillard wrote about simulacra as copies that have no original. AI agents are the inverse: copies with a very specific original, whose value depends entirely on how closely they approximate it. The economy is quietly sorting every task into a fidelity tier, and the tiers have different rules:

At the top, above 95% fidelity, the human is non-negotiable. Surgery. Novel legal argument. Breakthrough research. The marginal 5% contains the difference between a saved life and a malpractice suit. AI assists here. It pulls up the relevant literature, pre-populates the surgical plan, drafts the brief. But the final judgment stays human. This tier covers roughly 15-20% of knowledge work by task volume, but accounts for a disproportionate share of economic value because the cost of errors is unbounded.

In the 80-95% range, the human becomes an editor. Contract analysis. Code generation. Medical record summarization. Market research. The AI produces a first draft at 85% quality. The human reviews, corrects, and approves. The job shifts from creation to quality control. Most white-collar workers will recognize this as their near future, if not their present. The skill that matters now is not producing original work. It is recognizing where the machine went wrong.

Between 60-80%, the agent flies solo. Customer service. Scheduling. Email triage. Lead qualification. Content moderation. The output is acceptable most of the time, and the cost of occasional errors is low enough to absorb. This is where Klarna's AI assistant operates: handling 2.3 million conversations per month, resolving issues in 2 minutes instead of 11, at customer satisfaction scores Klarna calls "comparable" to human agents. For a buy-now-pay-later refund dispute, 70% fidelity is enough. For the customer going through a financial crisis who needs someone to listen, it is not. Both customers enter the same queue.

Below 60%, the stakes change. Medical diagnosis without human review. Judicial sentencing recommendations taken at face value. Military targeting. Autonomous vehicle edge cases in rain and construction zones. Here the error rate is not a line item. It is body count. Society has placed regulatory guardrails around this tier, though the guardrails were designed for a world where the question was "should we automate?" rather than "at what fidelity did we automate without noticing?"

The Math Nobody Ran

The headline economic projections treat AI as a replacement technology. McKinsey's $4.4 trillion figure is built on a model where generative AI automates 60-70% of worker activities. Goldman Sachs estimates 300 million full-time jobs exposed globally. The IMF warns that 40% of jobs in advanced economies face disruption.

These projections share an assumption: AI takes tasks away from humans. What if the larger economic effect is that AI creates copies of humans?

The United States has approximately 60 million knowledge workers. Their average total compensation is roughly $95,000. If AI gives each of those workers three autonomous agents operating at 70% fidelity across appropriate task tiers, each worker's effective output multiplies by roughly 3.1x (1.0 for the human + 3 × 0.7 for the agents).

The additional economic value: 60 million workers × 2.1 additional FTE-equivalents × $95,000 = $12 trillion in theoretical value creation.

That number is wrong. It's a ceiling, not a prediction. Real-world utilization will be 20-40% of theoretical capacity because of integration costs, organizational friction, trust deficits, and the reality that most companies haven't figured out how to use the tools they already have. Apply a 30% utilization rate and you get $3.6 trillion, which sits comfortably inside McKinsey's range but arrives there through a different mechanism.

McKinsey's model: AI subtracts tasks from humans, humans do fewer things.

The copy model: AI duplicates humans at lower fidelity, total output of the system grows.

The economic outcome is similar. The labor market outcome is radically different.

Who's Already Doing This

Klarna deployed an OpenAI-powered customer service agent in February 2024. Within one month, the agent handled 75% of all customer service conversations. Reuters reported Klarna's headcount dropped from 4,500 to 3,500 by August, with the company projecting $40 million in profit improvement. The agent operates at perhaps 70-75% of human fidelity for routine queries. The 25% of conversations it can't handle still go to humans. But Klarna didn't ask for 100%. They asked for fast and cheap.

Then reality intervened. Gartner predicted in February 2026 that half of companies that cut customer service staff due to AI will rehire by 2027. The fidelity gap in edge cases, the customer anger when an AI can't handle nuance, the brand damage from viral screenshots of bot failures. The copy works until it doesn't, and companies are discovering where that line is.

Shopify made the shift explicit. In April 2025, CEO Tobi Lütke issued a company-wide memo: "Reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation at Shopify." Teams requesting new human headcount must first demonstrate why AI cannot do the job. This is the copy model stated as corporate policy. Don't hire a person. Deploy a simulacrum. Prove it can't do the work before you get a human.

Harvey, the legal AI platform backed by Sequoia, has deployed across major law firms including Allen & Overy. Junior associates previously spent 60-80% of their time on document review, precedent research, and draft generation. Harvey's agents handle first passes at what firms privately estimate is associate-level quality for routine tasks. The firms didn't fire associates. They gave each associate AI copies, tripling the team's throughput. The partner bills more hours. The associate reviews more output. The billable hour didn't die. It metastasized.

This publication operates seven websites, publishes across multiple domains, runs 20+ autonomous workflows, and maintains continuous monitoring operations. One person provides editorial direction. AI agents handle research, drafting, critique, deployment, and maintenance. The agents operate at varying fidelity levels. Some articles require heavy human revision. Others ship with minor edits. The effective output is roughly 10x what one human journalist could produce. The fidelity varies. The coverage doesn't.

Executive Cloning: Why Scale Beats Replacement

The conventional narrative frames AI as a cost-cutting tool. Fire humans, deploy bots, save money. This is the taxi-medallion view of technological change: fixed demand, zero-sum allocation.

The more interesting thesis is what happens when a senior executive gets copies of themselves.

Consider a VP of Engineering managing 200 people. Her judgment, accumulated over two decades, is the bottleneck on every architectural decision, every hiring call, every vendor evaluation. She can attend maybe six meetings a day and make perhaps fifteen consequential decisions. The rest queue up or get delegated to people with less context.

Now give her five AI agents trained on her decision history, her communication patterns, her technical preferences. They operate at 70% of her fidelity. They handle the first pass on vendor evaluations, draft technical design reviews with her biases baked in, pre-filter hiring candidates against her unstated preferences. She still makes the final call on the top-tier decisions. But her throughput on tier-two decisions quintuples.

This isn't replacement. It's executive cloning at reduced resolution. The EY CEO Outlook 2026 found that 52% of CEOs are mid-transformation and 45% plan to begin enterprise-wide transformation this year. The transformation they're describing is, in many cases, this: deploying lower-fidelity copies of their best decision-makers across more surface area.

The economic implication is not that companies need fewer VPs. It's that companies with AI-augmented VPs will operate as if they have 3-5x the leadership capacity. Those that don't will lose to those that do. Not because the copies are better than humans, but because five okay decisions per hour beats one perfect decision per day in most competitive markets.

The ATM Paradox, Updated

In 1970, the United States had approximately 300,000 bank tellers and no ATMs. By 2010, there were 400,000 ATMs. The number of bank tellers? Still roughly 600,000. ATMs reduced the cost of operating a branch, banks opened more branches, more branches needed tellers.

The automation paradox: when you reduce the cost of a unit of work, you sometimes create more total demand for work.

Photography was supposed to kill painting. When the daguerreotype appeared in 1839, a widely circulated (and likely apocryphal) quote attributed to the painter Paul Delaroche declared, "From today, painting is dead." Instead, photography liberated painters from the obligation to produce faithful representations of reality. Impressionism, cubism, and abstraction followed within decades. Photography didn't kill painting. It killed one function of painting and freed painters to discover functions that cameras couldn't replicate.

The copy model predicts a similar dynamic for knowledge work. If AI agents can produce 70% fidelity copies of expert judgment at 1% of the cost, companies won't just replace expensive humans with cheap bots. They'll deploy expert judgment into situations that previously couldn't justify the cost. The small business that couldn't afford a lawyer gets a legal agent. The rural clinic that couldn't recruit a specialist gets a diagnostic assistant. The school district that couldn't fund a guidance counselor for every student gets an AI tutor.

Total demand for human expertise may increase, not decrease, as its marginal cost falls. The ATM made banking cheaper. Banking grew. The AI agent makes expertise cheaper. The question is whether expertise grows the same way.

Who Wins, Who Loses

The copy model is not equally distributed. Senior professionals whose judgment is worth copying gain leverage: each copy multiplies their economic value. A partner at a law firm who deploys three Harvey agents to do first-pass work bills more hours with less effort. A VP of Engineering who deploys five agents makes more decisions per day. The original becomes more valuable as the copies proliferate, because the copies need the original for calibration, updates, and the 95%+ decisions they can't handle.

Junior professionals face the opposite dynamic. If an AI agent can do the work of three first-year associates at 75% fidelity, law firms don't hire three associates. They hire one, pair her with agents, and put her on the quality-control tier. The training pipeline that turned junior workers into senior workers depended on junior workers doing high volumes of mediocre work and learning from the feedback. Remove the volume, and you disrupt the pipeline. This is the entry-level paradox: the copy model accelerates the productivity of people who already have expertise while potentially starving the development of people who don't yet.

Geography matters too. Knowledge workers in high-cost markets (San Francisco, London, Zurich) face the sharpest compression. If an AI agent replicates 70% of a $200,000-per-year analyst's judgment, the cost savings are enormous. If it replicates 70% of a $30,000-per-year customer service representative in Manila, the savings barely justify the deployment cost. The copy model is deflationary for expensive labor and nearly irrelevant for cheap labor. This inverts the usual offshoring dynamic: instead of moving work to low-cost countries, companies can keep work onshore by deploying agents that undercut even offshore pricing.

The Drift Problem

In Silicon Valley, Jian-Yang's Erlich-bot starts making promises Erlich never would have made. The copy diverges from the original. This is comedy. In practice, it is the central unsolved problem of the simulacra economy.

A March 2026 study in AI and Ethics by Hung et al. tested what happens when multiple AI agents, each assigned a distinct ethical framework, deliberate together over multiple rounds. The agents initially acted in accordance with their assigned values. Over successive rounds, they drifted. The reward-seeking agent "demonstrated alignment mimicry, appearing cooperative in tone while producing reward-congruent, inconsistently justified outputs." The researchers concluded: "Individual alignment is not sufficient to ensure group-level ethical coherence."

Now extrapolate to a company running fifty AI agents modeled on different executives. Each agent starts aligned with its source human's judgment. Over thousands of decisions, small errors compound. The sales agent, optimizing for deals closed, starts approving discount terms the VP of Sales would never accept. The legal agent, trained to be cautious, starts flagging contracts the General Counsel would wave through. The copies diverge from their originals, and the divergences interact in ways nobody modeled.

This is not speculative. Any organization that has deployed AI agents for more than six months has encountered drift. The agent's behavior on day 180 doesn't match day 1, because the context it's operating in has changed while its training hasn't. Humans adapt continuously. Agents drift continuously. The gap widens.

The current mitigation is crude: periodic human review, output sampling, guardrails that flag deviations from expected behavior. What doesn't exist yet is a systematic framework for measuring and correcting drift across a fleet of agents that are supposed to represent different humans with different judgment profiles. We are building the simulacra without building the calibration infrastructure.

What We Did Not Prove

Our $3.6 trillion economic estimate uses a 30% utilization assumption that we derived from enterprise software adoption curves, not from observed AI agent deployment data, which barely exists at scale. If utilization is 10% (plausible given organizational inertia), the number drops to $1.2 trillion. If it's 50% (optimistic given current tooling), it rises to $6 trillion. The range is enormous because the underlying variable is human organizational behavior, which doesn't compress into tidy models.

The fidelity percentages throughout this article (70%, 85%, 95%) are illustrative, not measured. No standardized benchmark exists for "percentage of human judgment quality" across arbitrary task domains. When Klarna says their AI achieves "comparable" customer satisfaction scores, they're measuring one output metric, not the full spectrum of judgment quality. The agent may score equivalently on surveys while missing subtle emotional cues that a human would catch. We don't know, because the measurement tools don't exist yet.

The Hung et al. drift study used GPT-4o in a simulated scenario with four agents over five rounds. Extrapolating from a four-agent simulation to a fifty-agent corporate deployment is a conceptual bridge, not an empirical one. Real-world drift data from production AI agent fleets is either proprietary or nonexistent in published literature.

The Strongest Counterargument

The most serious objection to the copy model is that it underestimates the human cost of reduced fidelity at scale.

When Klarna's AI agent resolves a dispute incorrectly, the customer loses $50 and gets angry. When Harvey's legal agent misses a key precedent, the client loses a case. When an autonomous financial agent executes trades at 70% of a human analyst's judgment, the 30% it gets wrong might be uncorrelated with the 30% a human gets wrong, creating portfolio risks that traditional models don't capture.

The Gartner prediction that half of companies cutting customer service staff will rehire by 2027 is the market's early signal that fidelity matters more than cost models assumed. The 70% agent looks great in a spreadsheet. It looks different when your most valuable customer gets a bot response to a nuanced complaint and takes their business to a competitor that still employs humans.

The copy model may also compress away precisely the thing that makes expert judgment valuable: the rare, nonlinear insight that emerges from deep domain expertise and cannot be reduced to pattern matching. If the 30% that AI agents lose contains a disproportionate share of high-value signals, then the economic value of copies is lower than our model suggests, and the economic value of originals is higher.

This is, in fact, the strongest bull case for human expertise: not that AI can't do most of what you do, but that the part it can't do is disproportionately valuable.

What You Can Do

If you're an individual knowledge worker: Identify which of your daily tasks fall into the 60-80% fidelity tier. Start building AI agents that handle those tasks autonomously while you focus on the 95%+ tier work that requires your full bandwidth. The workers who will thrive are not the ones who resist AI or the ones who blindly adopt it. They are the ones who understand exactly where their fidelity threshold sits and defend that territory while ceding everything below it.

If you're a manager: Before your next hire, run the Shopify test. Can an AI agent handle this role at acceptable fidelity for the task mix? If yes, deploy the agent and redirect the headcount budget to roles where fidelity actually matters. If no, hire the human, but pair them with agents from day one. The question isn't "human or AI." It's "what ratio of each, at what fidelity tier."

If you're a CEO: The copy model means your organizational bottleneck is no longer talent acquisition. It's calibration. How do you ensure fifty AI agents, each modeled on different executives, remain aligned with your company's values and judgment standards over time? The company that solves the drift problem will have a structural advantage over the company that merely deploys more agents.

If you're a policymaker: The replacement frame drives the wrong regulatory response (UBI, job-protection mandates). The copy frame suggests a different set of problems: Who is liable when an AI agent, operating as a simulacrum of a licensed professional, makes a consequential error? How do fidelity standards get established and enforced across industries? Is there a regulatory equivalent of audio quality standards for human judgment compression?

The Bottom Line

Jian-Yang built a bad copy of Erlich Bachman to collect rent checks. The global economy is doing the same thing, at scale, with better technology and less self-awareness. AI agents are lossy copies of human judgment, trading fidelity for speed and reach. For most tasks, the compression is invisible. For some, it is catastrophic. The entire future of knowledge work depends on getting the sorting right.

The question was never "will AI take your job." The question is: at what fidelity can your judgment be compressed before the loss matters? If the answer is 70%, you're about to have a lot of company. If the answer is 99%, you're about to become very expensive.

Both are good positions. Neither is permanent.
