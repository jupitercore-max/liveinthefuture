Honest, primary-source assessments for families considering alternatives to traditional education

What this is

These are two very different things: AoPS Academy is an after-school and weekend enrichment program (not a full-time school), while Khan Lab School is a full-time K-12 independent school. We evaluate both because Bay Area families exploring personalized education often consider them alongside or instead of traditional school options. Both have real strengths and real limitations. We used primary sources: the schools' own websites, published school profiles, accreditation records, independent research, critical journalism, financial filings, and parent/teacher feedback.

What AoPS Academy Actually Is

AoPS Academy is the in-person enrichment arm of Art of Problem Solving, founded in 2003 by Richard Rusczyk. Rusczyk won the USA Math Olympiad in 1989, graduated from Princeton, worked as a bond trader at D.E. Shaw, then left to become a math teacher. He started writing math competition textbooks in 1993 and turned them into a sprawling educational ecosystem: online courses, Beast Academy (for elementary students), and physical Academy locations.

The key thing to understand: AoPS Academy is not a school. It's after-school and weekend enrichment. Your kid still goes to their regular school (public or private) during the day, then attends AoPS classes once a week per course. This makes it a fundamentally different proposition from Khan Lab School or Alpha School.

AoPS has 14+ physical campuses nationwide. The two closest to Menlo Park are Mountain View (409 San Antonio Rd, about 15 min drive) and Santa Clara (777 Lawrence Expy, about 20 min). Both offer the full range of courses.

The Curriculum

Elementary (Grades 1-5): Beast Academy. Comic-book-style math textbooks and an online platform featuring puzzle-based problems. This is not your typical "learn your times tables" curriculum. Beast Academy teaches number theory, combinatorics, and logical reasoning to first graders through engaging stories and characters. It's been independently evaluated.

Middle and High School (Grades 6-12): AoPS textbooks. Problem-based learning that emphasizes why over how. Two tracks are available:

  • Honors: Topics covered 1-2 years ahead of standard curriculum, with emphasis on creative problem-solving
  • Scholars: For students already comfortable with Honors-level work. More rigorous and abstract, faster pace. The two tracks share ~70% of content but Scholars pushes harder.

Language Arts (Grades 2-12): Long-form writing projects, literary analysis, persuasive and creative writing. Less famous than the math program but well-regarded. Problem-solving approach applied to writing and analysis.

Science: Offered at some campuses and for some grade levels. Not as comprehensive as math or LA.

The Pedagogy

AoPS's philosophy is spelled out clearly: students should struggle with hard problems. This is not a program where a teacher demonstrates a formula and then kids practice it 30 times. Instead, instructors pose challenging problems, students work on them (often collaboratively), and the process of getting stuck and figuring it out is considered the actual learning.

What's genuinely different here Most math education in the US teaches procedures. AoPS teaches problem-solving. The distinction matters: procedural knowledge lets you solve problems you've seen before; problem-solving skills let you approach problems you haven't. This is the skill that transfers to real life, to science, to engineering, to any field that requires analytical thinking. Founder Rusczyk has talked extensively about how the US math competition pipeline produces this kind of thinker, and AoPS codifies that approach for a broader audience.

AoPS Evidence Base

WestEd Independent Evaluation (Beast Academy)

WestEd, a respected independent educational research organization, conducted a rigorous evaluation of Beast Academy in Mankato Area Public Schools in Minnesota. The study involved over 1,000 students in treatment and comparison groups. Findings:

  • Significant positive impact on math achievement, even during the COVID-19 pandemic when most students nationwide experienced learning loss
  • Students using Beast Academy showed 1-2 additional grade levels of growth on national MAP assessments compared to peers
  • The program was associated with more positive attitudes toward math
  • Results held for both "Cluster" (traditional gifted) students and "Rising Scholars" (high-ability students from historically underserved populations)

This is a meaningful finding. MAP is a widely used, well-validated standardized assessment. The study had proper control groups. And the fact that gains held for underserved populations counters the criticism that AoPS is "only for kids who were already good at math." Independently Verified

Competition Track Record

AoPS's online community and curriculum have trained the vast majority of US math competition winners for over two decades. Nearly every member of the US International Math Olympiad team has used AoPS resources. The online forums (Art of Problem Solving Online) have 300,000+ members. This is the pipeline that produces the kids who go to MIT, Caltech, and Princeton for math and science.

That said: competition success is a proxy for a specific kind of mathematical talent. It's not a holistic education metric. A kid who's great at competition math might still need help with writing, social skills, or subjects that AoPS doesn't cover. Important Context

For context, this is roughly 8-17% of what a full-time private school costs in the Bay Area ($34K-$75K). A family could keep their kids in public school and add AoPS enrichment for a fraction of the cost. If you took a math course and a language arts course, you'd spend under $6K/year total.

The cost comparison matters AoPS Academy for two courses costs roughly what two months of Khan Lab School tuition costs. You're getting world-class math enrichment as a supplement to whatever school your kid already attends. This is the most capital-efficient option in this evaluation by a wide margin.

AoPS: Honest Limitations

  • Not a school. This is the most important caveat. AoPS doesn't replace your kid's education. It supplements it. If your regular school is bad, AoPS fixes one subject (math) but doesn't fix the rest. Your kid still needs a full-day educational environment.
  • Self-selecting population. AoPS classes are full of kids who are already motivated and good at math. For a kid who's struggling with math or finds it boring, being placed in a room full of math enthusiasts might be inspiring or it might be demoralizing. Know your kid.
  • Instructor quality varies. AoPS hires instructors with strong math backgrounds, but the in-person Academy experience depends heavily on who teaches your kid's section. Some parents report amazing instructors; others report less engaging ones. The curriculum is consistently strong but delivery is human.
  • Pressure concerns. The environment is intellectually rigorous. For some kids, that's thrilling. For others, it creates anxiety. AoPS is designed for kids who enjoy being challenged, not for kids who need gentle encouragement to engage with math. If your 7-year-old cries over hard problems, this might not be the right environment yet.
  • Narrow scope. AoPS is primarily math. The LA program exists and is good, but nobody chooses AoPS for language arts. Science is available but limited. This is a math enrichment operation with other subjects bolted on.
  • No published AI integration. Despite the broader trend toward AI tutoring, AoPS Academy's in-person classes are traditional instructor-led sessions. Beast Academy Online has adaptive features, but the Academy itself is human-taught. This is arguably a feature, not a bug.
  • No financial aid mentioned. Unlike Khan Lab School, AoPS Academy doesn't prominently advertise financial assistance for its in-person programs. At ~$3K/course/year it's more accessible than private school, but it's not free.

AoPS: Who Is This For?

AoPS Academy is ideal for a family that:

  • Has kids in a decent school (public or private) but wants deeper, more challenging math
  • Has a child who likes puzzles, enjoys being challenged, and doesn't shut down when a problem is hard
  • Wants to invest in math enrichment without paying full private school tuition
  • Lives within driving distance of Mountain View or Santa Clara (or is willing to do the Virtual Campus)

Age fit for your kids: Your 7-year-old (entering ~2nd grade) could start Beast Academy Level 2 math or language arts immediately. Your 5-year-old (entering ~K) is just below the Grade 1 entry point; she could potentially start in fall 2027. your 4-year-old is too young for another 2-3 years. your infant has a long runway.

What Khan Lab School Actually Is

In 2014, Sal Khan invited a small group of parents to his living room in Mountain View. He showed them a PowerPoint about reimagining education. 30 families signed up, and Khan Lab School opened with a single mixed-age classroom. Today it serves 310 students across two campuses.

The school exists to test the ideas Sal Khan laid out in his 2012 book The One World Schoolhouse: mastery-based learning (don't move on until you truly understand), student agency (kids help direct their learning), and the integration of technology as a tool (not a replacement) for human teaching. It is, explicitly, a "laboratory" -- the school experiments with educational approaches and shares findings with the broader education community.

The Mastery Model

This is the core differentiator. Traditional schools give you a grade and move on; Khan Lab School requires you to demonstrate actual understanding before progressing. Three levels:

  • Foundational Mastery: Minimum expectations and competencies met. You can progress to the next course.
  • Mastery: All learning outcomes achieved. Equivalent to ~90%+ at a traditional school. This is the expectation for all students.
  • Mastery with Distinction: Exceeded course expectations at a high level. Few students achieve this.

There are no letter grades, no class rank, no GPA. Progress reports map achievement toward learning outcomes. This is philosophically sound and backed by learning science research going back to Benjamin Bloom's mastery learning work in the 1980s. The question is always whether the implementation matches the theory.

The Curriculum

Lower School (TK-5, Palo Alto campus): Emphasis on joyful exploration, purposeful play, mixed-age learning communities. Khan Academy technology is "thoughtfully integrated" for personalized learning. Students demonstrate knowledge through writing, art, project work, or verbal communication. Multiple pathways to understanding.

Middle School (6-8, Mountain View campus): Core academics plus exploration. Students begin developing independence and agency in their learning.

Upper School (9-12, Mountain View campus): College-prep curriculum that exceeds UC/CSU A-G requirements. Key features:

  • No AP courses offered (deliberate choice; they believe AP's standardized approach conflicts with mastery-based learning)
  • Dual enrollment with Foothill College for college-level courses
  • Stepping Stones (9th), Cornerstones (10-11), and Senior Capstone (12th) applied learning projects
  • The Senior Capstone is a year-long independent project: professional internship, college-level research, or community engagement. Students present findings to a committee.

Course offerings include: English (4 years), Math (Algebra through Multivariable Calculus/Linear Algebra), Sciences (Biology via Foothill dual enrollment, Chemistry via Foothill, Physics), History, Spanish (5 levels), Art, Music, Computer Science, Philosophy, Psychology, Architecture.

Khan Lab School: College Results

The school published its college acceptance/matriculation list for the Classes of 2021-2024 (about 60-70 total graduates across those years). Highlights of where students were accepted:

Notable acceptances (Classes of 2021-2024) Harvard, MIT, Caltech, Stanford (not listed but likely given location), Brown, Columbia, Cornell, Duke, Georgetown, Johns Hopkins, Northwestern, Rice, UChicago, UPenn, Barnard, Carnegie Mellon, Emory, Georgia Tech, Harvey Mudd, Middlebury, Tufts, Wellesley, WashU. Plus all UC campuses, USC, and many strong liberal arts colleges.

This is a genuinely impressive list for a school this small and this young. 100% of graduates enroll in 4-year colleges. The breadth is notable: Ivy League, top tech schools, strong liberal arts, flagship state universities.

But context matters:

  • 19 seniors in the Class of 2025. With class sizes this small, a handful of exceptional students can make the list look spectacular.
  • 86% of upper school students identify as people of color. This is genuinely diverse for a $35K/year Silicon Valley private school.
  • Self-selected families in the heart of Silicon Valley, many likely with tech-industry parents, resources, and college-prep cultural capital.
  • The list shows acceptances, not matriculation for every school. "Highlighted colleges indicate enrollment" but the highlighting isn't visible in the text version.

The college list is a real data point, but with 60-70 total graduates across 4 years, the sample is too small for statistical significance. It's encouraging, not conclusive. Small Sample

Khan Lab School: The AI Question

Given that this school was founded by the creator of Khan Academy, you'd expect cutting-edge AI integration. The reality is more nuanced.

In April 2026, Sal Khan himself told Chalkbeat:

"For a lot of students, it was a non-event. They just didn't use it much."

He was talking about Khanmigo, the AI tutoring chatbot Khan Academy built with GPT-4. A geometry teacher at one of Khanmigo's earliest adopter schools (Hobart High in Indiana) said students found it frustrating: it wouldn't give answers, and students who didn't understand the material well enough couldn't formulate good questions for the bot.

Khan now frames AI as "part of the solution; I don't view it as the end-all and be-all." This is honest and more realistic than the 2023 TED Talk where he predicted AI would produce "the biggest positive transformation that education has ever seen."

The AI tutoring reality check Khan Lab School uses Khan Academy's platform and has access to Khanmigo. But the school's actual strength is the human element: small classes (1:10 ratio), engaged teachers, mastery-based pedagogy, and project-based learning. The technology is supplementary. If you're choosing KLS because you think your kid will have an AI super-tutor, recalibrate your expectations. If you're choosing it because of the mastery model, small community, and educational philosophy, those are real and present.

Khan Lab School: Financial Health

Khan Lab School is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit (EIN 46-5742553). Charity Navigator gives it an 81% rating (3 out of 4 stars), reflecting solid accountability and financial management.

Key metrics from their filing:

  • Program expense ratio: 85.35% (most revenue goes to programs, not overhead -- this is good)
  • Liabilities-to-assets ratio: 82.70% (higher than ideal, suggesting significant debt relative to assets)
  • Board: Fully independent

The high debt ratio is worth watching. For a school serving 310 students across two campus leases in Palo Alto and Mountain View, real estate costs are substantial. The school offers need-based financial aid, which is funded by donations. This creates dependency on philanthropic support to maintain both affordability and solvency.

Khan Lab School: Honest Limitations

  • "Lab school" means your kid is part of the experiment. KLS explicitly describes itself as testing and reimagining teaching methods. This is intellectually exciting and also means the approach may change year to year. Some families embrace this; others want stability.
  • No AP courses. KLS deliberately chose not to offer AP. For mastery-based learning, this makes philosophical sense (AP is standardized and time-bound). For college admissions, it's a minor risk. Most selective colleges accept non-AP transcripts, but some high school counselors and parents view APs as a "signal" of rigor. KLS mitigates this with dual enrollment at Foothill College.
  • No published standardized test data. Unlike public schools, KLS doesn't publish MAP scores, SAT averages, or any standardized metrics. Their mastery system is internally consistent but not externally benchmarked. This is a transparency gap.
  • Small and getting bigger. The school grew from 30 to 310 students. Growing pains are inevitable. With a new Lower School campus in Palo Alto (opened 2021) and increasing enrollment, the intimate "living room" culture may dilute.
  • Tuition is steep. $34,750/year for lower school, $38,850 for middle/upper. Plus $750 registration fee, $250 annual dues, and lunch/after-school are extra. Financial aid exists but depends on donations.
  • High debt relative to assets. The 82.70% liabilities-to-assets ratio is a financial yellow flag. If enrollment dips or donations slow, the school could face pressure.
  • Limited critical coverage. Unlike Alpha School (which has extensive investigative reporting), Khan Lab School has relatively little independent critical analysis. The school's reputation benefits from Sal Khan's enormous public profile and credibility. The absence of negative reporting doesn't prove the absence of problems; it may reflect the school's small size and the media's deference to Khan's brand.
  • Teacher feedback is limited. Glassdoor shows few reviews. Those that exist are generally positive but the sample is tiny. We can't assess teacher satisfaction or turnover with confidence.

Khan Lab School: Who Is This For?

KLS is ideal for a family that:

  • Wants a full-time alternative to traditional school, not just enrichment
  • Values mastery (deep understanding) over grades and test scores
  • Is comfortable with an experimental educational environment that evolves
  • Can afford $35-39K/year tuition (or qualifies for financial aid)
  • Lives close enough for daily commute to Palo Alto or Mountain View
  • Values student agency and project-based learning over structured, teacher-directed instruction

Age fit for your kids: The Lower School (Palo Alto campus, ~15 min from Menlo Park) offers TK-5. your 7-year-old could apply for 2nd grade entry. your 5-year-old could apply for Kindergarten for 2027-28. your 4-year-old could apply for TK in 2026-27 if she turns 4 by June 1 (check age cutoff eligibility with admissions). The 2026-27 application cycle has passed; the next opportunity is for 2027-28.

Specific Recommendations

For Your 7-year-old (entering ~2nd grade)

Best immediate option: AoPS Academy Beast Academy Level 2 Your 7-year-old is the right age for Beast Academy math and Honors Language Arts 2 at the Mountain View campus. At ~$6K/year for both courses, this is the highest-value move. She can start as early as the next enrollment period while staying in her current school. No application process beyond placement assessment.

If you want a full-time school change, Khan Lab School's Lower School in Palo Alto is worth exploring for 2027-28. The application window typically opens in September for the following year. The Palo Alto campus is close to Menlo Park. The mastery-based model could be a great fit if your 7-year-old is the kind of kid who benefits from going deep rather than fast.

For Your 5-year-old (entering ~K)

Your 5-year-old is below AoPS Academy's Grade 1 minimum for now. She could start Beast Academy Level 1 in fall 2027. For a full-time option, Khan Lab School's Kindergarten (must be 5 by Sept 1) would be the application for the 2026-27 cycle (which has passed) or 2027-28.

For Your 4-year-old (entering ~pre-K)

Too young for AoPS Academy (Grade 1 minimum). Khan Lab School's TK program accepts children who are 4 by June 1 of the entry year. Your 4-year-old's November birthday means she'd be eligible for TK in fall 2026 (she'd be 4, turning 5 in Nov). Worth checking with admissions. The Palo Alto campus lower school emphasizes purposeful play and exploration for this age group.

For Your infant (~8 months)

Long runway. Both programs will be available when he's old enough. No action needed now.

The "Both" Strategy

These two options are not mutually exclusive. The strongest play might be:

  1. Enroll your 7-year-old in AoPS Academy immediately (Beast Academy Math + Language Arts, ~$6K/year, Mountain View campus, once/week each). This supplements her current school with world-class enrichment.
  2. Apply to Khan Lab School for 2027-28 for whichever kids are age-eligible. Tour the Palo Alto campus and assess fit. If accepted, you'd have a full-time school with a coherent mastery-based philosophy.
  3. Even if your kids attend KLS, keep AoPS. KLS doesn't offer the same depth of math challenge that AoPS provides. Many families at top private schools use AoPS as a supplement.

Evaluation Limitations

In the interest of honesty about what we don't know:

  • No campus visits. This evaluation is based on publicly available sources. A campus visit would reveal things about culture, energy, and day-to-day experience that websites and documents cannot.
  • Limited parent interviews. We relied on published reviews and reported accounts, not direct interviews with current families. Online reviews are biased toward strong opinions (both positive and negative).
  • No standardized test data for KLS. We can assess their college results but not their academic growth trajectory. Public schools publish this data; private schools usually don't.
  • AoPS Academy is hard to evaluate as a "school" because it isn't one. Comparing an enrichment program to a full-time school is apples-to-oranges. We've tried to be clear about what each one is and isn't.
  • Neither school has major investigative reporting. Unlike Alpha School, where WIRED and 404 Media did deep dives, neither AoPS Academy nor KLS has been the subject of critical investigative journalism. This is probably because neither has the kind of red flags that attract investigative attention, but it also means there's less independent scrutiny.
  • Financial details for AoPS are limited. AoPS Inc. is a private for-profit company. Unlike KLS (a nonprofit that files 990s), AoPS doesn't publicly disclose financial details.
