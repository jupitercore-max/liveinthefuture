Flock Safety Solved 700,000 Crimes Last Year. A Texas Deputy Used It to Track a Woman Who Had an Abortion.

Automated license plate readers cut San Francisco major crimes 44% and helped Tulsa solve 100% of its 2024 homicides. They also let a single law enforcement query search 83,345 cameras across nearly the entire United States with no warrant, no case number, and no oversight. Your neighborhood can get the crime reduction without the mass surveillance. Here is exactly how.

By Elena Vasquez · April 6, 2026 · ☕ 14 min read

On a Tuesday in early 2025, a Johnson County, Texas deputy opened Flock Safety's search interface and typed two words: "had abortion." The system returned results from 6,809 connected law enforcement networks accessing 83,345 cameras spread across nearly the entire country. The deputy was investigating what officials classified as a "death investigation" of a "non-viable fetus." A second search probed the same network. The notes read: "had an abortion, search for female."

That same year, Flock Safety reported its cameras had contributed to solving 700,000 crimes, locating more than 1,000 missing persons, and helping Tulsa, Oklahoma achieve a 100% homicide clearance rate for the first time in 50 years. In Lakewood, Washington, a city of 65,000 that had averaged a murder per month, Flock helped deliver its first full year without a single homicide in two decades.

Both of these things are true at the same time. That is the problem.

The Numbers That Make You Want Cameras

San Francisco deployed Flock cameras alongside other policing reforms starting in 2023. By 2025, major crimes had fallen 44%. Larceny was cut in half. Car thefts dropped 54%. Burglaries fell 33%. Flock's founder Garrett Langley started the company in 2017 after a string of burglaries in his Atlanta neighborhood. The first prototype was a $1,000 camera he built on his dining room table with co-founders Matt Feury and Paige Todd, all Georgia Tech alumni.

The technology is straightforward. Solar-powered cameras with LTE connectivity photograph every vehicle that passes. Computer vision extracts not just the license plate but what Flock calls a "Vehicle Fingerprint": make, model, color, bumper stickers, roof racks, damage patterns. Even a car with no plate or a stolen plate generates a searchable identity. The system performs over 20 billion vehicle scans per month across more than 5,000 communities in 49 states.

The pitch to neighborhoods is simple: crime is an information problem. Most property crimes go unsolved because police have no leads. A camera at the entrance to your subdivision captures every vehicle that enters. When someone's catalytic converter gets stolen at 3 AM, police can pull the footage, identify the vehicle, cross-reference it against hot lists, and make an arrest. The FBI's 2019 data showed 83% of property crimes went unsolved. In Flock neighborhoods, that number drops precipitously.

Andreessen Horowitz, the venture capital firm, published an extensive profile of Flock in 2025 making exactly this case. Full disclosure: a16z is a Flock investor. Their data is real. Their incentive to present it favorably is also real.

The Numbers That Make You Want to Rip Them Out

In December 2025, the Electronic Frontier Foundation published the results of a year-long investigation based on datasets representing more than 12 million Flock searches logged by over 3,900 law enforcement agencies between December 2024 and October 2025.

The findings were systematic, not anecdotal.

Protest surveillance. Agencies logged hundreds of searches related to political demonstrations. Nineteen agencies conducted dozens of searches specifically tied to the No Kings protests in June and October 2025. Three agencies used the system to target activists from Direct Action Everywhere, an animal-rights organization. Delaware State Police queried the Flock network nine times in a single month related to the group's civil disobedience actions.

Racial profiling. More than 80 law enforcement agencies used language perpetuating harmful stereotypes against Romani people when searching the network. Grand Prairie Police Department in Texas searched for a racial slur six times while using Flock's "Convoy" feature, which identifies vehicles traveling together. The searches targeted an entire traveling community without specifying any suspected crime.

Reproductive rights. The Johnson County case was not hypothetical. It was logged. Deputies ran searches, consulted prosecutors about possible charges, and used a nationwide surveillance network to investigate a woman's private medical decision.

The architectural issue is not that bad actors misused a good system. It is that the system's architecture makes misuse trivially easy. One query. Eighty-three thousand cameras. Nearly the entire country. No warrant required. No case number required. No real-time oversight. The search logs EFF obtained were only available because of Freedom of Information Act requests, which took months to process. By the time the public learns about a search, the surveillance has long since occurred.

The Architecture Is the Problem

Flock announced privacy-enhancing features in June 2025: geofencing, retention limits, audit tools. EFF's assessment was blunt. "Flock Safety's feature updates cannot make ALPRs safe," they wrote. "The company promised privacy-enhancing features in response to public pressure. But these tweaks don't address the core problem: Flock's business model depends on building a nationwide, interconnected surveillance network that creates risks no software update can eliminate."

This is the crux. Flock's crime-solving power comes from network scale. A camera in your subdivision is useful. A camera in your subdivision connected to 83,000 other cameras across the country is powerful. But that same interconnection is what lets a deputy in Texas search the entire nation for a woman's license plate because she had an abortion. The feature and the bug are the same thing.

Your HOA cannot fix this with a better contract. Flock's Terms of Service give law enforcement access to the network. Your cameras feed the same system that surveilled protesters and profiled Romani travelers. You cannot opt into the crime reduction and opt out of the mass surveillance. The architecture does not permit it.

Where Are the Privacy Advocates When Homes Get Broken Into?

This is the question that every neighborhood meeting lands on, and it is the reason the debate is stuck.

Privacy advocates operate in the abstract. Civil liberties are theoretical until they are violated. The ACLU's position is principled and internally consistent: mass surveillance infrastructure is dangerous regardless of how much crime it solves, because the potential for abuse scales with the system, not the intent.

Crime victims operate in the visceral. Nobody whose catalytic converter was stolen at 3 AM, who watched the security footage of a stranger's car in their driveway, who filed a police report that went nowhere because there were no leads, nobody in that position cares about Romani profiling in Grand Prairie, Texas. They want the camera. They want the plate number. They want the arrest.

Both sides are right about their own concerns and wrong about dismissing the other's. The privacy advocates are correct that 83,000 interconnected cameras with minimal oversight is a mass surveillance apparatus regardless of who operates it. The homeowners are correct that 83% of property crimes going unsolved is an unacceptable failure of public safety. Telling a burglary victim to care about civil liberties theory is as tone-deaf as telling a wrongfully surveilled protester to care about property crime statistics.

The resolution is not rhetorical. It is architectural. You need cameras that solve crimes but do not feed a nationwide surveillance network. That system exists. You can build it this weekend.

Option 1: Community-Owned, Locally Processed

The simplest version: your neighborhood buys cameras, stores footage locally, and shares it with police only when a crime actually occurs.

Hardware: Reolink or Amcrest PoE cameras run $60 to $150 each. A 4-bay Synology NAS with 8TB of storage (enough for 30 days of footage from 8 cameras at 1080p continuous recording) costs approximately $450. Total hardware for a 4-entrance subdivision: $700 to $1,100. Annual cost after setup: electricity, roughly $60 per year. No subscription.

Software: Frigate NVR is open-source, runs on a Raspberry Pi 5 with a Hailo-8L AI accelerator, and supports real-time object detection. It distinguishes people, vehicles, animals, and packages. It integrates with Home Assistant for automated alerts. When a person is detected on your driveway at 3 AM, you get a push notification with a snapshot. The footage stays on hardware you own in a house in your neighborhood.

The critical difference: no cloud. No national network. The footage exists on a hard drive in someone's garage. Police do not have standing access. When a crime occurs, the HOA or the affected homeowner pulls the relevant clips and provides them voluntarily. This is how security cameras worked for 40 years before Flock turned them into a surveillance network.

The trade-off is real. You lose proactive hot-list alerts (the ability to flag a stolen vehicle the moment it enters your neighborhood). You lose the Vehicle Fingerprint matching that works even without plates. You lose the network effect that makes Flock powerful for solving crimes that span jurisdictions. What you gain is complete data sovereignty and zero exposure to the surveillance architecture EFF documented.

Option 2: Add Your Own License Plate Recognition

OpenALPR is an open-source automatic license plate recognition library. It runs on commodity hardware, reads plates from camera feeds, and stores the results in a local database. A Raspberry Pi 5 with a quality camera module can process plates in real time for under $150 in hardware.

Combined with Frigate, this gives you most of Flock's core functionality: vehicle detection, license plate capture, searchable history. The difference is that your database lives on your hardware, not Flock's cloud. You can maintain your own hot list (vehicles reported stolen in your area, vehicles associated with prior incidents in your neighborhood) without connecting to any external network.

The technical lift is meaningful. OpenALPR requires configuration: camera angle, IR illumination for night capture, database setup, alert routing. This is not a plug-and-play consumer product. But it is well-documented open-source software with an active community. If your neighborhood has one person comfortable with a Raspberry Pi and a terminal window, the setup takes a weekend.

Total cost for a 4-entrance neighborhood with ALPR: approximately $1,200 to $1,800 in hardware (cameras, Pi units, NAS, cabling). No ongoing subscription. Five-year cost: under $2,000. The same coverage from Flock: approximately $50,000 over five years.

Option 3: The Hybrid Model

For neighborhoods that want Flock's proven effectiveness but need to limit exposure, there is a contractual approach. Some HOAs have negotiated Flock contracts with specific restrictions:

  • 30-day data retention. Footage older than 30 days is automatically deleted. Flock's default varies by contract.
  • Geographic query limits. Only your local police department can search your cameras. Cross-jurisdiction access requires a warrant or written HOA approval.
  • Quarterly audit reports. Flock provides the HOA with a report of every search conducted against neighborhood cameras, including the searching agency, date, and stated purpose.
  • Purpose limitation. Searches must reference a specific case number tied to a reported crime. Broad surveillance searches (protest monitoring, ethnic profiling, immigration enforcement) are contractually prohibited.

The weakness of this approach is enforcement. EFF's investigation found that Flock's own feature updates and policy promises did not prevent the abuses documented in their dataset. A contractual restriction is only as strong as the monitoring behind it, and most HOAs do not have the resources to audit compliance. That said, a contract with audit provisions is meaningfully better than Flock's default terms, which give law enforcement broad network access with minimal oversight.

What a Community-Controlled System Actually Looks Like

One approach combines off-the-shelf hardware with community radio infrastructure. Cameras at neighborhood entry points feed into Frigate NVR on a local server. Object detection identifies vehicles and people. Optional ALPR captures plates. Alerts route through a private messaging group or radio channel. A GMRS repeater extends voice communication across the neighborhood for real-time coordination during incidents.

The architecture: cameras produce data that stays on local hardware. AI processes it locally. Alerts go to community members, not law enforcement networks. When a crime occurs, the relevant footage is pulled and shared with police voluntarily. The community controls what is recorded, how long it is stored, and who gets access. No deputy in Texas can query your cameras because the cameras are not connected to anything beyond your neighborhood's network.

This is not theoretical. Community radio networks using license-free GMRS frequencies already coordinate neighborhood watch activities across thousands of American communities. Police scanner monitoring via software-defined radio is legal and common. Adding AI-powered cameras to existing community communication infrastructure is an incremental step, not a moonshot.

What We Did Not Prove

This article does not prove that community-owned cameras reduce crime as effectively as Flock's network. Flock's crime reduction statistics come from cities with professional law enforcement integration, hot-list matching across jurisdictions, and the network effect of 83,000+ interconnected cameras. A neighborhood running Frigate on a NAS does not have these advantages. The deterrence effect of visible cameras may be similar, but the investigative power is substantially lower.

We also cannot verify Flock's crime reduction claims independently. The 44% drop in SF major crimes and Tulsa's 100% homicide clearance rate coincided with multiple policing changes, not just Flock deployment. Attributing the full effect to cameras requires controlled studies that do not exist at scale. Flock's data is self-reported and promoted by a venture-backed company with strong incentives to emphasize positive outcomes.

The cost comparison between Flock and DIY assumes a technically capable volunteer in the neighborhood handles setup and maintenance. Many neighborhoods do not have that person. Flock's professional installation and included maintenance have real value that the cost table understates.

The Strongest Counterargument

The best case for Flock's architecture is that network scale is precisely what solves the hardest crimes. A burglary ring operating across three counties is caught because cameras in all three counties are connected. A missing child is found because an AMBER alert triggers real-time plate matching across 83,000 cameras simultaneously. A serial catalytic converter thief is identified because the same Vehicle Fingerprint appears in footage from 14 different neighborhoods.

A community-owned camera that is not connected to any network cannot do any of this. The isolation that protects privacy also limits effectiveness. The 700,000 crimes Flock claims to have helped solve in 2025 were disproportionately solved because of network-scale matching. Local-only cameras would have solved fewer of them.

This is a genuine trade-off, not a false one. The question is whether the marginal crime-solving value of network interconnection justifies the documented surveillance abuses that interconnection enables. Reasonable people disagree. This article does not pretend to resolve that disagreement. It provides the alternative for people who have decided where they stand.

What You Can Do

If your neighborhood is considering Flock: Before signing, request the contract template and look for data retention terms, geographic query limits, audit provisions, and law enforcement access scope. Ask specifically whether your footage can be searched by agencies outside your local police department. Ask whether searches require a case number. If the contract does not address these, negotiate. If Flock will not negotiate, you have your answer about their priorities.

If you want community-controlled cameras: Start with two Reolink RLC-810A cameras ($110 each) at your neighborhood's primary entrances, a Raspberry Pi 5 ($80) running Frigate NVR, and a 2TB external drive ($60). Total: under $400. Run it for 60 days. When you have footage of a coyote at 2 AM, a package delivery at 3 PM, and your neighbor's contractor truck at 7 AM, you will understand both what the system captures and how little of it requires cloud connectivity.

If you want ALPR without the network: Add OpenALPR to the Frigate setup. Budget an additional weekend for configuration and an IR illuminator ($30) for night plate capture. Maintain a local hot list from your police department's published stolen vehicle reports. Your system will flag known stolen vehicles entering your neighborhood without connecting to Flock's national network.

If you want to push Flock to reform: The four architectural changes that would address EFF's documented abuses: mandatory geographic scoping on queries (default to local PD only), mandatory per-search case numbers tied to reported crimes, automatic public audit logs (not FOIA-dependent), and 30-day hard retention limits. These are engineering decisions, not philosophical ones. Flock could implement all four in a quarter. They have not.

The Bottom Line

Flock Safety built a system that demonstrably reduces crime. They also built a system that demonstrably enables mass surveillance, protest tracking, racial profiling, and reproductive rights violations. These are not separate systems. They are the same system. The feature that solves 700,000 crimes is the same feature that lets a Texas deputy search 83,000 cameras for a woman who had an abortion.

Your neighborhood does not have to accept this trade-off as given. For less than the cost of a single year of Flock's subscription for one camera, you can build a community-owned system with local AI processing, no cloud dependency, no national network exposure, and complete data sovereignty. You lose network-scale crime solving. You gain the certainty that your cameras will never be used to track protesters, profile ethnic minorities, or surveil someone's private medical decisions. For a growing number of communities, that certainty is worth more than the network.
