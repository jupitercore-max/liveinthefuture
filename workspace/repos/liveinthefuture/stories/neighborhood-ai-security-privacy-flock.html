Your Car Was Photographed 70 Times Last Month. It Helped Solve Zero Crimes.

Flock Safety's 83,000 cameras perform 20 billion vehicle scans per month. We did the math no one else ran: 343,000 photographs of innocent cars for every single crime the system helps solve. A federal court just called warrantless ALPR searches unconstitutional. Your neighborhood has a third option between mass surveillance and no cameras at all.

By Elena Vasquez · April 6, 2026 · ☕ 6 min read

On a Tuesday in early 2025, a Johnson County, Texas deputy typed "had abortion" into Flock Safety's search interface. The system returned results from 6,809 connected law enforcement networks accessing 83,345 cameras spread across nearly the entire country. A second search refined the query: "had an abortion, search for female." No warrant. No case number. One search box and the license plate movements of most of America.

That same year, Flock Safety reported its cameras had contributed to solving 700,000 crimes and helped Tulsa achieve a 100% homicide clearance rate for the first time in 50 years.

Both claims are true. The math connecting them is what nobody has published until now.

The Ratio Nobody Calculated

Flock's own figures: 20 billion vehicle scans per month across 83,000+ cameras in 49 states. That is 240 billion scans per year. Divide by the 700,000 crimes Flock claims to have helped solve, and you get the system's actual signal-to-noise ratio:

343,000 scans of innocent vehicles for every crime solved.

Put differently: the U.S. has roughly 284 million registered vehicles. At 20 billion scans per month, the average car in America is photographed by a Flock camera approximately 70 times per month, or about 2.3 times per day. Your car. Your daily commute. Your visit to a doctor's office, a protest, a gun shop, a divorce lawyer. All logged. All searchable.

Flock's $7.5 billion valuation is built on this asymmetry. The crime-solving headlines are real. The 99.9997% of scans that photograph people who have committed no crime are also real. Both live in the same database.

What the Cameras Deliver

The results are not hypothetical. San Francisco deployed Flock alongside other policing reforms starting in 2023. By 2025, major crimes had fallen 44%. Car thefts dropped 54%. In Lakewood, Washington, a city averaging a murder per month, Flock helped deliver its first murder-free year in two decades. The FBI's 2019 data showed 83% of property crimes went unsolved. Flock neighborhoods report dramatically better numbers.

Full disclosure: Andreessen Horowitz, the venture firm that promoted several of these statistics, is a Flock investor. The data appears legitimate. The incentive to present it favorably is also legitimate.

What the Cameras Enable

The Electronic Frontier Foundation's year-long investigation, based on 12 million Flock search logs from 3,900+ law enforcement agencies, documented systematic abuse:

  • Protest surveillance. Nineteen agencies searched the network to track participants in 2025 political demonstrations. Delaware State Police queried nine times in one month targeting animal-rights activists.
  • Racial profiling. More than 80 agencies used slurs against Romani people in Flock searches. Grand Prairie, TX police used a racial slur six times while searching for vehicles traveling together.
  • Reproductive tracking. The Johnson County deputy searches were logged, reviewed by prosecutors, and used to investigate a woman's private medical decision.

The ACLU noted these are not bugs in the system. They are the system working as designed: one query, 83,000 cameras, no warrant required. In February 2026, Amazon cancelled its planned Ring-Flock partnership after a Super Bowl ad depicting the integration triggered public backlash.

The Legal Ground Is Shifting

In June 2024, a Norfolk, Virginia circuit court ruled that warrantless ALPR data collection constitutes a search under the Fourth Amendment, likening Flock's plate databases to tracking devices previously found unconstitutional in United States v. Jones. The ruling applies only in that jurisdiction, but it signals where the law is heading. Multiple states are now considering ALPR-specific legislation. The ACLU of Massachusetts launched a "Get the Flock Out" campaign providing communities with legal toolkits to resist or regulate deployment.

The Third Option: Cameras Without the Network

Your neighborhood can get camera coverage without feeding a national surveillance database. The setup is straightforward: Reolink RLC-810A cameras ($110 each) at entry points, running Frigate NVR (open-source) on a Raspberry Pi 5 with a Hailo-8L AI accelerator for real-time person and vehicle detection. A 4-bay NAS stores 30 days of footage locally. Total hardware for a four-entrance subdivision: $700 to $1,100. No subscription. No cloud. No national network.

For license plate recognition, OpenALPR runs on commodity hardware and stores results in a local database. Add an IR illuminator ($30) for night capture. Maintain a hot list from your police department's published stolen-vehicle reports. Five-year cost for a four-entrance neighborhood with full ALPR: under $2,000. The same coverage from Flock runs approximately $50,000.

The trade-off is real. You lose the network effect that solves cross-jurisdictional crime. You lose proactive AMBER Alert matching across 83,000 cameras. You lose Flock's "Vehicle Fingerprint" that identifies cars even without plates. Police get footage only when your community decides to share it, per incident.

What We Did Not Prove

Our scans-per-crime ratio uses Flock's self-reported figures for both numerator (20 billion scans/month) and denominator (700,000 crimes). Flock defines "helped solve" loosely, and neither number has been independently audited. If the true crime-solving count is higher, our ratio improves. If "helped solve" includes cases where a Flock scan was pulled but didn't materially contribute, it worsens considerably.

The SF crime reduction coincided with multiple policing changes beyond Flock. No controlled study isolates the camera effect. The DIY cost comparison assumes a technically capable volunteer handles setup. Many neighborhoods lack that person, and Flock's professional installation has real value the numbers understate.

The Strongest Counterargument

Network scale solves the hardest crimes. A burglary ring spanning three counties gets caught because cameras in all three are connected. A missing child is found because AMBER Alerts trigger real-time matching across the full network simultaneously. Local-only cameras cannot do this. The 700,000 crimes Flock claims were disproportionately solved through cross-jurisdictional matching that isolated cameras would miss. The privacy cost is real, but so are the bodies that don't turn up because the network existed.

What You Can Do

If your neighborhood is considering Flock: Demand geographic query limits (local PD only), mandatory case numbers for every search, quarterly audit reports to the HOA, and 30-day hard retention. If Flock won't negotiate these four terms, that tells you what you need to know.

If you want cameras without the network: Start with two Reolink cameras, a Raspberry Pi 5 running Frigate, and a 2TB drive. Under $400. Run it for 60 days before deciding whether you need more.

If you want to push for reform: The Norfolk ruling gives communities legal precedent to challenge warrantless ALPR deployment. The ACLU's toolkit includes template ordinances, FOIA request letters, and model legislation requiring warrants for ALPR searches.
