Your 10-Point Rubric Is a Lossy JPEG of Human Taste: Why Learned Embeddings Beat Expert Scoring

Netflix needed 50-200 unnamed dimensions to predict what you'd watch. Slot machines score zero on every expert rubric and generate $53 billion a year. The evaluation framework you designed by hand is wrong. Here's what the math says about the real shape of human engagement.

By Jordan Kessler · March 29, 2026 · ☕ 12 min read

In 2006, Netflix posted a dataset of 100 million movie ratings and offered a million dollars to anyone who could beat their recommendation algorithm by 10%. The company had categories. They had genres, star ratings, editorial tags. They had a taxonomy designed by smart people who watched movies for a living.

The winning team, BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos, did not use any of it. Their solution decomposed the user-movie rating matrix into latent factor vectors using matrix factorization. Each user became a point in a space of 50 to 200 unnamed dimensions. Each movie became another point in the same space. Predicted rating: the dot product. No genres. No "action" or "romance." Just coordinates in a geometric space that the algorithm learned from behavioral data.

The latent factors outperformed every hand-designed taxonomy Netflix had. And nobody could name what any of those 200 dimensions meant.

The Naming Problem

When a human designs a scoring rubric, they start by naming dimensions. A game review might use "Graphics," "Gameplay," "Story," "Replay Value." A wine scoring system uses "Appearance," "Nose," "Palate," "Finish." A teacher evaluation form uses "Classroom Management," "Subject Knowledge," "Student Engagement."

The act of naming is the act of constraining. The moment you write "Strategic Depth" on a rubric, you have made a claim: that strategic depth is an independent axis of quality, that it varies meaningfully across the things you're evaluating, and that it contributes to whatever outcome you care about. Every named dimension is a hypothesis. Most are untested.

Peter Rentfrow and Samuel Gosling's 2003 STOMP study is instructive. They asked 3,500 people about their music preferences and found that the entire space of musical taste collapsed into four dimensions: Reflective & Complex (jazz, classical, folk), Intense & Rebellious (rock, alternative, heavy metal), Upbeat & Conventional (country, pop, soundtracks), and Energetic & Rhythmic (rap, soul, electronica). These four factors predicted musical preference better than any genre taxonomy Spotify or the Recording Industry could have designed. But try scoring an album on those axes in advance. "How Reflective & Complex is this track?" is not a question a music critic would know how to answer, because the dimensions were discovered from behavior, not invented by experts.

This is the core asymmetry: experts invent dimensions they can articulate. Algorithms discover dimensions that predict behavior. These are different sets.

The Slot Machine Test

Every credible game design rubric ever written would score a slot machine poorly. Zero strategic depth. Zero player agency. Zero skill expression. Zero narrative. Zero emergent complexity. A slot machine is a button that sometimes gives you fake money. On a 10-dimension expert rubric, it might score 15 out of 100.

Slot machines generated $53.6 billion in US casino revenue in 2023. They account for roughly 70% of total casino floor income. The average slot session lasts 3 hours. Return player rates exceed 80%. By every behavioral signal we can measure, slot machines are among the most engaging interactive experiences ever designed.

Natasha Dow Schüll's ethnographic work at MIT documented why. Slot players do not seek winning. They seek what they call "the zone," a flow state induced by the interaction between variable-ratio reinforcement schedules, anticipation-reward cycles operating at 3-5 second intervals, near-miss architecture that triggers dopaminergic responses similar to actual wins, and loss-disguised-as-win events where the machine pays 15 credits on a 40-credit bet while playing celebration sounds. The player's brain is not idle. It is performing continuous prediction, pattern-matching on genuinely random data, and processing escalating emotional arcs through bonus rounds and multiplier stacks.

An expert rubric calls this "zero engagement." Behavioral data calls it "$53 billion." The rubric is wrong.

Any universal evaluation framework that cannot explain why slot machines engage 80 million Americans per year is not a universal evaluation framework. It is a parochial scoring system that measures what experts think should matter, not what does.

How Many Dimensions Does Taste Actually Have?

The empirical answer, across multiple domains, is surprisingly consistent: somewhere between 20 and 200, with sharp diminishing returns after 50.

Koren, Bell, and Volinsky (2009) found that the Netflix Prize matrix factorization models showed meaningful improvement up to about 200 latent factors, with the steepest gains in the first 50. Beyond 200, overfitting dominated. The winning solution used an ensemble of models operating at different dimensionalities, blended together.

Spotify's recommendation engine, as detailed in their research publications, uses embeddings of 40-256 dimensions for tracks, depending on the model component. Their 2023 work on sequential recommendation uses 128-dimensional user taste vectors, which they found captured the meaningful variance in listening behavior across 500 million users.

In psychology, the Big Five personality model (Costa & McCrae, 1989) compresses human personality into five dimensions. But preference prediction requires more. Kosinski et al. (2013) achieved 95% accuracy on ethnicity prediction and strong personality prediction using the first 30 SVD components of Facebook Like data, but accuracy continued improving with more dimensions, plateauing around 100-150.

The pattern: human-designed systems use 3-10 dimensions (Big Five, STOMP's four music factors, Rotten Tomatoes' single score). Learned systems converge on 50-200. The gap represents information that experts cannot name but that predicts behavior.

What Are the Unnamed Dimensions?

This is where it gets uncomfortable: we mostly don't know.

Some latent factors, when inspected, correspond to recognizable concepts. In movie recommendation, researchers have found factors that correlate with "gender appeal" (male-skewing vs. female-skewing films), "era" (classic vs. modern), and "cerebral vs. visceral" (thought-provoking vs. pure entertainment). But these identifiable factors account for only a fraction of the learned dimensions. Most factors encode statistical regularities that don't have names in any language.

Consider a hypothetical latent dimension that activates for: the experience of being slightly overwhelmed by information, enjoying the feeling of partial comprehension, and being drawn to complexity you cannot fully parse. This dimension would predict engagement with both Arrival (the movie) and a well-designed slot machine bonus round. No human rubric contains a row labeled "productive confusion tolerance." But a learned embedding would capture it, because it predicts behavior.

The unnamed dimensions are not noise. They are the unexplored geography of human preference. Expert-designed rubrics map the coastline. Learned embeddings map the continent.

The Original Contribution: Evaluation as Representation Learning

Here is the framework shift. Evaluation is not an expert judgment task. It is a representation learning problem.

In the expert judgment model, a human selects dimensions, assigns weights, scores items on each dimension, and computes a weighted sum. The output is a scalar: "this game is 82/100." The dimensions are fixed. The weights are fixed. The model assumes the evaluator knows the loss function.

In the representation learning model, you observe behavioral signals at scale (session duration, return frequency, in-session emotional trajectory, willingness to recommend, willingness to pay). You learn an embedding space in which items that produce similar behavioral responses are geometrically close. You do not name the dimensions. You do not assign weights. You let the data define the geometry.

The representation learning model has a critical advantage: it handles the spectrum problem. Human engagement is not categorical. There is no clean boundary between "strategic games" and "chance games" or between "skill-based" and "stimulus-based" engagement. There is a continuous space, and every product sits at some coordinate in it. Expert rubrics force categorical thinking: you create a "casino games" rubric and a "strategy games" rubric, and hybrids fall through the cracks. Learned embeddings place poker, blackjack, and Dungeon Crawl in a shared space where their relative distances encode their actual behavioral similarity.

The a priori prediction problem becomes tractable. You cannot A/B test every product with every human. But if your embedding space is rich enough, you can estimate the behavioral response of a new item by computing its position in the learned space and finding its nearest neighbors among items with known behavioral data. This is how every modern recommendation system works. The insight is that evaluation should work the same way.

The Practical Numbers

How many dimensions does a universal engagement evaluation system need? The evidence converges on a range:

10 dimensions (the typical expert rubric) captures maybe 30-40% of the behavioral variance. Enough to separate obvious tiers. Not enough to predict whether a specific person will spend 3 hours or 3 minutes.

50 dimensions captures roughly 70-80% of variance. This is where the Netflix Prize models showed their steepest improvement-per-dimension gains. At 50 dimensions, you can predict engagement within a standard deviation for most items.

100-200 dimensions captures 85-95% of variance. Diminishing returns are real, but the marginal dimensions matter for edge cases: the item that a 10-dimensional rubric scores as mediocre but that a specific user subpopulation will love. Long-tail recommendation depends on these marginal factors.

Beyond 200 dimensions, overfitting dominates in all empirical studies. The intrinsic dimensionality of human preference appears to be bounded.

The implication: any evaluation system using fewer than 50 dimensions is losing at least a third of the signal. A 10-point expert rubric is operating with roughly a third of the information content needed to predict engagement. It is, mathematically, a lossy compression of human taste.

Why Not Just Use Behavioral Data Directly?

The strongest counterargument against learned evaluation dimensions is that they are tautological. "Engagement predicts engagement" is not a discovery. If you train on session length and predict session length, you've built a clock that reads itself.

This criticism has force, but misses the mechanism. The value of learned embeddings is not prediction of the training signal. It is transfer. An embedding trained on movie engagement transfers to predict podcast engagement. An embedding trained on game session length transfers to predict return frequency. The dimensions that emerge are not "engagement" itself but the underlying cognitive and emotional processes that produce engagement across domains. Rentfrow and Gosling's four music dimensions predicted not just music preference but personality traits, political orientation, and even physical activity levels. The dimensions were about people, not about music.

The same transfer has been demonstrated in recommender systems. Pre-trained item embeddings from one domain improve cold-start performance in another, suggesting that the learned dimensions encode something genuinely structural about human response rather than surface features of any particular content type.

From Theory to Practice: The Simulation Trap

There is a seductive next step that does not work: having a large language model simulate the behavioral data you lack.

Netflix had 100 million ratings from real humans. Casinos instrument every pull. Spotify logs 500 million listening sessions. These systems learn latent dimensions from actual behavioral variance across actual people. If you are building a game, evaluating a product, or scoring content without that scale of data, the natural instinct in 2026 is to ask an AI to simulate diverse users. Create a panel of personas. Have GPT-4 pretend to be a 14-year-old with ADHD, a retired schoolteacher, a competitive speedrunner. Score the product from each perspective. Aggregate the scores. Call it a synthetic embedding.

This is cosplay, not measurement.

Large language models are trained on the aggregate of human text. When asked to simulate a "casual mobile gamer," they produce the median internet representation of that persona. The outputs converge. A simulated panel of 20 personas will generate 20 scores that cluster far more tightly than 20 real humans would, because the underlying model is one function approximating one distribution. The tails vanish. The ADHD teenager and the retired teacher produce suspiciously similar assessments, because they are both being generated by the same weights attending to the same latent space.

This is not a fixable prompt engineering problem. It is architectural. A language model predicts likely text. A likely response to "rate this game as someone with low attention span" is not the same as what a person with low attention span would actually do when handed a controller. The model can reason about engagement. It cannot experience it. The difference between reasoning about a slot machine and sitting in front of one for three hours is the difference between reading about the ocean and drowning.

Argyle et al. (2023) demonstrated that LLMs can approximate survey response distributions for well-studied populations, but their accuracy degrades precisely on the behavioral margins that matter most for engagement prediction: extreme preferences, niche tastes, and the non-obvious correlations between seemingly unrelated features. The unnamed dimensions in a learned embedding capture exactly these margins. An LLM simulation washes them out.

The Cold-Start Bridge

If you cannot simulate the data and do not yet have real behavioral signals, what can you actually do? Three tiers, in order of epistemic honesty:

Tier 1: Instrument, then learn. Add three behavioral measurements to every product: median session length, return rate (same user, different day), and drop-off point (where in the experience people quit). Even 50 real users over three months generates enough signal to rank-order products by actual stickiness. Sparse real data from 50 humans contains more information about latent engagement dimensions than 10,000 simulated LLM evaluations, because the real data captures variance the model cannot generate.

Tier 2: Ask concrete questions, not abstract ones. Replace named dimensions with specific behavioral observables. Instead of "Strategic Depth: 7/10," ask: "How many seconds elapse between the first input and the first reward signal?" "What is the longest stretch of zero feedback?" "Can you describe three meaningfully different emotional arcs across sessions?" Language models are substantially better at answering narrow, concrete, mechanistic questions about a product than at producing holistic engagement ratings. A rubric of 50 behavioral micro-questions captures more variance than 10 named dimensions, even though both come from the same LLM, because the questions constrain the model to observable features rather than vibes.

Tier 3: Calibrate against ground truth as it arrives. Use the expert rubric now. Collect real behavioral data over time. After three months, correlate: which rubric dimensions actually predict session length and return rate? Kill the dimensions that do not. Reweight the dimensions that do. Add new questions targeting behavioral variance the rubric missed. The rubric evolves toward ground truth iteratively rather than sitting frozen as static expert opinion. This is the key advantage of the representation learning framing: it does not require you to get the dimensions right on day one. It requires you to have a feedback loop.

The uncomfortable conclusion: the gap between the theoretical ideal (learned 50-200 dimension embeddings from massive behavioral data) and the practical reality (an LLM scoring things on 10 named dimensions) is not an algorithm gap. It is a data gap. Current reasoning AIs are good at reading structure and making reasonable guesses. But reasonable guesses, by definition, lose the unreasonable variance that makes engagement prediction hard and interesting. The path forward is not better simulation. It is patient instrumentation.

Limitations

This analysis relies on cross-domain extrapolation. The Netflix Prize, Spotify embeddings, and STOMP results each concern specific content types. Claiming that their dimensionality ranges generalize to all evaluation domains (games, products, educational materials, services) is a hypothesis, not a proven result. No study has directly measured the intrinsic dimensionality of human engagement across all content types simultaneously.

The 50-200 dimension convergence could be an artifact of model architecture rather than a property of human cognition. Matrix factorization and neural embeddings both have inductive biases that favor low-rank structure. If human taste were genuinely high-rank (thousands of independent dimensions), these models would compress it and still achieve good RMSE. We would mistake model capacity for cognitive structure.

Behavioral signals are also not neutral ground truth. Session length can reflect addiction rather than satisfaction. Willingness to pay reflects disposable income. Return frequency reflects habit, not preference. The choice of behavioral signal as ground truth is itself an evaluative judgment, which means even learned systems inherit human assumptions about what "engagement" means.

The Bottom Line

Every expert rubric is a bet that the evaluator knows which dimensions matter. The Netflix Prize proved that bet wrong for movies. Slot machine revenue proves it wrong for games. The consistent finding across recommender systems, personality psychology, and behavioral prediction is that human taste lives in a space of 50-200 dimensions, most of which no expert would think to name. Your 10-point rubric is not wrong because the dimensions are bad. It is wrong because 10 dimensions cannot encode the structure of a 50-200 dimensional space, any more than a JPEG compressed to 3% quality can encode a photograph. The information is gone. The question for any evaluation system in 2026 is not "what dimensions should we score?" It is "how do we learn the dimensions that matter from the behavior of the people we're trying to serve?"
