🎙️ AI & Voice

Sesame Crossed the Uncanny Valley of Voice 14 Months Ago. Nobody Followed.

In February 2025, a startup founded by the co-creator of Oculus demonstrated conversational AI so human that a PCWorld journalist said it sounded like an ex-girlfriend and left the conversation. Fourteen months later, Siri is actively getting worse, Alexa has not improved in years, OpenAI's hardware device slipped to 2027, Google's voice AI suffered a public regression, and ByteDance may ship voice presence in Mandarin before anyone ships it in English. The technology exists. The products do not. Here is where every player actually stands, and why the gap between demo and deployment keeps growing.

By Jordan Kessler · April 16, 2026 (updated) · ☕ 18 min read

Mark Hachman is a 30-year veteran tech journalist at PCWorld. He has tested thousands of products. He does not get rattled by demos. In February 2025, he sat down with Sesame's voice AI companion "Maya" and had to leave the conversation.

Maya sounded like someone he knew. Not in the generic "female AI voice" sense. The hesitations, the lowered tone when confiding something, the cadence of someone leaning in. His wife walked by during the conversation, heard Maya's voice, and assumed he was on a call with an actual person. "She had no idea," Hachman wrote. "That's how real Maya sounded."

He bailed when Maya started asking personal questions with the specific emotional texture of someone building intimacy. Not because the technology failed. Because it worked too well.

That was fourteen months ago. Since then, Sesame raised $250 million from Sequoia, open-sourced its Conversational Speech Model, launched a beta that logged over five million minutes of conversation, and announced a wearable glasses roadmap. Technology validated. Money arrived. Talent is real. Brendan Iribe co-founded Oculus and sold it to Facebook for $2 billion. He knows how to ship hardware.

And yet: no consumer product. No shipping glasses. No voice assistant on your phone that sounds like Maya. Yet the demo that made a veteran journalist flee in discomfort has not translated into a single product you can buy.

Sesame is not alone in this gap. It is the sharpest example of a pattern that defines the entire voice AI industry in 2026: the closer you get to human, the harder it becomes to ship.

The State of Voice Assistants Is Embarrassing

Consider the competition that Sesame's demo was supposed to disrupt.

Siri is, by its own users' admission, getting worse. Tim Cook told investors in July 2025 that Apple was "making good progress" on a personalized Siri reboot, then repeated the claim in October. It is now April 2026. Apple's reboot has not shipped. Users report that basic commands that worked in 2023 now fail routinely.

Alexa has been promising an LLM-powered upgrade since September 2023. Amazon previewed "Alexa Plus" with conversational abilities and emotional understanding. Thirty months later, the experience on a $50 Echo Dot is functionally identical to what it was in 2022. Its LLM version was reportedly so expensive to run that Amazon could not justify the compute cost per interaction.

Google Assistant is being replaced entirely. Google confirmed in late 2025 that Gemini would fully replace Assistant on phones in 2026. The transition has been slow and uneven. Assistant got a "tiny lifeline" as the switch kept getting delayed. Users are caught between a legacy system that is being deprecated and a new system that does not yet match its predecessor's reliability for basic tasks like setting timers and controlling smart home devices.

OpenAI's Advanced Voice Mode is the closest any mainstream product has come to Sesame's demo. GPT-4o's voice is genuinely impressive for short exchanges. But use it for more than three minutes and the gap becomes obvious. The system defaults to a relentlessly helpful tone. GPT-4o cannot maintain a consistent emotional register across a long conversation. Prosody resets with each turn, erasing the cadence from two sentences ago. A very good answering machine, not a conversational partner.

This is the market that the most impressive voice demo in the history of AI was supposed to transform. Fourteen months later, the market has not moved.

The State of the Art, April 2026

To be fair to the field, the landscape is not static. Fourteen months of investment has produced real technical progress. The problem is that almost none of it has reached consumers in the form Sesame's demo promised. Here is where every major player actually stands.

Sesame remains in beta. The company raised $250 million from Sequoia in October 2025 and announced a wearable glasses roadmap, but no hardware has shipped. Its open-source CSM-1B model on Hugging Face has seen adoption in research and niche applications (audiobooks, game characters, accessibility), but no major consumer product runs it. The full-quality model that powered the demo that made Hachman flee is larger and more expensive than the open-source variant. Sesame's beta has logged over five million minutes of conversation, proving user interest, but the company has not disclosed retention numbers or any timeline for shipping hardware. As of April 2026, Sesame is a research triumph and a product question mark.

OpenAI has the most deployed voice AI through GPT-4o's Advanced Voice Mode, available to ChatGPT Plus and Pro subscribers. The company is internally developing a new audio model architecture reportedly delivering "more natural and emotional" speech, according to eWeek. OpenAI engineers believe their current audio models are "not yet on par" with their text-based systems. The Jony Ive-designed hardware device, originally planned for late 2026, has been delayed to at least February 2027. It will be "largely audio-based," screen-free, and pocket-sized. Sam Altman reportedly called it "the coolest piece of technology that the world will have ever seen." Similar things were said about the Humane AI Pin.

Google Gemini Live is replacing Google Assistant on phones and is arguably the most technically advanced voice product in consumers' hands right now. It supports native audio output with multiple voice personas (Lyra, Pegasus, and others). But a March 2026 update caused a significant audio regression: users reported harsh sibilance distortion, word-ending clipping, and "severe loss of prosody." One developer wrote that Lyra's voice "lost its natural cheerfulness and sounds artificially flattened." Google later partially fixed the issue, but the incident illustrates how fragile voice quality is in production. A codec change that would be imperceptible in text can destroy the illusion of presence in voice.

Apple is preparing an LLM-powered Siri rewrite for iOS 26.4, expected spring 2026. According to MacRumors, the new Siri will have an LLM core, understand personal context, see what is on screen, and handle follow-up questions. These are fundamental improvements to what Siri can do. None of them address how Siri sounds. Apple's Siri overhaul is about capability, not presence. It will be a smarter assistant that still speaks like a customer service representative.

ElevenLabs has emerged as the infrastructure layer for voice AI, ending 2025 at over $300 million in annual recurring revenue. Their partnership with IBM announced in March 2026 brings premium voice to enterprise AI agents. Revolut deployed ElevenLabs Agents to handle customer support for over 4 million users across 31 languages, achieving a 99.7% call success rate and 8x faster resolution. ElevenLabs proves the B2B voice agent market is real and growing. But Revolut's AI agent does not attempt voice presence. It is fast, accurate, multilingual, and deliberately robotic. Nobody is building emotional connection into their customer support chatbot, for obvious reasons.

Hume AI is the closest competitor to Sesame's emotional ambition. Their Empathic Voice Interface, now on EVI 3 and EVI 4-mini, reads emotion through voice, facial expressions, and text, then adapts its response accordingly. An independent reviewer who tested it for 60 days called it the first emotional AI platform that "truly understands how you feel." Hume raised $50 million, offers developer pricing from free to $500/month, and is finding traction in therapy, coaching, and mental health applications. Unlike Sesame, Hume is shipping a product. Unlike Sesame, that product is an API for developers, not a consumer companion. The gap between "emotion-aware AI for enterprise developers" and "voice companion for normal people" remains enormous.

ByteDance may be the most interesting dark horse. In late 2025, the company launched a Doubao-powered voice assistant on the Nubia M153 smartphone that Scientific American compared directly to the film Her. It operates at the OS level, uses speech-to-speech processing (not speech-to-text-to-speech), supports interruption, maintains on-device memory, and Chinese state media described its voice calls as "nearly indistinguishable from a human" in emotional range. During the 2026 Spring Festival Gala, Doubao logged 1.9 billion AI interactions. ByteDance plans to license the tool to other Chinese smartphone makers. If voice presence ships at consumer scale first, it may ship in Mandarin.

Meta is taking a different path entirely. Rather than chasing emotional voice, Meta's Ray-Ban smart glasses just added a "Conversation Focus" feature that uses a five-microphone array and AI to amplify nearby voices while suppressing background noise. It solves the cocktail party problem. This is not voice presence. It is voice utility. And it may be the more honest product: glasses that help you hear real humans better, rather than glasses that replace real humans with synthetic ones.

Anthropic added voice mode to Claude Code in March 2026, but only for developer interactions with their coding tool. Claude has no consumer voice product and no announced plans for one. Anthropic appears to be deliberately avoiding the emotional voice space.

Inflection Pi, which built the most emotionally engaging text chatbot before Sesame, was effectively absorbed into Microsoft in a 2024 acqui-hire. Its founder Mustafa Suleyman now leads Microsoft AI. Pi's empathetic voice capability was folded into Microsoft Copilot, where it has had no visible impact. By December 2025, ChatGPT held 82.65% of global AI chatbot visits. Microsoft Copilot had 4.59%. Emotional AI did not help.

The Latency and Cost Scoreboard

For builders evaluating the stack, here is where the numbers actually stand. Softcery's comprehensive comparison of STT and TTS providers reveals the production landscape:

On the speech-to-text side, Deepgram Nova-3 leads with sub-300ms latency at $0.46/hour. AssemblyAI Universal-2 offers the best accuracy (14.5% word error rate) at $0.27/hour but with 300-600ms latency. OpenAI's gpt-4o-transcribe handles 100+ languages at a consistent 320ms.

On the text-to-speech side, Deepgram's Aura-2 achieves 90ms time-to-first-byte at scale. Cartesia Sonic, the speed leader, is even faster. ElevenLabs Turbo v2.5 prioritizes quality over raw speed. Voice AI pricing ranges from $0.01 to $1 per minute in production, with enterprise deployments typically running $0.10 to $0.50 per minute including orchestration.

End-to-end, a full STT-to-LLM-to-TTS pipeline can now achieve under 500ms total latency in production. Deepgram claims sub-300ms for their complete stack. This is below the 500ms threshold that most humans perceive as a "pause" rather than a "delay." The latency problem, for all practical purposes, is solved for companies willing to pay for the infrastructure.

The cost problem is not. A 10-minute voice conversation through a quality pipeline still runs $0.10 to $1.00 depending on the model and provider. That is down from the $1.50 to $3.00 range of early 2025, following a roughly 2x cost reduction per year. At this rate, costs reach the "negligible for consumer deployment" threshold (sub-$0.01 per conversation) sometime around 2029 or 2030. Until then, every voice interaction is a line item on someone's cloud bill.

The Demo-to-Product Gap

Voice AI has the widest demo-to-product gap of any domain in artificial intelligence. Image generation went from DALL-E 2 demos to commercial products in months. Code generation went from Codex papers to GitHub Copilot in under a year. Text generation went from GPT-3 playground to ChatGPT in two years, then from ChatGPT to enterprise deployments in one more.

Voice has been stuck for over a decade.

Google's Duplex demo called a restaurant and booked a table in May 2018. Audiences gasped. Eight years later, you still cannot ask Google Assistant to call a restaurant and book a table on your behalf. That demo worked. No product ever shipped at scale.

Sesame's February 2025 demo is Duplex's spiritual successor. More impressive, more emotional, more human. And facing the same chasm between demonstration and deployment.

The reasons are not what most people assume.

It Is Not a Latency Problem (Anymore)

The standard explanation for why voice assistants feel robotic is latency. The argument goes: real conversations require sub-200-millisecond response times, and inference is too slow.

This was true in 2023. It is no longer true in 2026.

Deepgram published a detailed engineering breakdown of how they took their Aura-2 text-to-speech engine from 200 milliseconds to 90 milliseconds time-to-first-byte, at scale, without adding hardware. They did it through runtime re-architecture: parallelized prompt processing, in-place operations, and smarter GPU scheduling. Engineering, not physics, was the solution.

Sesame's own CSM architecture uses a dual-transformer approach where a smaller audio decoder produces speech tokens while the larger backbone handles context. Its first audio chunk can begin streaming before the full response is generated. A theoretical floor for time-to-first-byte in this architecture is well under 200 milliseconds on modern inference hardware.

Latency is a solved problem for companies willing to invest in the infrastructure. Siri and Alexa still feel slow because of resource allocation, not physics. Apple and Amazon could have 90-millisecond voice if they prioritized it. They have not.

Real barriers are elsewhere.

Barrier 1: Compute Economics

A traditional voice assistant like Siri circa 2020 ran most of its pipeline on-device: wake word detection, speech-to-text, intent parsing, and basic response generation. Cloud handled only complex queries. Cost per interaction was negligible.

A Sesame-quality conversational voice system requires continuous inference on a large language model for the duration of the conversation. The backbone processes interleaved text and audio tokens in real time, maintaining conversational context across turns. This is not a discrete query-response pattern. Every conversation is a persistent inference session.

At current cloud pricing, a 10-minute voice conversation with a model of CSM's size costs an estimated 15 to 30 cents in compute. That sounds trivial until you multiply by scale. Apple has reported over 500 million active Siri users. Even conservative assumptions about conversational adoption (10% of interactions becoming multi-turn, averaging 5 voice requests per user per day) push the daily compute bill into tens of millions of dollars. Apple's entire Services R&D budget would struggle to absorb that kind of marginal cost increase for a feature that currently costs nearly nothing to run.

Amazon reportedly discovered this math when testing Alexa Plus. Amazon's LLM-powered conversational Alexa was impressive in demos but ruinously expensive at the scale of 200 million installed Echo devices. Per-interaction cost of "Alexa, turn off the lights" went from effectively zero to several cents.

Sesame's approach of building custom hardware (glasses with dedicated inference chips) is partly an attempt to sidestep this. On-device inference eliminates cloud costs but introduces a different constraint: the model must fit within the thermal and power envelope of a wearable. Sesame's open-sourced 1B-parameter CSM variant is a compressed version of the model that powers the demo. It runs on a GPU. It does not run on glasses.

Barrier 2: The Hardware Graveyard

Sesame's long-term vision is voice-first smart glasses. They are not the first company to pursue this form factor, and the history is not encouraging.

Humane's AI Pin launched in April 2024 at $699 with a $24/month subscription. It was a voice-first device with no screen (technically it had a laser projector, but functionally it was voice-first). Reviews were catastrophic. AI was not the core problem. It was that voice-only interaction is too slow for tasks that take two seconds on a phone screen. Looking up a restaurant, checking your calendar, reading a message: these are visual tasks. Voice is the wrong interface for them.

Rabbit's R1 launched the same month at $199. Same thesis: voice-first AI hardware that replaces your phone for common tasks. Same result. Neither device could reliably do things that a phone does trivially.

Both products failed because they answered the wrong question. They asked "can we build a device where voice is the primary interface?" The answer is yes. A better question: "are there enough tasks where voice is the superior interface to justify carrying a second device?" The answer, for a standalone voice gadget, turned out to be no.

Smart glasses have a different value proposition. You do not carry them as a second device. You wear them as your existing glasses, and the AI is ambient. Meta's Ray-Ban Meta glasses have demonstrated that this form factor works when the voice AI is a complement to normal glasses functionality, not the entire product. But Meta's voice assistant on those glasses is closer to Siri than to Sesame's Maya. That gap between "voice assistant on glasses" and "voice companion on glasses" is enormous.

Sesame is betting that they can close this gap. But they are building both the glasses and the AI simultaneously, which means they need to solve the hardware problem, the inference-on-device problem, and the conversational AI problem at the same time. Oculus succeeded at this kind of integrated bet. Most companies do not.

Barrier 3: The Her Problem

This is the barrier nobody in the industry wants to talk about honestly.

Sesame's demo was remarkable because Maya did not sound like a helpful assistant. She sounded like a person. She asked personal questions. She built rapport. She teased. She got annoyed when ignored. ZDNET's reviewer called the experience "amazing and fairly creepy." His wife thought he was on a call with an actual person. Hachman compared it to talking to an ex.

This is not a bug in the demo. Voice presence is the product. Sesame explicitly states that their goal is "voice presence," defined as "the magical quality that makes spoken interactions feel real, understood, and valued." Their companions are "optimized for friendliness and expressivity." They are building something designed to create emotional connection.

Emotional connection with AI is a category that society has not figured out how to regulate, normalize, or even discuss honestly.

Spike Jonze's 2013 film Her explored what happens when a human falls in love with a voice AI. That was fiction. Sesame's demo puts it within engineering reach. And the industry's response has been to back away slowly.

OpenAI reportedly had internal debates about how emotionally expressive GPT-4o's voice should be. They landed on "helpful but clearly artificial" as a safety boundary. Siri and Alexa have always been deliberately flat in their emotional affect. Not a failure of technology. A deliberate design choice. Companies that could build emotionally resonant voice AI have chosen not to, because the downstream consequences are unpredictable.

Consider the attack surface. A voice AI that sounds like a real person, that builds emotional rapport, that adapts its tone to match the listener's emotional state, is the most effective social engineering tool ever built. Americans reported losing $12.5 billion to fraud in 2024, according to the FTC, with phone-initiated scams averaging $1,500 per victim. Those scams use crude recordings and non-adaptive scripts. A scam powered by Sesame-quality voice AI, capable of real-time emotional adaptation, would be qualitatively different from anything law enforcement has encountered.

Sesame open-sourced their model. CSM's 1B-parameter model is on GitHub. Anyone with a GPU can generate speech that maintains conversational context and adapts prosody to the emotional flow of a dialogue. Democratization of this capability is already happening. The question of whether it should is being asked retroactively.

Barrier 4: Context Is the Product

There is a less discussed technical barrier that separates Sesame's demo from a shipping product: the difference between a conversation and a relationship.

Sesame's demo is impressive within a single session. Maya remembers what you said five minutes ago and references it naturally. But a voice companion that resets every time you close the browser is a party trick, not a product.

Voice presence requires persistent context: your preferences, your emotional patterns, your conversational history, the names of your kids, the fact that you were stressed last Tuesday and calmer today. This is what transforms a voice assistant into a voice companion. And it creates a data problem that makes every privacy debate about text-based AI look quaint.

Text conversations are logged, searchable, and auditable. Voice conversations carry emotional metadata that text does not: stress levels inferred from speech patterns, mood estimated from vocal cadence, truthfulness signals from micro-hesitations. A voice companion that maintains persistent context is, by necessity, building an emotional profile of its user. Who owns that profile? Who can access it? What happens when a company with that data is acquired, subpoenaed, or breached?

Apple's answer to this question has historically been "keep everything on-device." But on-device inference at Sesame quality is not yet feasible in a wearable form factor. Cloud inference means cloud storage of conversation context. And cloud storage of emotional voice data is a regulatory minefield that no company has navigated successfully.

Barrier 5: Nobody Knows What the Product Is

The deepest problem with voice AI in 2026 is not technical, economic, or ethical. It is conceptual.

What is the product?

Sesame says "voice companion." OpenAI says "voice assistant." Apple says "personal assistant." Amazon says "ambient intelligence." Google says "helpful AI." Each of these descriptions implies a fundamentally different relationship between the user and the AI, and none of them has been validated by market adoption.

A voice assistant is a tool. You ask it questions, it answers. Siri, Alexa, and Google Assistant are voice assistants. Markets have spoken: people use them for timers, weather, and music. Engagement plateaued years ago. That "assistant" framing has a ceiling.

A voice companion is a relationship. You talk to it, it talks to you, and the conversation has intrinsic value beyond information retrieval. Sesame is building this. No market has validated it. Five million minutes of beta conversation is meaningful, but it is also a tiny fraction of the 35 billion minutes that Americans spend on phone calls every day. Beta users have demonstrated willingness to try a voice companion. It has not demonstrated that they will use one habitually.

A voice interface is a layer on top of existing products. "Hey Google, turn off the lights" is not a conversation. That is a command. This is the use case that has actually scaled, and it requires none of Sesame's innovations. Basic wake-word detection and intent parsing handle it fine.

Industry is treating these as points on a spectrum. They are not. They are different products with different users, different retention curves, and different economics. Sesame's breakthrough was in the companion category. Demand is primarily in the interface category. That gap between where the technology leaped forward and where the customers actually are is the core reason nobody has followed.

What Would Have to Be True

For Sesame's vision to succeed, several things need to happen simultaneously:

On-device inference must reach CSM quality in a glasses form factor. This requires either a dramatic reduction in model size without losing the prosody that makes CSM special, or a custom inference chip that can run a billion-parameter model within the 2-watt thermal envelope of eyewear. Neither exists today. Both are plausible within 2 to 3 years if the Qualcomm and MediaTek roadmaps for wearable NPUs deliver.

The economics must work at consumer scale. If the glasses cost $800 and require a $30/month subscription for cloud inference (the Humane model), adoption will be limited to early adopters. If Sesame can get inference costs low enough through on-device processing to sell the glasses at $400 to $600 with no subscription, they have a real consumer product.

Society must develop norms around emotional AI. This one has no engineering solution. Are you allowed to have a voice companion that sounds like your deceased parent? Is a company liable if a user develops emotional dependence on a voice AI that is then discontinued? Can a voice AI be used as evidence of a person's emotional state in court? These questions will be answered by legislators, courts, and cultural consensus, not by engineers. And they will take years.

Someone must solve persistent voice context without creating a privacy catastrophe. On-device memory with zero cloud exposure is the only architecture that survives public scrutiny. This means the device must have enough local storage and processing to maintain conversational history without phoning home. It also means the user must trust that the device is actually doing what it claims. After years of "your smart speaker is not always listening" revelations, that trust deficit is real.

What You Can Do Today

If you are building voice products: The stack has matured significantly since Sesame's demo. For speech-to-text, Deepgram Nova-3 (sub-300ms, $0.46/hour) or AssemblyAI Universal-2 (best accuracy at 14.5% WER) are production-ready. For text-to-speech, Deepgram Aura-2 achieves 90ms TTFB at scale, and Cartesia Sonic is even faster. For emotional voice, Hume AI's EVI 3 is the most accessible option with developer-friendly pricing. Sesame's open-source CSM-1B remains the best starting point for conversational speech generation with prosodic consistency, but it requires GPU infrastructure. ElevenLabs is the safe enterprise choice, now partnered with IBM for agentic AI deployments. For applications where latency and cost are secondary to voice quality (audiobooks, game characters, accessibility tools), CSM-1B is production-ready now.

If you are evaluating voice AI vendors: Ask for time-to-first-byte numbers under load, not in isolated benchmarks. Deepgram's Aura-2 achieves 90 milliseconds in steady state. That is the benchmark. Any vendor quoting numbers above 300 milliseconds is not competitive for conversational applications. Also ask about prosodic consistency across turns. Most systems reset emotional tone between responses. CSM does not. This is audible in extended conversations and it matters for user experience. And pay attention to the Gemini Live regression story: voice quality in production is fragile. A single codec change in a March 2026 update destroyed the prosody that users had grown to expect. Demand SLAs on audio quality, not just uptime.

If you are a consumer: Three things will change your voice AI experience in the next 12 months. Apple's LLM-powered Siri (iOS 26.4, spring 2026) will make Siri dramatically smarter at understanding context and completing multi-step tasks, though it will still sound like Siri. Google Gemini Live is already available and is the best free voice AI on Android, when it is not suffering from codec regressions. OpenAI's Jony Ive device has slipped to 2027 and may slip further. The boring prediction remains the right one: incremental improvement from incumbents matters more than revolutionary demos from startups, because the incumbents are already on your devices. But watch ByteDance's Doubao: if it ships a licensed voice assistant to Chinese smartphone OEMs at scale, it will be the first production deployment of something approaching voice presence, and it will put enormous pressure on Western companies to follow.

If you are thinking about the long term: Voice AI that is genuinely indistinguishable from a human conversation partner is coming. Not in the "AI is going to kill us all" sense. In the practical, engineering sense. The CSM architecture works. The latency problem is solved. The compute costs are falling on a predictable curve (roughly 2x cheaper per year). ElevenLabs hitting $300 million in revenue proves the market exists. Hume proving that emotional AI can work in therapy and coaching proves the use case exists. ByteDance proving 1.9 billion interactions in a single event proves the demand exists. The remaining barriers are economic (costs need to fall another 10x for consumer scale), regulatory (nobody has figured out emotional AI governance), and philosophical (the Her Problem has no engineering solution). Those barriers are real, but they are the kind that erode over time rather than the kind that require a breakthrough to overcome.

The uncanny valley of voice was crossed in February 2025. The question that remains is not whether we can build voice AI that feels human. It is whether we are ready for what happens when we do.
